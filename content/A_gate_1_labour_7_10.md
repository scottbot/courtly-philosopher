=== id: gate
title_en: The Entrance: the Swan above the Gate
title_es: la puerta de la opinion (with "vn Cisne" above it)
board_it: Guarda / l'fine (under the arch); Noscete ipsum (Latin, issuing from the trumpet)
kind: gate
rule_short: Every counter starts outside this gate. Your first throw counts squares from here into the track.
table: Every petitioner enters by the Gate of Opinion, and the swan on the skull above it warns each to know themselves and look to the end before beginning [@barrosFilosofiaCortesanaMoralizada1588, pdf 26–27].
see: |
  At the bottom left of the sheet, just before square 1, stands a round arch on two pillars, its vault drawn in perspective and ribbed in a grid; a small obelisk rises in the hilly landscape to its left. On the crown of the arch lies a human skull. A swan stands on the skull with one foot, its wings raised; its other foot is lifted and grips a long straight trumpet, held in its beak, whose bell points down to the left; from the bell runs the Latin motto "Noscete ipsum" (Nosce te ipsum, "Know thyself", engraved as one word). Between the two pillars, in Italian only, is engraved "Guarda / l'fine" ("Look to the end") [board]. Barros calls the skull a *muerte*, a death, and says the swan has one foot raised on it [@barrosFilosofiaCortesanaMoralizada1588, pdf 27].
why: |
  In Barros's allegory this is where a career at court begins. Whoever starts to seek office enters by the Gate of Opinion, misled by their own self-esteem and by the confidence they place in their patrons [@barrosFilosofiaCortesanaMoralizada1588, pdf 26]. Above the gate the swan stands on a death, the end of all things, and trumpets that everyone should know themselves and look to the end of what they seek, so that they will not complain of Fortune later if she goes against them, since perhaps she does them no wrong; the difficulties of the end, Barros says, are often born of the choice made at the beginning [@barrosFilosofiaCortesanaMoralizada1588, pdf 26–27]. The board splits that one sentence of Barros's into two mottoes, "Know thyself" in Latin and "Look to the end" in Italian (our observation).

  How a game begins. Each player puts one stake (a *tanto*, of whatever value the players agree) into the pot and takes a marker unlike anyone else's; in Barros's worked example three players use a ring, a silver two-real coin and a gold doubloon [@debarrosFilosofiaCortesana1587, pdf 11; @sanchezEdicionesAntiguasFilosofia2016oct16, 189]. Madrigal's 1587 rules say to play with two dice, or with six "arenillas", dice marked on one face only [@debarrosFilosofiaCortesana1587, pdf 11; @web:https://dle.rae.es/arenilla]. Players throw in turn; each counter comes in through this gate and is set on the square whose number equals the points thrown [@barrosFilosofiaCortesanaMoralizada1588, pdf 66]. If a counter lands where another already stands, the newcomer stays and the other must take the square the newcomer came from, which Barros calls the custom of rivalry [@barrosFilosofiaCortesanaMoralizada1588, pdf 27–28, pdf 66]. Madrigal's text adds that at the very start of the game the displaced player is left with no square at all, that is, back outside the gate [@debarrosFilosofiaCortesana1587, pdf 12]. Only Madrigal's edition says in so many words that the first to reach 63 with the exact count takes the pot [@sanchezEdicionesAntiguasFilosofia2016oct16, 189–90]; the Naples book gives the exact count but never says how the game is won [@barrosFilosofiaCortesanaMoralizada1588, pdf 68].
context: |
  Early Goose boards also open with a herald-animal that calls the players to the game (the goose, or the monkey of Altiero Gatti's board of 1588), and Collar de Cáceres treats Barros's swan as entirely comparable to the goose of the original game [@decaceresTableroItalianoFilosofia2009, 88]. Lucero makes the same point: {q|luceroFilosofiaCortesanaJuego2021juin20|pdf9|140|La sonora trompeta del cisne sobre la calavera que timbra el arco de entrada, trasunto peculiar de la oca primitiva} ("the ringing trumpet of the swan on the skull that crowns the entrance arch, a peculiar transformation of the primitive goose") [@luceroFilosofiaCortesanaJuego2021juin20, 140]. Wood reads Gatti's *Game of the Monkey*, printed the same year, as a parody that replaces this memento mori with a trumpeting monkey [@woodChancingItPrint2019, 475].

  The swan was believed to sing before it died; Collar traces the idea to Horapollo's *Hieroglyphica* and to Plato's *Phaedo* 85c [@decaceresTableroItalianoFilosofia2009, 93, 103]. Madrigal's 1587 text gives the twist: this swan announces not its own end, as swans do, but someone else's [@debarrosFilosofiaCortesana1587, pdf 11; @decaceresTableroItalianoFilosofia2009, 103]. Alciato used the bird otherwise: Alciato's *Insignia poetarum* makes the swan, sacred to Apollo, the badge of poets [@web:https://www.emblems.arts.gla.ac.uk/alciato/emblem.php?id=A21a184].

  "Nosce te ipsum" is the maxim of Apollo's temple at Delphi, which Collar calls Socratic [@decaceresTableroItalianoFilosofia2009, 93]. Martínez Millán makes self-knowledge the first of the petitioner's two tools; the second is the four means Barros names, liberality, flattery, diligence and labor [@millanFilosofiaCortesanaAlonso1996, 468]. Lucero notes that court writers had adopted the Delphic maxim, ties it to humility, and cites Guevara's warning that not everyone is made for the court [@luceroFilosofiaCortesanaJuego2021juin20, 140]. The book opens on the same note: its first sentence says that the end of the work is its beginning, since a house is planned for living in before its first stone is laid [@barrosFilosofiaCortesanaMoralizada1588, pdf 22–23]; Bidwell-Steiner points out the double sense of *fin*, end and purpose [@bidwell-steinerFilosofiaCortesanaAlfonso2024, 197]. Barros knew the gate from inside: he had held the modest post of royal *aposentador* (quartermaster of the court) since 1563 and spent years seeking more [@luceroFilosofiaCortesanaJuego2021juin20, 137].
variants: |
  - The swan's foot on the death. The Madrid princeps of the widow of Alonso Gómez (G) describes only a swan with a trumpet; Madrigal's edition (M) adds the foot raised on a death as the end of things, and the Naples book (C) keeps it [@sanchezEdicionesAntiguasFilosofia2016oct16, 185; @barrosFilosofiaCortesanaMoralizada1588, pdf 27].
  - The maxim on beginnings. G says the choice of the beginning is the cause (*es causa*) of the difficulties of the end; M and C say those difficulties are born (*nacen*) of it [@sanchezEdicionesAntiguasFilosofia2016oct16, 184; @barrosFilosofiaCortesanaMoralizada1588, pdf 26].
  - Board and book. Neither "Noscete ipsum" nor "Guarda l'fine" is printed in the book; they exist only on the board [board]. Lucero notes that the only mottoes not given in both languages lie outside the track: the corner mottoes, which have no Italian, and the admonition under the portico, which is Italian only [@luceroTableroJuego15882019, 203].
  - Engraved forms. The sheet reads "Noscete ipsum" as one word and "Guarda / l'fine"; "Guarda il fine" is a normalization [board]. Collar and Lucero read "Guarda l'fine" [@decaceresTableroItalianoFilosofia2009, 93; @luceroTableroJuego15882019, 203].
  - Rules. C prints only a short section of rules; M alone has the long "Declaración del juego" with the stake, the markers, the two dice and the worked example [@barrosFilosofiaCortesanaMoralizada1588, pdf 66; @sanchezEdicionesAntiguasFilosofia2016oct16, 189–90]. G and C speak of throwing the die (*el dado*) in the singular [@sanchezEdicionesAntiguasFilosofia2016oct16, 189; @barrosFilosofiaCortesanaMoralizada1588, pdf 66]; M, after prescribing two dice, also says *el dado* in its rules [@debarrosFilosofiaCortesana1587, pdf 11–12]. In M's example the displaced player also pays one stake for going back; C's only such clause follows its overshoot rule [@debarrosFilosofiaCortesana1587, pdf 12; @barrosFilosofiaCortesanaMoralizada1588, pdf 66–69].
readings: |
  Collar de Cáceres reads the swan as joining three ideas: the swan-song and death, the summons to the game of court ambition, and the call to weigh one's claims against one's merits [@decaceresTableroItalianoFilosofia2009, 93]. Wood reads the portal as a memento mori that opens a "virtual pilgrimage" and asks the player to look to the end, which for her is either the sea of suffering or the palm of victory [@woodChancingItPrint2019, 463, 466]. Wilson, working from the Madrid book and without the board, took the trumpet for fame: {q|wilsonCervantesItemEmmanuel1968|pdf6|367|A swan blows the trumpet of fame and rests his foot on a death's head.} [@wilsonCervantesItemEmmanuel1968, 367]. Martínez Millán, also writing before the board was found, supposed that a fourth corner held a Fortune [@millanFilosofiaCortesanaAlonso1996, 475]; on the 1588 sheet that corner is this gate [board]. Zollinger describes the entrance as an unnumbered square formed by an arch on two columns, in the Italian manner [@GameGooseLargest]. Bidwell-Steiner reads the invitation to return to the start, where the swan proclaims "Nosce te ipsum", as the last step of an active life [@bidwell-steinerFilosofiaCortesanaAlfonso2024, 212]. The La Bella Donna blog renders the gate motto "Watch for the end" [@TranslationFilosofiaCortesana].

=== id: sq1
title_en: The Gate of Opinion: the Peacock
title_es: el Pauon
board_it: (none engraved)
kind: marked
rule_short: No penalty or bonus. The first square of the track, which every petitioner's career passes.
table: Fresh through the gate, the petitioner shows off their merits like a peacock spreading its tail, until time tells them to look down at the feet of their failings [@barrosFilosofiaCortesanaMoralizada1588, pdf 26].
see: |
  A peacock stands frontally on a strip of ground, its head turned to the right, its tail raised and fanned into a full wheel of eyed feathers, a small crest on its head. The tail fills the top of the bay, and no numeral 1 can be seen. Below it, between the pedestals, the couplet is engraved in Italian and then in Spanish: "Reason looks down to the feet; / Opinion, to the wheel's conceit." The Italian says the same [board]. No heading is engraved on the square, and the book does not name it separately: the petitioner enters by the Gate of Opinion and spreads their tail like the peacock (*el Pauon*) [board; @barrosFilosofiaCortesanaMoralizada1588, pdf 26].
why: |
  Barros explains the peacock with the petitioner who has just come through the gate: they go about so pleased with themselves, parading their merits, that they spread their tail like the peacock, until time warns them to look down at the feet of their demerits and fold it [@barrosFilosofiaCortesanaMoralizada1588, pdf 26]. The wheel of feathers is their opinion of themselves; the feet are what the petitioner is. "Opinion" here is both self-regard and reputation, what a person thinks of themselves and what others say of them.

  The square carries no rule. When two dice are used no throw is smaller than two, so a counter comes to rest on this square only in the one-die variant or with the six *arenillas*, dice marked on one face only (our observation); the peacock works as a picture every petitioner walks past on the way in.
context: |
  Madrigal's 1587 text glosses the bird further: as the peacock that draws Juno's chariot it signifies greatness, and when it proudly makes its wheel it signifies opinion [@debarrosFilosofiaCortesana1587, pdf 11; @decaceresTableroItalianoFilosofia2009, 93]. Collar de Cáceres adds Pierio Valeriano's *Hieroglyphica*, where the peacock stands for ostentation and vainglory [@decaceresTableroItalianoFilosofia2009, 93, 103], and Martínez Millán cites Ripa on arrogance and the peacock [@millanFilosofiaCortesanaAlonso1996, 468]. Ripa's Opinion, as Collar reports, is a richly dressed woman with wings on her shoulders and hands, because opinions change so fast [@decaceresTableroItalianoFilosofia2009, 103].

  Lucero reads the peacock as a cure for pride, a *desengaño de soberbia* [@luceroIdeaTrabajoFilosofia2019, 830], and links it to the "Know thyself" of the gate [@luceroFilosofiaCortesanaJuego2021juin20, 140]. For him opinion, one's own and other people's, is what drives the whole game and also sets its limits [@luceroFilosofiaCortesanaJuego2021juin20, 142]. Court culture could say the opposite: Galileo, drafting a letter to the Medici secretary Vinta on how the grand duke should receive his discovery of the Medicean Stars, wrote that {q|biagioliGalileoCourtierPractice1993|pdf150|135|whoever wants to be esteemed ought to have self-esteem first} [@biagioliGalileoCourtierPractice1993, 135]. Barros's couplet sets itself against that kind of self-promotion (our observation). The peacock's "wheel" also prepares the player for Fortune's wheel at square 51 (our observation).
variants: |
  - Couplet. The board's Spanish reads "y a la rueda la opinion"; the La Bella Donna blog drops the "a" [board; @TranslationFilosofiaCortesana]. The Naples book prints the couplet in the same words [@barrosFilosofiaCortesanaMoralizada1588, pdf 27].
  - Prose. C reads *engañado de pensamiento* and misprints *dsehaga* for *deshaga* [@barrosFilosofiaCortesanaMoralizada1588, pdf 26]. The modernized transcription of the Madrid text has *engañado de su pensamiento* [@debarrosFilosofiaCortesana1587, pdf 4]. G spells *propria* where M has *propia* [@sanchezEdicionesAntiguasFilosofia2016oct16, 186].
  - Placement. Only M's "Declaración" says where the bird is: at the door of the first square, with the swan above it [@debarrosFilosofiaCortesana1587, pdf 11]. On the 1588 board it fills square 1, just past the gate [board].
readings: |
  Bidwell-Steiner reads the peacock as the petitioner's infatuation with themselves and calls it an innovation: the first square to illustrate the game's theme [@bidwell-steinerFilosofiaCortesanaAlfonso2024, 198]. Collar ties this first square closely to the gate, as its continuation [@decaceresTableroItalianoFilosofia2009, 93]. Wilson describes the petitioner's idea of themselves as a peacock "with fanned-out tail and ugly feet" [@wilsonCervantesItemEmmanuel1968, 367]. The La Bella Donna blog renders the couplet "Watch the feet for reason and the wheel for the view" [@TranslationFilosofiaCortesana].

=== id: labour
title_en: Labor (the nine squares of the Oxen)
title_es: las casas del Trabajo (los bueyes)
board_it: trauaglio (on 4, 12, 17, 30, 34, 41, 48, 57); tauaglio [sic] on 23
kind: labour
rule_short: Don't stop: move on again by the number you just threw (and again, if you land on oxen once more).
table: There is no resting in a petition: like the oxen, who work hardest and feel it least, you go cheerfully on by as many squares again [@barrosFilosofiaCortesanaMoralizada1588, pdf 28–31].
see: |
  Nine squares, 4, 12, 17, 23, 30, 34, 41, 48 and 57, carry the same device: a yoke of two oxen on the strip of ground that runs through every bay [board]. On every Labor square a cluster of fruit and leaves hangs at the middle of a cord slung between the two balusters, above the oxen [board]. Each Labor square has a small cartouche headed *trauaglio*, Italian for labor, and its own couplet in Italian and in Spanish. The oxen are posed differently each time, walking, standing or turning their heads; only on 30 do they draw a plow [board]. The eight couplets of squares 12 to 57 stand along the track in the order in which the 1588 book prints them (our observation) [board; @barrosFilosofiaCortesanaMoralizada1588, pdf 32].
why: |
  Barros names four means by which a petitioner advances: liberality, flattery, diligence and labor [@barrosFilosofiaCortesanaMoralizada1588, pdf 24]. Labor gets the most squares, and you may not rest on one. There must be no moment of rest in petitioning, he says, or the thread of the fruits that labor yields will break. Hence the picture: two oxen plowing under fruit strung on a thread, the fruits of just labor, each hanging from the next [@barrosFilosofiaCortesanaMoralizada1588, pdf 28–29]. Oxen were chosen because they work hardest and feel it least if they are fed and rewarded; so the petitioner feels their fatigue only when the pay their hope promised them is refused [@barrosFilosofiaCortesanaMoralizada1588, pdf 29]. Everything a person seeks must come through labor, and labor is a duty inherited from our first parents; so the player who lands on a Labor square must not balk but go cheerfully on as many squares again as they threw [@barrosFilosofiaCortesanaMoralizada1588, pdf 29–31]. Barros's word *trabajo* means both work and hardship, and several couplets play on both senses.

  Why the oxen are scattered. Barros says the Labor squares keep no order because the subject, a petition at court, has none; besides the first there are eight more, and their verses teach the petitioner not to fear labor but to seek it [@barrosFilosofiaCortesanaMoralizada1588, pdf 31]. In the Goose game the geese come at regular steps, so a lucky first throw could run from goose to goose to the end, and the Goose game needed a special first-throw rule to prevent it [@luceroFilosofiaCortesanaJuego2020, 122–23]. With Barros's irregular oxen no first throw can win the game at once [@luceroFilosofiaCortesanaJuego2020, 126]. Madrigal's text says the rule applies every time one lands on oxen [@debarrosFilosofiaCortesana1587, pdf 12]; read that way, a throw of seven can carry a counter from 34 to 41 to 48 and on to 55, "I Thought…", which sends it back to Diligence at 20 (our observation).

  The book also gives the whole picture a motto about labor: "No son of Adam can be fed / unless by labor for his bread." It closes Barros's introduction: no lot in life lacks its *trabajos*, its hardships [@barrosFilosofiaCortesanaMoralizada1588, pdf 25]. That motto is not engraved on the board [board].
context: |
  The ox takes the goose's place, and the scholars we cite treat that as the key choice of the design. Lucero: {q|luceroIdeaTrabajoFilosofia2019|pdf5|825|No puede considerarse una cuestión menor o accidental que el buey sustituya a la oca en el tablero.} ("It cannot be thought a minor or accidental matter that the ox replaces the goose on the board.") [@luceroIdeaTrabajoFilosofia2019, 825]. Collar de Cáceres calls labor {q|decaceresTableroItalianoFilosofia2009|pdf11|91|el verdadero motor del juego} ("the real motor of the game") [@decaceresTableroItalianoFilosofia2009, 91]. Bidwell-Steiner sets the two animals against each other: {q|bidwell-steinerFilosofiaCortesanaAlfonso2024|pdf5|199|La oca del juego original representa suerte, abundancia y diversión.} ("The goose of the original game stands for luck, abundance and fun."), where the yoked oxen stand for ceaseless toil, submission and seriousness [@bidwell-steinerFilosofiaCortesanaAlfonso2024, 199]. González Ramírez, reviewing Lucero's edition, reports his equation of the goose with idleness (*oca*, *ocio*) and of the ox with labor [@ramirezAlonsoBarrosFilosofia2020].

  At court the choice had a political edge. Lucero ties the praise of labor to the "Castilian" faction of Barros's dedicatee, the royal secretary Mateo Vázquez de Leca, a man of doubtful lineage who had risen by tireless personal effort [@luceroIdeaTrabajoFilosofia2019, 822], and reads it as a criticism of an idle nobility [@luceroIdeaTrabajoFilosofia2019, 825]. He also notes the paradox that the Madrigal title page carries Vázquez's coat of arms, which asserts noble blood against the board's praise of work [@luceroDEDICATORIAFILOSOFIACORTESANA2019, 49]. Earlier writers had made the ox an image of servitude at court: Castillejo's courtier bows their head to the patron "like the ox", and Barros himself later wrote that no submission is more servile than petitioning for favor [@luceroIdeaTrabajoFilosofia2019, 825–26]. Lucero's sharpest objection is that the game runs on dice: favors at court were graces, not wages, and {q|luceroIdeaTrabajoFilosofia2019|pdf9|829|Entonces, Fortuna siempre vence.} ("Then Fortune always wins.") [@luceroIdeaTrabajoFilosofia2019, 829]. Biagioli describes the same structure in Italian courts, where a courtier's reward was like a Christian's grace: no action could guarantee it [@biagioliGalileoCourtierPractice1993, 51].

  Emblem sources. For Pierio Valeriano plowing oxen foretell abundance of fruits; Collar also cites the device "Passo a passo" of René of Anjou (in Giovio) and one of Gabriel Simeoni's devices [@decaceresTableroItalianoFilosofia2009, 91, 103]. Bidwell-Steiner adds Simeoni's two oxen at the plow as true companions [@bidwell-steinerFilosofiaCortesanaAlfonso2024, 200]. Lucero's closest parallel is Juan de Horozco's *Emblemas morales* (Segovia, 1589), book II, emblem 3: an ox skull under Fortune's winged wheel, motto *Par est fortuna labori* ("Fortune matches labor") [@luceroIdeaTrabajoFilosofia2019, 827; @luceroFilosofiaCortesanaJuego2021juin20, 137]. He also cites Juan de Borja's *Ex labore fructus* and the *Apólogo de la Ociosidad y el Trabajo*, a book Barros owned, where the ox is Labor's most precious gift [@luceroIdeaTrabajoFilosofia2019, 822, 824, 827]. Lucero conjectures that Barros and Horozco, both tied to Segovia, knew each other [@luceroIdeaTrabajoFilosofia2019, 828].

  The Adam motto. Lucero calls it {q|sanchezEdicionesAntiguasFilosofia2016oct16|pdf17|185|una letra nueva con raíces bíblicas que rubrica la fuerza de la Fortuna-Providencia} ("a new motto with biblical roots that confirms the power of Fortune-as-Providence") [@sanchezEdicionesAntiguasFilosofia2016oct16, 185]. The root is God's sentence on Adam, "in the sweat of thy face shalt thou eat bread" (Genesis 3:19; our identification). Collar heads his section on the oxen with it [@decaceresTableroItalianoFilosofia2009, 91]. The Labor couplets had readers of their own: Gutierre Marqués de Careaga quoted them with approval in his *Desengaño de Fortuna* (Madrid, 1612) [@wilsonCervantesItemEmmanuel1968, 370; @sanchezEdicionesAntiguasFilosofia2016oct16, 174].
variants: |
  - Number of squares. M and C speak of the first Labor square and eight others, nine in all, as on the board [@barrosFilosofiaCortesanaMoralizada1588, pdf 31; @luceroTableroJuego15882019, 203]; G too prints eight couplets for the squares after the first [@sanchezEdicionesAntiguasFilosofia2016oct16, 187–88]. Wilson, before the board was found, wrote of eight [@wilsonCervantesItemEmmanuel1968, 367].
  - The couplets of G. The princeps prints a different set of eight couplets for the squares after the first. Only four of them survive in M and C, and those four are reordered [@sanchezEdicionesAntiguasFilosofia2016oct16, 187–88]. G's other four are: "However great the yoke may prove, / it is not felt when laid by love"; "Yoke and plowshare, plow and all, / for love's sake never tire at all"; "There is no toil but it will break / the one who deems it hard to take"; and "Who makes a virtue of his need / by working finds his rest indeed" [@sanchezEdicionesAntiguasFilosofia2016oct16, 187–88]. G's order is: yoke of love; yoke and plow; the toil that breaks; the fruit of hope (34 on the board); Fortune kind to labor (48); virtue of need; Fortune yields (30); no great trouble (23). M drops the two love-and-yoke couplets and the other two, and adds those of squares 12, 17, 41 and 57 [@sanchezEdicionesAntiguasFilosofia2016oct16, 187–88]. Lucero thinks the dropped pair suited love more than labor. In his view the new ones stress the fruits of work, the reordering is unexplained, and the changes are Barros's own [@sanchezEdicionesAntiguasFilosofia2016oct16, 187–88]. The collation we cite does not include the first couplet, "Toil is never felt…", so G's wording of it is not known to us [@sanchezEdicionesAntiguasFilosofia2016oct16, 187–88].
  - Lost boards. Lucero, who takes these to be the only mottoes that change between editions, argues that the lost board issued with G must have differed from the Naples one [@luceroTableroJuego15882019, 203 n. 15]. The order of the lines at 53 also changes, and the Dice verse at 28 may differ as well (see those squares). He adds that Barros cared little where the oxen stood, so they may have sat on different nearby squares on different boards [@luceroTableroJuego15882019, 203–4]. In his thesis he suggests that the princeps board spread them more evenly [@sanchezFILOSOFIACORTESANAALONSO, 20].
  - Wording of the prose. G misprints *buyes … fructas*, which M corrects to *bueyes … frutas* [@sanchezEdicionesAntiguasFilosofia2016oct16, 183]. G says only that the squares keep no order; M and C name them as the squares of Labor [@sanchezEdicionesAntiguasFilosofia2016oct16, 184; @barrosFilosofiaCortesanaMoralizada1588, pdf 31]. G speaks of the fatigue *de lo que trabaja*, M of that *de sus trabajos* [@sanchezEdicionesAntiguasFilosofia2016oct16, 186]; C misprints *cansacio* [@barrosFilosofiaCortesanaMoralizada1588, pdf 29].
  - The Adam motto is absent from G, added in M and kept in C [@sanchezEdicionesAntiguasFilosofia2016oct16, 185; @barrosFilosofiaCortesanaMoralizada1588, pdf 25].
  - Rules. C's short rules say only that a player who lands on the oxen goes on as many squares again [@barrosFilosofiaCortesanaMoralizada1588, pdf 66]. M's worked example has a player throw 4, reach the square of Labor and go on to 8 [@sanchezEdicionesAntiguasFilosofia2016oct16, 190].
  - Board and book. The book prints the couplets in Spanish only; the board adds Italian versions and differs in small spellings, noted square by square [board; @barrosFilosofiaCortesanaMoralizada1588, pdf 32]. Every cartouche reads *trauaglio* except square 23's *tauaglio* [board].
readings: |
  Lucero calls the oxen the most important, most frequent and most symbolically loaded square, the one that gives the parent game its name [@luceroIdeaTrabajoFilosofia2019, 825]; he also classes them, with the Dice, as "mechanical" squares that move the player, unlike the marked squares of court life [@luceroFilosofiaCortesanaJuego2021juin20, 135]. see: |
  A pelican with its wings raised bends its neck to pierce its own breast, and drops of blood fall. why: |
  The pelican was the classic image of self-sacrifice: it fed its young with its own blood. Here it pours out its entrails, which Barros glosses as its estate, not to its chicks but to cats [@barrosFilosofiaCortesanaMoralizada1588, pdf 40]. That is the petitioner who has been betrayed by false friends. Barros's remedy is for the petitioner to go back to being prodigal, even with those they most suspected, since at first everyone seems suspect until worse turn up. The picture shows the violence petitioning does to reason: a petitioner must be not only liberal to friends but prodigal to enemies, knowing how little they gain by it [@barrosFilosofiaCortesanaMoralizada1588, pdf 39–40]. The verse says how long such friendship lasts: as long as the prodigal keeps a table.

context: |
  Barros is inverting a sacred image. In the medieval bestiary the pelican kills its young and raises them with its blood after three days, a figure of Christ [@web:https://www.abdn.ac.uk/bestiary/ms24/f35r]. Collar de Cáceres adds that the pelican stood for friendship and piety in Ripa and in Hadrianus Junius's *Aenigmata* (1565), and for folly in Valeriano [@decaceresTableroItalianoFilosofia2009, 93, 103–4]. Bidwell-Steiner cites the *Physiologus*, Isidore and the exegesis of Psalm 101, notes that the pelican was also an emblem of King Alfonso II of Naples, and calls Barros's version, in the context of Christian emblematics, a blasphemy: the pelican embodies Christ, and cats belonged to black magic [@bidwell-steinerFilosofiaCortesanaAlfonso2024, 202]. For Martínez Millán the pelican meant Christ's sacrifice from the late Middle Ages [@millanFilosofiaCortesanaAlonso1996, 479].

  The cats may also carry a pun. Lucero notes that *gato* was the word for a purse, perhaps once made of cat's skin, and cites examples in *Guzmán de Alfarache* and *Don Quijote* [@luceroFilosofiaCortesanaJuego2021juin20, 144–45]. He sets the square against Hernando de Soto's emblem *Liberalis, et non prodigus* ("liberal, not prodigal"), by a friend of Barros [@luceroFilosofiaCortesanaJuego2021juin20, 144]. For Lucero liberality has no square of its own but works along the whole route; the Prodigal is its excess [@luceroFilosofiaCortesanaJuego2021juin20, 144].

  Court writers described the same economy. Biagioli quotes Agostino Mascardi: {q|biagioliGalileoCourtierPractice1993|pdf54|39|The courtier was bound to "impoverish himself in order to become rich; to give in order to receive."} [@biagioliGalileoCourtierPractice1993, 39]. Della Casa's *Galateo* advised clients to seem liberal beyond their duty [@biagioliGalileoCourtierPractice1993, 85].
variants: |
  - Couplet. The board's Spanish reads "qua[n]do come co[n] testigos" (when he eats with witnesses); the book, in Madrigal's edition and in Naples, reads "Quanto come con testigos" (as long as he eats with witnesses) [board; @decaceresTableroItalianoFilosofia2009, 103; @barrosFilosofiaCortesanaMoralizada1588, pdf 41]. The board's Italian *accompãgato* abbreviates *accompagnato*; Collar prints *accompag(n)to*, dropping a letter [board].
  - Prose. The clause saying that at first everyone is suspect until worse turn up is not in G; M adds it and C keeps it [@sanchezEdicionesAntiguasFilosofia2016oct16, 185; @barrosFilosofiaCortesanaMoralizada1588, pdf 39–40]. C and M read *se topan otros peores* [@barrosFilosofiaCortesanaMoralizada1588, pdf 40; @sanchezEdicionesAntiguasFilosofia2016oct16, 185]; the *con* of the modernized transcription (*se topan con otros peores*) is its own [@debarrosFilosofiaCortesana1587, pdf 7].
  - Number. The prose does not number the square; the rules put the Prodigal at 7 [@barrosFilosofiaCortesanaMoralizada1588, pdf 67].
  - Image. Wilson, working from the Madrid text without the board, wrote of a pelican pecking its breast and a single cat licking up the blood [@wilsonCervantesItemEmmanuel1968, 368]; the board shows three [board].
readings: |
  Collar de Cáceres reads the image as the pelican of friendship and of Christ turned into the prodigal and false friendship, and sums up that money buys everything [@decaceresTableroItalianoFilosofia2009, 93]; he calls Poverty, square 60, its reverse [@decaceresTableroItalianoFilosofia2009, 96]. Lucero finds the image enigmatic and perhaps meant only to show {q|luceroFilosofiaCortesanaJuego2021juin20|pdf13|144|la fuerza que se hace a la razón con la pretensión} ("the force done to reason by petitioning"). He offers the purse pun only as a possible refinement [@luceroFilosofiaCortesanaJuego2021juin20, 144–45]. Bidwell-Steiner calls the lesson a rather worldly cross [@bidwell-steinerFilosofiaCortesanaAlfonso2024, 202]. The La Bella Donna blog describes "a swan and wolves"; the board shows a pelican and cats [@TranslationFilosofiaCortesana; board].

=== id: sq10
title_en: Flattery
title_es: la Adulacion
board_it: Adulatione
kind: marked
rule_short: No penalty here. Players sent back from the Change of Ministers (43) arrive here after paying one stake.
table: After a change of ministers the petitioner must flatter again and bow to the successor; the flatterer changes color like the chameleon and sings like the siren [@barrosFilosofiaCortesanaMoralizada1588, pdf 42–43].
see: |
  A siren rises out of the waves: a woman to the waist, with a scaly fish's tail curling up behind her. In her right hand she holds up a hand-mirror on a handle; on her left hand sits a chameleon, a small crested lizard with a curled tail. The numeral 10 is above her head. Under the heading *Adulatione*, the couplet in Italian and Spanish: "Flattery and Fraud will sell / fine samples, and false cloth as well." The Italian says the same: flattery and deceit sell a good sample and poor cloth (our gloss) [board].
why: |
  The verse describes a draper's trick: show a fine swatch, deliver shoddy cloth (our gloss). The petitioner comes here after a change of ministers. The minister the petitioner had won over has been replaced, or has changed (the Spanish may also mean that the petitioner has moved on), so they must go back to flattery and bow to whoever has taken the patron's place [@barrosFilosofiaCortesanaMoralizada1588, pdf 41–42]. Barros explains each figure. The flatterer persuades the person they mean to deceive with examples of vice among great persons, shown to the victim as in a mirror and cloaked in virtue, so that the victim copies them without recognizing them. The flatterer changes shape to suit the other's taste, as the chameleon takes the color of whatever is nearest. The flatterer's talk is the siren's song, which kills whoever, lost in their own vanity, enjoys the music and does not stop their ears with cotton [@barrosFilosofiaCortesanaMoralizada1588, pdf 42–43].

  The square itself has no rule. Square 43, the Change of Ministers, sends the player back here, and its label adds "Paga" (pay one stake) [@barrosFilosofiaCortesanaMoralizada1588, pdf 67; board].
context: |
  Both animals come from the emblem tradition. In Alciato's *In adulatores* the chameleon lives on air and takes every color but red and white, as the flatterer feeds on popular breath and copies only the prince's dark features [@web:https://www.emblems.arts.gla.ac.uk/alciato/emblem.php?id=A21a053]. Alciato's *Sirenes* shows fish-tailed sirens singing to Ulysses, bound to his mast; they carry no mirror [@web:https://www.emblems.arts.gla.ac.uk/alciato/emblem.php?id=A21a116]. Collar de Cáceres takes Barros's stopped ears as an allusion to Ulysses and cites Alciato and Ripa for the chameleon as the flatterer [@decaceresTableroItalianoFilosofia2009, 94, 104]. The sources we cite do not trace the siren's mirror to an emblem model. Juan de Horozco treats the sirens in his *Emblemas morales* (1589), book II, emblem 30 [@decaceresTableroItalianoFilosofia2009, 104]. Sebastián de Covarrubias has sirens and a "color-changing, flattering" chameleon in his *Emblemas morales* of 1610 [@luceroFilosofiaCortesanaJuego2021juin20, 145; @millanFilosofiaCortesanaAlonso1996, 476]. Both books appeared after Barros's; Martínez Millán's parallel in Ripa is also later, since the *Iconologia* first appeared after the *Filosofía cortesana* [@millanFilosofiaCortesanaAlonso1996, 479; @lucerosanchezAsnoPenseQue2019, 166].

  At court the chameleon was the courtier's animal. Damasio de Frías advised the discreet courtier to be a chameleon with the great in superficial things [@luceroFilosofiaCortesanaJuego2021juin20, 145]. Martínez Millán cites Lope, whose *Los Guzmanes del Toral* calls courtiers chameleons [@millanFilosofiaCortesanaAlonso1996, 479–80]. Díaz Gito finds it telling that {q|gitoTodaVidaEs2020|pdf3|185|fuese el camaleón el animal simbólico del cortesano} ("the chameleon should be the courtier's symbolic animal") [@gitoTodaVidaEs2020, 185]. Hernando de Soto, Barros's friend, warned, as Cicero had, that flattery should not be used on one's own people because it can destroy them [@decaceresTableroItalianoFilosofia2009, 94, 104]. In Galileo's Italy a client tested a patron with extravagantly flattering letters and watched the reply [@biagioliGalileoCourtierPractice1993, 27].
variants: |
  - Couplet. On the board the Spanish lines run "Vende adulacion y e[n]ganno / muestra fina y falso paño"; the book reverses them, "Muestra fina, y falso panno; / Vende adulacion, y enganno" [board; @barrosFilosofiaCortesanaMoralizada1588, pdf 43; @decaceresTableroItalianoFilosofia2009, 104]. The Naples book spells *panno* and *enganno* with the Italian double n for ñ; so does the board's *e[n]ganno* [@barrosFilosofiaCortesanaMoralizada1588, pdf 43; board].
  - Prose. In G the siren kills the person in suspense who enjoys her music (*gustando de su música*); M and C make the victim someone who, raised up in their vanity, enjoys it (*elevado en su vanidad*) [@sanchezEdicionesAntiguasFilosofia2016oct16, 185; @barrosFilosofiaCortesanaMoralizada1588, pdf 43]. G has the petitioner make their reverence anew (*de nuevo*) to the successor; M adds *en aquel lugar*, in that place [@sanchezEdicionesAntiguasFilosofia2016oct16, 186]. C reads *como en espejo*, the modernized transcription of M *como en un espejo* [@barrosFilosofiaCortesanaMoralizada1588, pdf 42; @debarrosFilosofiaCortesana1587, pdf 7].
  - Number. The prose does not number the square; the rules put Flattery at 10 [@barrosFilosofiaCortesanaMoralizada1588, pdf 67].
  - Martínez Millán's text prints "Maestra fina" for "Muestra fina", a slip [@millanFilosofiaCortesanaAlonso1996, 479].
readings: |
  Collar de Cáceres takes the siren as the common symbol of seduction and deceit, and the chameleon as the proper emblem of flattery [@decaceresTableroItalianoFilosofia2009, 94]. Seville puts the pieces together differently from Barros: {q|sevilleImportanceTextPrinted2026|pdf12|73|Flattery is represented as a Siren, whose music will kill if the ears are not stopped.} In his account the mirror shows bad conduct and the chameleon makes it seem virtuous [@sevilleImportanceTextPrinted2026, 73]. Barros's text instead puts the cloak of virtue in the mirror clause and gives the chameleon the changing of shape [@barrosFilosofiaCortesanaMoralizada1588, pdf 42–43]. Lucero writes {q|luceroFilosofiaCortesanaJuego2021juin20|pdf14|145|Propone Barros definir esta casa con la sirena o el camaleón.} ("Barros proposes to define this square with the siren or the chameleon"); the board shows both [@luceroFilosofiaCortesanaJuego2021juin20, 145; board]. Bidwell-Steiner reads the siren as speech, the chameleon as transformation and the mirror as false reflection. She counts the siren among the board's three female figures, all negative or ambiguous [@bidwell-steinerFilosofiaCortesanaAlfonso2024, 202–3] (the board also shows a woman in the Occasion of the top right corner [board]). The La Bella Donna blog describes "a mermaid looking into a mirror" and does not mention the chameleon [@TranslationFilosofiaCortesana].
