=== id: sq15
title_en: The Pass of Hope
title_es: Passo de la esperança
board_it: Passo della speranza
kind: marked
rule_short: Don't stop here: pay one stake, move on to 26, the Favorite, and pay one more there.
table: Friends praise the newcomer's merits, hope swells, the toll is paid, and the petitioner goes to beg at the Favorite's door [@barrosFilosofiaCortesanaMoralizada1588, pdf 32–34].
see: |
  A bearded man in a short tunic stands on a brick bridge of two round arches, with water
  pouring out through the arches beneath his feet. He holds up one hand, and a string of
  coins falls from it; in the other he keeps a purse or bag [board]. The numeral 15 is cut
  on either side of his head [board]. Under the picture are the heading *Passo della
  speranza*, an Italian couplet and a Spanish couplet: "There is no hope worth having still / that rests upon another's will" [board]. A separate box in the white gutter inside the ring, facing
  the square, carries the rule: *Al priuato ·nº· 26. Paga*, "To the Favorite, no. 26. Pay"
  [board].
why: |
  This is the first thing that happens to the new petitioner on starting out. They tell
  their friends about their petition, and the friends praise the petitioner's merits so lavishly
  that the newcomer comes to rate them above what they had even hoped for. The petitioner "pays
  the toll", asks who has influence with the person they need, learns that it is the *privado*
  (the Favorite), and goes to the Favorite's house to ask for help. Because they are new to
  petitioning, having to beg hurts them, and they say the verse to themselves
  [@barrosFilosofiaCortesanaMoralizada1588, pdf 32–34]. Hope, in other words, costs
  money, and it carries you straight to the Favorite's door. The verse gives the lesson: hope
  that hangs on someone else's will is worth nothing.

  The rule follows the story. According to the 1588 rules, a player who reaches the Pass of Hope
  goes on to the Favorite at 26, paying one stake "for each"
  [@barrosFilosofiaCortesanaMoralizada1588, pdf 66–67]. The Madrid rules of Pedro Madrigal
  (1587) spell this out as one stake for each of the two squares [@debarrosFilosofiaCortesana1587, pdf 12; @millanFilosofiaCortesanaAlonso1996, 470–71]. Madrigal's worked example shows the rule in use. Pedro, standing
  on 6, throws 9 and lands on 15. He reads the label above the square and pays one stake to the
  pot "for the good hope that offers itself". When he puts his marker on 26 he pays another
  "for the favor he hopes for" [@debarrosFilosofiaCortesana1587, pdf 12].
context: |
  The square adapts the bridge of the Game of the Goose. On goose boards the bridge stood at
  square 6, and the rule there was to pay and jump ahead: {q|woodChancingItPrint2019|pdf9|458|at number six, where there is a bridge, one pays a toll and advances to the twelfth space}
  [@woodChancingItPrint2019, 458]. The goose game reached Philip II's court shortly before Barros wrote. In 1585 the
  king's jester Gonzalo de Liaño complained to Francesco de' Medici about the new Tuscan game,
  in which, among other things, {q|infantesPinturaQueSe2010|pdf11|135|dan en una puente y meten un real}
  ("they land on a bridge and put in a real") [@infantesPinturaQueSe2010, 134–35]. Lucero
  traces Barros's square to that bridge: it {q|luceroFilosofiaCortesanaJuego2021juin20|pdf13|144|procede más o menos remotamente de la oca original, de ahí que sea preciso pagar el portazgo sobre el Puente}
  ("derives more or less remotely from the original goose game, hence one must pay the toll
  over the Bridge") [@luceroFilosofiaCortesanaJuego2021juin20, 144]. Barros moved the bridge
  from 6 to 15 and made the jump lead to 26 instead of 12 (our observation). On goose boards 26
  is one of the two Dice squares, reached by a lucky first throw of 6 and 3
  [@luceroFilosofiaCortesanaJuego2020, 122; @decaceresTableroItalianoFilosofia2009, 102 n.68].
  Barros's shortcut therefore ends on what had been a lucky square (our observation).

  The Academy's dictionary defines *portazgo* as "Derechos que se pagan por pasar por un sitio determinado de un camino" (dues paid for passing a given point on a road) and derives it from the older *portadgo* [@web:https://dle.rae.es/portazgo]. Barros's first edition, printed by the
  widow of Alonso Gómez, spells it *portadgo*
  [@sanchezEdicionesAntiguasFilosofia2016oct16, 183]. In Barros the toll is a figure of speech:
  the text does not say who is paid. Lucero ties it to Liberality, the first of the four "means" Barros names, which {q|luceroFilosofiaCortesanaJuego2021juin20|pdf13|144|No tiene, a diferencia de los restantes, una casa específica, sino que despliega una influencia transversal a lo largo de todo el itinerario} (unlike the others, has no specific square, but exerts its influence across the whole route); {q|luceroFilosofiaCortesanaJuego2021juin20|pdf13|144|en ella se basa la esperanza que se ha de poner en el objetivo} (on it rests the hope that must be placed in the goal) [@luceroFilosofiaCortesanaJuego2021juin20, 144].

  Traditionally, hope was shown as a ship in a stormy sea [@millanFilosofiaCortesanaAlonso1996, 477]. Alciato's *Spes proxima* has a storm-tossed ship
  saved by the twin stars Castor and Pollux
  [@millanFilosofiaCortesanaAlonso1996, 477; @web:https://www.emblems.arts.gla.ac.uk/alciato/emblem.php?id=A21a043]. Barros's bridge
  breaks with that image, and his text does not describe the picture
  [@decaceresTableroItalianoFilosofia2009, 94]. Collar de Cáceres reads the bridge as the passage
  one must take to reach the far bank: {q|decaceresTableroItalianoFilosofia2009|pdf14|94|El puente así mostrado es el paso obligado hacia otra orilla, que representa la esperanza en prosperar.}
  [@decaceresTableroItalianoFilosofia2009, 94]. Asking "who has influence with so-and-so" [@barrosFilosofiaCortesanaMoralizada1588, pdf 33] is how
  patronage worked in the courts Biagioli studies. Biagioli writes that {q|biagioliGalileoCourtierPractice1993|pdf34|19|an early modern patron was often a broker}, standing {q|biagioliGalileoCourtierPractice1993|pdf34|19|between the client and a higher source of power such as a greater patron}, and that {q|biagioliGalileoCourtierPractice1993|pdf34|19|A client's access to a patron was not just a technical matter of getting to know the right ways to contact the patron} [@biagioliGalileoCourtierPractice1993, 19].
variants: |
  - Spelling of the toll: *portadgo* in G, *portazgo* in M and C
    [@sanchezEdicionesAntiguasFilosofia2016oct16, 183; @barrosFilosofiaCortesanaMoralizada1588, pdf 33].
    Lucero lists this among the errata M corrected
    [@sanchezEdicionesAntiguasFilosofia2016oct16, 183]. The dictionary gives *portadgo* as the older
    form of the word [@web:https://dle.rae.es/portazgo], so G's form may be old-fashioned rather
    than wrong (our observation).
  - In M the petitioner visits "friends and patrons" (*amigos y valedores*)
    [@millanFilosofiaCortesanaAlonso1996, 477 n.73; @debarrosFilosofiaCortesana1587, pdf 6]. C has "friends" only
    [@barrosFilosofiaCortesanaMoralizada1588, pdf 33].
  - Payment: C says one stake "por cada vna" (for each), which leaves open what is being counted
    [@barrosFilosofiaCortesanaMoralizada1588, pdf 67]. M says "por cada una de las dos" (for
    each of the two squares) [@debarrosFilosofiaCortesana1587, pdf 12; @millanFilosofiaCortesanaAlonso1996, 470–71].
  - Rule label: the Naples board reads *Al priuato ·nº· 26. Paga* [board]. In M's worked example
    Pedro reads the label on the lost Madrid board; the modernized transcription gives it as
    "Al privado. un. 26. pi." [@debarrosFilosofiaCortesana1587, pdf 12]. That may be a misreading of *nº* and *pa[ga]*
    (our observation; uncertain).
  - Verse: the book and the board agree except for spelling (*ajena* in the book, *agena* on the
    board) [@barrosFilosofiaCortesanaMoralizada1588, pdf 34; board]. The Italian, *Nulla
    spera[n]za per buona s'intende / che dalla uolonta d'altrui dipe[n]de*, has the same sense
    [board]. The plate reads *per buona* where Collar prints *por buona*
    [board; @decaceresTableroItalianoFilosofia2009, 94].
readings: |
  Readers disagree about what the man is doing with the coins. Collar de Cáceres has him throwing
  coins into the water, as one "paying the toll", and reads the square as paying for favors and
  buying another person's will [@decaceresTableroItalianoFilosofia2009, 94]. Bidwell-Steiner sees {q|bidwell-steinerFilosofiaCortesanaAlfonso2024|pdf6|200|un monedero en la mano izquierda mientras su contenido se escurre entre los dedos de su mano derecha} (a purse in the left hand while its contents slip through the fingers of the right), and takes Barros to mean that the newcomer {q|bidwell-steinerFilosofiaCortesanaAlfonso2024|pdf6|200|tiene que comprar la buena voluntad de sus «amigos» con un «peaje» para avanzar a la «Casa del privado»} (has to buy the goodwill of their "friends" with a "toll" to move on to the "House of the Favorite") [@bidwell-steinerFilosofiaCortesanaAlfonso2024, 200]. Zollinger describes {q|GameGooseLargest|pdf0|web|le nouveau courtisan sur un pont, les mains pleines d’argent} (the new courtier on a bridge, his hands full of money) [@GameGooseLargest].

  Martínez Millán, who had not seen the board, read the square through the emblem of the ship
  in a storm as a hopeful image [@millanFilosofiaCortesanaAlonso1996, 477]. Collar rejects that reading because the board shows a bridge and a man paying [@decaceresTableroItalianoFilosofia2009, 94, 104 n.104]. Seville places at the bridge the bribe of
  "first fruits" and quotes the verse that belongs to square 26
  [@sevilleImportanceTextPrinted2026, 73]. In Barros's text both belong to the Favorite's house
  (our observation) [@barrosFilosofiaCortesanaMoralizada1588, pdf 34]. The La Bella Donna blog
  renders the heading "Steps of Hope" and *privato* as "Private"
  [@TranslationFilosofiaCortesana].
barros_pdf: 32-34, 66-67

=== id: sq20
title_en: Diligence
title_es: Diligencia
board_it: Diligentia
kind: marked
rule_short: No penalty here. Players sent back from "I Thought…" (55) arrive here after paying one stake, to start their diligence again.
table: Sent back from "I Thought…", the idler learns diligence from the beetle straining to roll its ball of dung home; and dung, Barros adds, is all we strive for [@barrosFilosofiaCortesanaMoralizada1588, pdf 49–50].
see: |
  A large beetle, a scarab or dung beetle, seen from above, pushes a big round ball that stands
  up behind it like a disc. It stands on the strip of ground that runs between the balusters
  [board]. The numeral 20 is above it. Under the picture are the heading *Diligentia* and the
  two couplets. The Spanish reads, in English: "For all the world's labor and care, / it's dung
  and rubbish everywhere" [board]. The square has no rule label [board].
why: |
  Barros brings in Diligence only after the square of "I Thought…" (55), the idler who assumes
  someone else will do the work. The idler is told to go back and be diligent, and to watch how
  the beetle does it. With more load than strength, the beetle struggles to push a ball of dung to
  its hole. Everything people pursue is dung in the end, Barros says, and the beetle shows how hard
  the world works and for what [@barrosFilosofiaCortesanaMoralizada1588, pdf 49–50]. So the
  square has two faces. It holds up the beetle's effort as a model, and it judges the prize
  worthless.

  Earlier, in the House of Fortune, Barros rejects the saying that diligence is the mother of good
  fortune. Diligence, he says, is Fortune's daughter, nursed at her breast
  [@barrosFilosofiaCortesanaMoralizada1588, pdf 45–46]. In play, Diligence has no effect of its
  own. It is the place the rules send a player from "I Thought…"
  [@barrosFilosofiaCortesanaMoralizada1588, pdf 68].
context: |
  Diligence is one of the four "means" Barros names for the petitioner's career, along with
  Liberality, Flattery and Labor [@barrosFilosofiaCortesanaMoralizada1588, pdf 24]. Lucero sees several squares of the board arranged on an axis of activity against inactivity [@lucerosanchezAsnoPenseQue2019, 162], and calls this pair the plainest: {q|lucerosanchezAsnoPenseQue2019|pdf3|163|La contraposición más característica en la Filosofía cortesana atañe a Pensé Que y Diligencia}
  [@lucerosanchezAsnoPenseQue2019, 163]. The penalty sends the lazy ass of square 55 back to
  the busy beetle [@lucerosanchezAsnoPenseQue2019, 175].

  The beetle had a long record in emblem books, and it could mean very different things. Juan de
  Borja's *Empresas morales* made it a "valiant warrior" (*Strenuus bellator*) that should shame
  the proud [@luceroIdeaTrabajoFilosofia2019, 830]. Collar de Cáceres adds Pierio Valeriano,
  who gives the beetle many meanings; among them Collar quotes {q|decaceresTableroItalianoFilosofia2009|pdf24|104|“labor y diligencia del hombre solo”} ("the labor and diligence of a man on his own"). He also notes
  darker uses: the blind person in Horozco and the dishonest person in Lorenzo de Zamora
  [@decaceresTableroItalianoFilosofia2009, 94; 104 n.106]. Lucero traces to Valeriano the old
  belief that the beetle rolls its ball from the dung of an ox or an ass. Covarrubias's dictionary
  later repeated it, adding that the ball imitated the shape and motion of the heavens
  [@lucerosanchezAsnoPenseQue2019, 176]. Bidwell-Steiner points to Horapollo's *Hieroglyphica*
  (I.10), where the scarab, thought to be born without a mother, stands for the only-begotten
  [@bidwell-steinerFilosofiaCortesanaAlfonso2024, 204].

  Barros's verse turns the image toward contempt of the world (*de contemptu mundi*). Lucero
  finds that surprising after Borja, and compares it with the Inn of the goose game, which
  scholars also read as a rejection of worldly goods [@luceroIdeaTrabajoFilosofia2019, 830;
  @luceroFilosofiaCortesanaJuego2021juin20, 144 n.15].
variants: |
  - Verse: the 1588 book spells it *vasura* [@barrosFilosofiaCortesanaMoralizada1588, pdf 50],
    and the board *basura* [board]. Wilson reads M as *Quanto trabaja y procura*
    [@wilsonCervantesItemEmmanuel1968, 371]. Martínez Millán prints *Quanto trabajo*, probably a
    slip [@millanFilosofiaCortesanaAlonso1996, 480].
  - The Italian couplet differs in sense: *Quanto il mondo si trauaglia / cosa uana e come
    paglia*, "however much the world toils, it is a vain thing, like straw" [board]. It swaps dung
    for straw, and *trauaglia* echoes *trauaglio*, the Labor squares (our observation). The plate
    reads *Quanto il mondo*. Collar prints *Quanto el mundo* and the La Bella Donna blog *Quando* [board; @decaceresTableroItalianoFilosofia2009, 94; @TranslationFilosofiaCortesana].
  - No differences between G and M are recorded for this passage in the sources we cite.
readings: |
  Collar de Cáceres finds the beetle ambiguous: {q|decaceresTableroItalianoFilosofia2009|pdf14|94|en lo positivo, el esfuerzo, la diligencia en el trabajo en suma; en lo negativo, la basura del mundo que arrastra, y la larvada convicción de que la sola vía del esfuerzo no conduce a la meta deseada} (on the positive side, effort, in short diligence in work; on the negative, the world's rubbish that it drags along, and the latent conviction that effort alone does not lead to the desired goal) [@decaceresTableroItalianoFilosofia2009, 94]. Lucero places the square firmly in the
  contempt of worldly goods. He adds that the petitioner must still be diligent, because diligence is
  what can bend the will of a patron or of Fortune [@luceroIdeaTrabajoFilosofia2019, 830–31].
  Bidwell-Steiner reads the Horapollo source, a beetle that breeds with no female, as {q|bidwell-steinerFilosofiaCortesanaAlfonso2024|pdf10|204|un primer indicio a la tendencia homosocial del texto} (a first sign of the text's homosocial tendency)
  [@bidwell-steinerFilosofiaCortesanaAlfonso2024, 204]. The La Bella Donna blog describes a
  "purple beetle" [@TranslationFilosofiaCortesana], a color the black-and-white engraving does
  not have (our observation).
barros_pdf: 45-46, 49-50, 68
sources: barrosFilosofiaCortesanaMoralizada1588, lucerosanchezAsnoPenseQue2019, luceroIdeaTrabajoFilosofia2019, luceroFilosofiaCortesanaJuego2021juin20, decaceresTableroItalianoFilosofia2009, bidwell-steinerFilosofiaCortesanaAlfonso2024, wilsonCervantesItemEmmanuel1968, millanFilosofiaCortesanaAlonso1996, TranslationFilosofiaCortesana

=== id: sq26
title_en: The Favorite
title_es: Casa del priuado
board_it: Il priuato
kind: marked
rule_short: Pay one stake, as the board's label says (the edition's decision). A player sent on from the Pass of Hope (15) pays here too, after paying at 15.
table: At the Favorite's house the petitioner offers the first fruits of their estate to win a friendly hearing: go to the great with your hands full [@barrosFilosofiaCortesanaMoralizada1588, pdf 34].
see: |
  Two men in cloaks. On the left, a man in a brimmed hat steps forward holding up a round dish or bowl in
  one hand; what it holds cannot be made out. He offers it to a bearded man in a cap on the right, who
  stands in a doorway and reaches out for it [board]. The numeral 26 is cut by the doorway.
  Under the picture are the heading, *Il priuato* ("The Favorite", with the u cut like an n), and the two couplets [board]. The Spanish reads, in
  English: "Before you ask another's hand, / fill yours, or empty-handed stand" [board].
  A small box in the white gutter carries the single word *Paga.*, "Pay" [board].
why: |
  Having reached the Favorite's house, the petitioner offers the Favorite "the first fruits of
  his estate" to win a friendly hearing. The petitioner has first waited for the right moment.
  Experience teaches them how much the gift matters, and they leave saying the verse
  [@barrosFilosofiaCortesanaMoralizada1588, pdf 34]. The gift buys a hearing, not yet the favor
  itself (our reading).

  In play the Favorite costs money. The board says "Pay" [board]. The rules name a payment
  here only within the move from the Pass of Hope, one stake at each square, and say nothing of
  a player who lands here directly
  [@barrosFilosofiaCortesanaMoralizada1588, pdf 66–67; @debarrosFilosofiaCortesana1587, pdf 12].
  Collar de Cáceres reads the board's *Paga* as applying whether the player arrives straight
  from the track or from square 15 [@decaceresTableroItalianoFilosofia2009, 94]. The edition
  follows the board and Collar: every player who lands on 26 pays one stake, whether they
  arrive directly or by way of the Pass of Hope (our decision). Competition works here too. In
  Madrigal's worked example Rodrigo reaches 15, goes on to 26, finds Pedro there and takes his
  place, paying two stakes. Pedro goes back to the square Rodrigo came from and pays one stake for going back [@debarrosFilosofiaCortesana1587, pdf 12].
context: |
  A *privado* was not just any powerful person. Biagioli notes that the favorite was typical of
  baroque courts, and that {q|biagioliGalileoCourtierPractice1993|pdf338|323|In Spain, the title was privado and represented a specific role within court hierarchy.}
  [@biagioliGalileoCourtierPractice1993, 323 n.50]. Bidwell-Steiner places the beginnings of the
  system of royal favorites (*validos*) in Philip II's centralized Madrid court
  [@bidwell-steinerFilosofiaCortesanaAlfonso2024, 209]. In Barros's text the petitioner asks who has
  influence with "so-and-so" and is told it is the *privado*
  [@barrosFilosofiaCortesanaMoralizada1588, pdf 33]. The Favorite here is the person who has the
  ear of whoever grants the favor (our reading). Barros dedicated the book to one such man, Philip
  II's secretary Mateo Vázquez de Leca. Lucero writes that Vázquez had won the king's *privanza*
  (his confidence) by the early 1580s [@luceroDEDICATORIAFILOSOFIACORTESANA2019, 37]. Martínez Millán calls him {q|millanFilosofiaCortesanaAlonso1996|pdf2|462|imprescindible para obtener cualquier gracia} (indispensable for obtaining any grace) or favor from the king at that time [@millanFilosofiaCortesanaAlonso1996, 462 n.5].

  Barros also wrote a short manuscript piece, *Discurso y difinición del privado*, kept among
  Vázquez's papers. It paraphrases Aristotle's *Nicomachean Ethics* on friendship (book 8; Lucero says "capítulo 8") [@luceroDEDICATORIAFILOSOFIACORTESANA2019, 42; @millanFilosofiaCortesanaAlonso1996, 465 n.16].
  In it he writes that {q|luceroDEDICATORIAFILOSOFIACORTESANA2019|pdf10|42|es casi una misma cosa la privanza que alcanza el menor con el mayor que el amistad que se traba entre los iguales}
  ("the favor the lesser wins with the greater is almost the same thing as the friendship formed
  between equals") [@luceroDEDICATORIAFILOSOFIACORTESANA2019, 42]. Collar de Cáceres finds this
  cynical, because Barros also argues that friendship based on usefulness lasts longer than the primary kind
  [@decaceresTableroItalianoFilosofia2009, 84].

  The gift of first fruits belongs to an economy of gifts that historians of court patronage
  describe. Writing about Medici Florence, not Madrid, Biagioli argues that {q|biagioliGalileoCourtierPractice1993|pdf54|39|Gift-giving was the best investment for a client because the patron was bound by his status to reciprocate gifts in proportion to his own (rather than the client's) status.}
  [@biagioliGalileoCourtierPractice1993, 39]. Barros's verse makes the same calculation from the client's side: go to the great with your hands full (our observation; Bidwell-Steiner likewise reads the gift as a climber's calculated investment [@bidwell-steinerFilosofiaCortesanaAlfonso2024, 201]).
variants: |
  - Rule: the board's *Paga.* stands on its own [board]. The books name a payment here only as
    part of the move from 15, "for each" (C) or "for each of the two" (M) [@barrosFilosofiaCortesanaMoralizada1588, pdf 67; @debarrosFilosofiaCortesana1587, pdf 12]. In M's worked example Pedro pays at 26 "for the favor he hopes for" [@debarrosFilosofiaCortesana1587, pdf 12]. The edition charges a player who lands here directly as well, following the board and Collar de Cáceres (our decision) [board; @decaceresTableroItalianoFilosofia2009, 94].
  - Verse: the book and the board agree except for spelling (*ajena* in the book, *agena* on the
    board) [@barrosFilosofiaCortesanaMoralizada1588, pdf 34; board]. The plate reads *No
    pidas*. The La Bella Donna blog's "No(n) pidas" is not supported [board; @TranslationFilosofiaCortesana]. The Italian, *Non
    cercar mano aliena / se la tua non serà piena*, "do not seek another's hand if yours will
    not be full", has the same sense [board].
  - Prose: C reads *aguardado coyu[n]tura, y como de la esperie[n]cia*; the modernized transcription of M reads *aguardando coyuntura, como de la experiencia* [@barrosFilosofiaCortesanaMoralizada1588, pdf 34; @debarrosFilosofiaCortesana1587, pdf 6]. M itself reads *aguardado* (and misprints *pa* for *para*), so the transcription's *aguardando* is its own [@sanchezEdicionesAntiguasFilosofia2016oct16, 184].
readings: |
  Collar de Cáceres reads the square bluntly: {q|decaceresTableroItalianoFilosofia2009|pdf14|94|Sólo el dinero abre las puertas para prosperar por esta vía.} (Only money opens the doors to prospering by this route.) He calls the disillusion it teaches {q|decaceresTableroItalianoFilosofia2009|pdf14|94|un verdadero ejercicio de realismo}
  ("a true exercise in realism") [@decaceresTableroItalianoFilosofia2009, 94]. For Martínez Millán the gift marks the courtier's loss of independence. A petitioner who comes to the Favorite's door already willing to spend their own property in service, and who must shape every act to a superior's liking, ends with a spirit that Martínez Millán calls "pobre y servil" (poor and servile); he finds the complaint already in Antonio de Guevara [@millanFilosofiaCortesanaAlonso1996, 477–78]. Bidwell-Steiner
  identifies the man in the doorway, from his posture and decorated cap, as a wealthy merchant;
  she compares a Neapolitan merchant in Abraham de Bruyn's costume book of 1577. In her words, {q|bidwell-steinerFilosofiaCortesanaAlfonso2024|pdf7|201|la semántica de generosidad pasa de un comportamiento habitual y ostentoso de los nobles a una calculada y necesaria inversión de un trepador social} (the meaning of generosity passes from the habitual, showy behavior of nobles to the calculated and necessary investment of a social climber) [@bidwell-steinerFilosofiaCortesanaAlfonso2024, 200–201].

  Lucero sums up the square as handing over the first fruits to one's patron
  [@luceroFilosofiaCortesanaJuego2021juin20, 144]. The La Bella Donna blog describes "a man
  passing a bag to another" [@TranslationFilosofiaCortesana]. The plate shows a dish or bowl
  [board].

=== id: sq28
title_en: The Dice
title_es: Los dados
board_it: Sorte
kind: dice
rule_short: No effect if you land here. Players from What Will They Say? (36) are sent back here.
table: Back at the Dice the petitioner tries another *suerte*, another throw; but for the unlucky, Barros says, the best throw would be a quick disillusion [@barrosFilosofiaCortesanaMoralizada1588, pdf 37–38].
see: |
  Three dice lie on a small table covered with a dotted cloth that has a scalloped fringe. Their
  upper faces show one, two and three pips [board]. The numeral 28 is beside them. Under the
  picture are the heading *Sorte* (Italian for "chance" or "throw") and the two couplets. The
  Spanish reads, in English: "If luck won't help your case along, / your throw of dice will turn out wrong" [board]. The square has no rule label [board].
why: |
  Barros ties this square to What Will They Say? (36). The petitioner who has "served" the fear of
  gossip goes back to the Dice at 28 "to seek another *suerte* at pressing his case": another throw,
  another way of trying their luck. But for someone unlucky, who meets with *azar*, a losing throw, in everything they put their hand to, the best throw of all would be a quick disillusion. The dice, Barros
  says, are painted showing "a low *azar*" [@barrosFilosofiaCortesanaMoralizada1588, pdf
  37–38]. The board's dice make 1 + 2 + 3 = 6 [board; @luceroFilosofiaCortesanaJuego2020, 124], a poor, low throw; Zollinger already calls it {q|GameGooseLargest|pdf0|web|une chance mineure} (a minor chance) [@GameGooseLargest]. The rules
  give no effect to landing here directly. The square is a place the game sends you to
  [@barrosFilosofiaCortesanaMoralizada1588, pdf 66–69; @decaceresTableroItalianoFilosofia2009,
  94].
context: |
  Two dice words carry the square. *Suerte* was a throw of the dice, and also a person's lot or
  luck. Bidwell-Steiner notes that the term hovers between fortune and destiny
  [@bidwell-steinerFilosofiaCortesanaAlfonso2024, 202]. *Azar* comes from the Arabic *zahr*, "die". Corominas gives the *Libro de Alexandre* as its first documentation; he finds the sense "unlucky throw of the dice" in Alfonso X's book of chess, dice and tables (1283), and "unlucky face of a die" in Nebrija and Covarrubias [@web:https://bibliamedieval.es/bibliateca.es/corominas/DATA/HTML/azar.html]. In the
  three-dice game Alfonso's book calls *azar*, the extreme totals, 3–6 and 15–18, were *azares*,
  and the middle totals, 7–14, were *suertes*
  [@web:https://paolocanettieri.wordpress.com/article/alfonso-x-libro-de-los-juegos-vyvpjuoxc2n0-98/].
  If Barros had that vocabulary in mind, "a low *azar*" would be a total from 3 to 6, and the
  board's 1-2-3 makes exactly 6 (our observation; the sources we cite do not make this link).
  The verse turns on the same word: when petitioning brings no luck, the *suerte* becomes an *azar*.

  On goose boards the Dice squares stood at 26 and 53. A player who threw 9 on their first turn
  went straight to one of them, 26 for 6 + 3 and 53 for 4 + 5. The rule stopped a first-throw 9
  from winning at once by jumping from goose to goose, and a player who later landed on either
  Dice square moved "from dice to dice" [@luceroFilosofiaCortesanaJuego2020, 122–23]. Lucero
  counts the Dice, with Death, among the squares the goose game owed to its arithmetic
  [@luceroFilosofiaCortesanaJuego2020, 124 n.14]. In that game they were "mechanical" squares
  that moved the player; Lucero still classes Barros's Dice that way: {q|luceroFilosofiaCortesanaJuego2021juin20|pdf4|135|las casas de carácter mecánico, como los Dados o los Bueyes (que sustituyen a las ocas), implican un desplazamiento en el jugador que arriba a una de ellas}
  [@luceroFilosofiaCortesanaJuego2021juin20, 135]. But he also shows that Barros cut them loose
  from that arithmetic: {q|luceroFilosofiaCortesanaJuego2020|pdf8|124|los Dados no conservan su posición estándar (se hallan en las casas 28 y 53) y no se representan con el valor supuesto que remite a la regla de la tirada inicial}
  [@luceroFilosofiaCortesanaJuego2020, 124]. Barros's rules attach no move to landing on 28 or 53
  and have no first-throw rule (our observation)
  [@barrosFilosofiaCortesanaMoralizada1588, pdf 66–69; @debarrosFilosofiaCortesana1587, pdf 12]. Collar de Cáceres says so outright: the square carries no
  penalty, and Barros does not use the goose rule that jumps between the two Dice squares
  [@decaceresTableroItalianoFilosofia2009, 94–95]. So here the Dice only receive players sent
  back from other squares.

  Lucero also reads the throw of the dice as the court's own logic. The petitioner's fate is {q|sanchezFILOSOFIACORTESANAALONSO|pdf26|26|identificado con la azarosa tirada de dados, y esta con la más o menos caprichosa voluntad de los señores}
  ("identified with the chancy throw of the dice, and this with the more or less capricious
  will of the lords") [@sanchezFILOSOFIACORTESANAALONSO, 26].
variants: |

readings: |
  Zollinger describes the square as {q|GameGooseLargest|pdf0|web|une chance mineure, les trois dés offrant 1, 2 et 3 points}

  down on the side of bad luck [@bidwell-steinerFilosofiaCortesanaAlfonso2024, 202]. The La
  Bella Donna blog renders the Spanish verse "luck becomes a hazard"
  [@TranslationFilosofiaCortesana].

=== id: sq32
title_en: The Well of Oblivion
title_es: Pozo del oluido
board_it: Pozzo del oblio
kind: marked
rule_short: Miss one round; pay one stake to each player and two to the pot, for the ropes. If someone lands on you here, you take their old square but still miss the round (the edition's decision).
table: The powerful forget even the cases they are most bound to; only "ropes of liberality", winning over those close to the patron, pull a petitioner out [@barrosFilosofiaCortesanaMoralizada1588, pdf 34–36].
see: |
  A round brick well-head, drawn in perspective, with a winch frame and crank handle on its
  right. A man inside the shaft, visible from the waist up, grips the rope with both hands and
  tries to haul himself out [board]. The numeral 32 is cut beside the winch. The heading *Pozzo del oblio* and the couplets are on the square's outer edge, below the picture [board].
  A separate two-line box in the gutter gives the rule in both languages, one of only two bilingual labels on the board (the other is the motto on the palm at 63): *Per le fune ·1· et ·2· fermesi / Para·sogas ·1· y ·2· estese*, "For the
  ropes, 1 and 2; let him stay" [board; @GameGooseLargest].
why: |
  Barros explains the well by the forgetfulness of the powerful. Because they take on so many
  affairs, they forget even those they are most obliged to, and so the Well of Oblivion stands at
  32 as the place "where they throw them" [@barrosFilosofiaCortesanaMoralizada1588, pdf 34–35].
  A player who falls in needs "ropes of liberality" to climb out. That means winning over the
  people who have influence with the player's patron, so that they remind the patron of the
  petition. So, having sat forgotten for one round, the player pays one stake to each player and
  two to the pot [@barrosFilosofiaCortesanaMoralizada1588, pdf 35–36]. The rules say the same: one
  to each player and two to the pot "for ropes", having gone one round without playing
  [@barrosFilosofiaCortesanaMoralizada1588, pdf 67]. The board's label compresses this into "for
  the ropes, 1 and 2; stay" [board; @decaceresTableroItalianoFilosofia2009, 104 n.108]. The verse names the fault: "The ingrate casts, without a thought, / into oblivion all he got."

  Two cases the books do not cover. If another player lands in the Well while you are in it,
  the general rule moves you to the square they left; the sources we cite do not say whether
  you still miss the round. The edition keeps the lost round, since Barros's player gets out by
  paying for ropes, not, as in the goose game, by being replaced by the next to fall in
  [@decaceresTableroItalianoFilosofia2009, 95] (our decision). And with the option of two
  throws at Fortune (51), a player whose extra throw ends in the Well loses any throw still
  owed (our decision).
context: |
  On goose boards the well stood at square 31 [@luceroFilosofiaCortesanaJuego2020, 123 n.12;
  @decaceresTableroItalianoFilosofia2009, 102 n.68]. There {q|woodChancingItPrint2019|pdf9|458|one pays another fine and remains until another player lands on the space}
  [@woodChancingItPrint2019, 458]. The version played at Philip II's court in 1585 had two wells:
  {q|infantesPinturaQueSe2010|pdf11|135|ay dos pozos} ("there are two wells")
  [@infantesPinturaQueSe2010, 135]. Barros moved the well by one square, as Méndez Filesi had already noted [@GameGooseLargest], and changed its rule.
  His player is not rescued by the next person to fall in. The player loses a round and has to pay
  their way out, which Collar de Cáceres finds more logical for a court
  [@decaceresTableroItalianoFilosofia2009, 95]. Collar also rejects Martínez Vázquez de Parga's
  positive reading of the goose-game well [@decaceresTableroItalianoFilosofia2009, 104 n.109].
  Lucero lists the well among the squares Barros took over almost literally from the goose game
  [@luceroFilosofiaCortesanaJuego2021juin20, 144 n.15].

  The payment reverses the Pass of Hope. There the petitioner paid for hope. Here the petitioner pays the other players, standing in for the go-betweens at court, to be remembered (our observation; Bidwell-Steiner likewise treats the other players as the courtier's peers paid to remind the patron [@bidwell-steinerFilosofiaCortesanaAlfonso2024, 201]). The
  sources we cite name no emblem-book model for the well. Rodríguez Mansilla links the forgetting
  to the "river of Oblivion" on which idlers drift in the engraving for *La pícara Justina* (1605),
  a parody of Barros's board [@rodriguezmansillaGRABADOPICARAJUSTINA2019, 190–91].
variants: |
  - Verse: the book reads *El ingrato echa en oluido / Quanto bien ha recebido* (C; M reads the
    same, per Collar) [@barrosFilosofiaCortesanaMoralizada1588, pdf 35;
    @decaceresTableroItalianoFilosofia2009, 104 n.107]. The board's Spanish is *Pone el ingrato en
    oluido / quanto bien hae recibido* [board]. The Italian is *Pone in oblio l'ingrato / tutto il ben che li fù dato*, "the ingrate puts into oblivion all the good that was given him" [board].
    The fixed English translation follows the book.
  - Rule: the books list the payments and the missed round
    [@barrosFilosofiaCortesanaMoralizada1588, pdf 35–36, 67]. The board gives only "for the
    ropes, 1 and 2; stay" [board].
  - Prose: C reads *auiendo estado oluidado vna mano*; the modernized transcription of M reads *habiendo estado olvidado dando una mano* [@barrosFilosofiaCortesanaMoralizada1588, pdf 35; @debarrosFilosofiaCortesana1587, pdf 6]. M itself reads *auiendo estado oluidado una mano*, as C does (C spells *vna*), so the transcription's *dando* is its own [@millanFilosofiaCortesanaAlonso1996, 478 n.].
  - The board reads *hae recibido*, not *recebido* as the La Bella Donna blog prints it, and *sogas*, not *sogar* (so also Zollinger) [board; @TranslationFilosofiaCortesana; @GameGooseLargest].
readings: |
  Bidwell-Steiner sees in the picture {q|bidwell-steinerFilosofiaCortesanaAlfonso2024|pdf7|201|un hombre en porte de ahorcado aferrado desesperadamente a la cuerda del pozo, con el agua literalmente hasta el cuello}
  ("a man like a hanged man, clinging desperately to the rope of the well, with the water
  literally up to his neck"). For her the well doubles the cost of the patronage exchange, and she treats the other players as the courtier's peers: {q|bidwell-steinerFilosofiaCortesanaAlfonso2024|pdf7|201|el cortesano caído no solamente tiene que pagar a sus iguales —es decir, a los otros jugadores— para recordar al mecenas sus grandes esfuerzos, sino que además tiene que pagar a la posta} (the fallen courtier not only has to pay his equals, that is, the other players, to remind the patron of his great efforts, but also has to pay the pot) [@bidwell-steinerFilosofiaCortesanaAlfonso2024, 201]. Wilson, working from M, summarizes: {q|wilsonCervantesItemEmmanuel1968|pdf7|368|Pay the others to remind the favourite of your existence, and pay twice what you pay them into the pool. Lose a turn.} [@wilsonCervantesItemEmmanuel1968, 368]. Collar de Cáceres identifies those
  thrown in the well as people the favorites forget, even the ones they are most obliged to
  [@decaceresTableroItalianoFilosofia2009, 95]. The La Bella Donna blog translates the label "By
  the rope. Stop on 1 and 2" [@TranslationFilosofiaCortesana]. The rules show that the numbers
  are payments, not squares [@barrosFilosofiaCortesanaMoralizada1588, pdf 67].

  The sources do not settle when the player pays: C's prose has the player pay "having been
  forgotten one round", which could mean after sitting out rather than on arrival (our observation)
  [@barrosFilosofiaCortesanaMoralizada1588, pdf 35].

=== id: sq36
title_en: What Will They Say?
title_es: Que diran
board_it: Che diranno
kind: marked
rule_short: Go back to 28, the Dice, and pay one stake.
table: Their money running out, the petitioner would go home but dreads what people will say, until they shake off the fear and try another throw [@barrosFilosofiaCortesanaMoralizada1588, pdf 36–37].
see: |
  A man in a cap and cloak sits on the ground with his cheek resting on his hand, a pose of
  melancholy. Beside him stands a large lopped tree stump with one cut-off branch [board]. The
  heading *Che diranno* and the couplets are in a box outside the square's outer edge. The
  Spanish reads, in English: "Who serves 'What Will They Say?' / must take whatever they pay"
  [board]. A box along the inner edge carries the rule *Alla sorte ·n·° 28 ·Paga*, "To the
  Dice, no. 28. Pay" [board].
why: |
  Barros's petitioner has pressed on through Labor until vexations and disillusions bring them to
  the fear of "what will they say". The fear comes from seeing their ruin. Their hopes no longer
  have the shape they first gave them, and their money is running out before they can finish what
  they began. They would go home, but they dread what people will say about returning
  empty-handed after staking so much. The fear keeps them undecided until they resolve to shake it
  off and do the best they can. The verse is there to push them to that decision
  [@barrosFilosofiaCortesanaMoralizada1588, pdf 36–37]. The petitioner then goes back to the Dice
  at 28 to try another *suerte*, another throw or way of pressing their petition
  [@barrosFilosofiaCortesanaMoralizada1588, pdf 37].

  The rules send the player from 36 back to 28 [@barrosFilosofiaCortesanaMoralizada1588, pdf 67].
  The board adds "Pay" [board]. That fits the closing clause of the rules, a stake paid "for
  each time he goes back, of whatever kind it may be"
  [@barrosFilosofiaCortesanaMoralizada1588, pdf 68–69]. In the Naples text the clause follows
  the overshoot rule, so it may cover only the rebound from 63. Madrigal's worked example,
  where the displaced Pedro pays
  {q|debarrosFilosofiaCortesana1587|pdf12|pdf 12|porque volvió atrás} (because he went back) [@debarrosFilosofiaCortesana1587, pdf 12], suggests that it covers every move back; Zollinger
  reads it that way in his summary of the general rules [@GameGooseLargest]. The edition
  charges a stake for every move back except a move onto Poverty (60) or from Poverty to 53,
  which it reads M as exempting (our reading; see Poverty and the Dice at 53).
context: |
  Lucero shows how long the idea stayed with Barros. More than ten years later his *Proverbios
  morales* (1598) still name the fear as a key to court life: {q|luceroFilosofiaCortesanaJuego2021juin20|pdf12|143|«Ni faltará a quien condene / el miedo de qué dirán»}
  ("nor will there be lacking those who condemn / the fear of what they'll say")
  [@luceroFilosofiaCortesanaJuego2021juin20, 143]. Anti-court writers had long said that a person
  could not leave the court without the success they came for. Lucero cites Castillejo's *Aula de
  cortesanos* and Sebastián de Covarrubias's emblem of the courtier who never leaves, *Tanta est
  fallatia tecti* [@luceroFilosofiaCortesanaJuego2021juin20, 143]. In his reading the square stands for {q|lucerosanchezAsnoPenseQue2019|pdf3|163|una inmovilidad que ata al hombre no ya al ocio, sino a la frustración del logro no obtenido en la Corte} (an immobility that binds a man not to idleness but to the frustration of what he failed to win at court) [@lucerosanchezAsnoPenseQue2019, 163].

  The board pictures a posture familiar from images of melancholy, but Collar de Cáceres denies that it means melancholy: {q|decaceresTableroItalianoFilosofia2009|pdf15|95|La imagen del hombre no representa en suma la melancolía, saturniano humor del temperamento creativo, sino la falta de actividad por un paralizador temor a la opinión ajena.} (In short, the man's image does not represent melancholy, the Saturnine humor of the creative temperament, but lack of activity from a paralyzing fear of others' opinion.) [@decaceresTableroItalianoFilosofia2009, 95]. Barros's
  text does not describe this picture at all [@luceroTableroJuego15882019, 201 n.9]. The sources
  we cite do not comment on the stump.

  In the classic layout 36 is a goose square. Lucero notes that, with What Will They Say? on 36, the Labor squares could not have been evenly spaced in the goose game's manner [@luceroFilosofiaCortesanaJuego2020, 126].
variants: |
  - Rule: the books send the player to 28 and say nothing of a payment at this point [@barrosFilosofiaCortesanaMoralizada1588, pdf 67; @debarrosFilosofiaCortesana1587, pdf 12]. The board adds *Paga*
    [board].
  - Verse: the board has *sireue* (*sic*) for the book's *sirue* [board;
    @barrosFilosofiaCortesanaMoralizada1588, pdf 37]. The Italian is *Chi seru' à quel che diranno
    / pigli il soldo che li danno*, "who serves 'what they will say', let him take the pay they
    give him" [board]. *Soldo* is also a soldier's wage (our observation).
  - Prose: C misprints *le na faltando* for M's *le va faltando*
    [@barrosFilosofiaCortesanaMoralizada1588, pdf 36; @debarrosFilosofiaCortesana1587, pdf 6].
  - G has *y para que ansí haga* where M has *y para que así lo haga* and C *y para que assi lo haga* [@sanchezEdicionesAntiguasFilosofia2016oct16, 186; @barrosFilosofiaCortesanaMoralizada1588, pdf 37]; no other G reading for this square is recorded in the sources we cite.
readings: |
  Bidwell-Steiner reads the square through the honor-and-shame code of Mediterranean societies,
  drawing on the anthropologists Peristiany and Pitt-Rivers. The work, she argues, makes all effort
  worthless without society's approval, and so {q|bidwell-steinerFilosofiaCortesanaAlfonso2024|pdf7|201|este pasaje contradice las interpretaciones que sitúan el texto en una vertiente neoestoica}
  ("this passage contradicts the readings that place the text in a Neostoic line")
  [@bidwell-steinerFilosofiaCortesanaAlfonso2024, 201]. Lucero stresses the player's dilemma:
  they cannot go back, and the court becomes a labyrinth
  [@luceroFilosofiaCortesanaJuego2021juin20, 143]. Collar de Cáceres, as above, rejects a reading
  through melancholy [@decaceresTableroItalianoFilosofia2009, 95]. Wilson calls the square "Fear
  of censure" [@wilsonCervantesItemEmmanuel1968, 368]. The La Bella Donna blog describes "a man
  sitting next to a chopped down tree" [@TranslationFilosofiaCortesana].

=== id: sq53
title_en: The Dice (the throw of eleven)
title_es: Los dados / la suerte
board_it: Sorte
kind: dice
rule_short: No effect here. Players sent from Poverty (60; 59 in the first edition) arrive with one stake of alms from each player, and pay nothing for going back (our reading of M).
table: Poverty's alms bring the petitioner here to try another throw: eleven is usually a good one, but for the poor, Barros says, no throw is good [@barrosFilosofiaCortesanaMoralizada1588, pdf 52–53].
see: |
  Three dice lie on a dice-tray, a board with a raised rim and turned posts at the corners, drawn in
  perspective. Their upper faces show four, four and three pips: a throw of eleven [board]. The numeral 53 is above. Below, on the square's outer edge, are the heading *Sorte* and the couplets.
  The Italian reads *Ogni sorte in mal fin cade / oue alberga pouertade*, "every throw comes to a
  bad end where poverty lodges" [board]. The Spanish reads *Qual quier suerte es de tristeza / en
  la casa do ay pobreza* [board]. The square has no rule label [board].
why: |
  This square receives the petitioner ruined by Poverty (60). They are given alms so that they can
  go and seek another *suerte* at pressing their case, and they go to the Dice at 53. There,
  Barros says, is "a throw of eleven", which is usually a good and welcome throw for the player.
  But for the poor no throw is good, even one they choose. Hence the verse: "In a house where
  poverty lies, / every throw's a throw of sighs" [@barrosFilosofiaCortesanaMoralizada1588, pdf 52–53].

  The rules send the player from Poverty to 53, and "they give him alms"
  [@barrosFilosofiaCortesanaMoralizada1588, pdf 68]. Madrigal's rules specify one stake from
  each player [@debarrosFilosofiaCortesana1587, pdf 12]. M's overshoot rule adds that a player
  who lands on Poverty does not pay but receives a stake from everyone; Wilson and Zollinger
  take this to mean a player who counts back onto 60
  [@wilsonCervantesItemEmmanuel1968, 368; @GameGooseLargest]. The edition applies the exemption
  to the move from 60 to 53 as well, so the poor player pays nothing for going back (our
  reading). The rules
  attach nothing to landing on 53 directly (our observation)
  [@barrosFilosofiaCortesanaMoralizada1588, pdf 66–69]. Collar de Cáceres likewise finds here {q|decaceresTableroItalianoFilosofia2009|pdf16|96|no hay penalidad, ni favor, sólo el adagio} (no penalty, no favor, only the saying) [@decaceresTableroItalianoFilosofia2009, 96].
context: |
  Why is eleven "good"? Lucero, in a note to his edition, explains that eleven is the winning
  number in many betting games [@bidwell-steinerFilosofiaCortesanaAlfonso2024, 204 n.38]. In the
  three-dice game Alfonso X's book calls *azar*, eleven falls among the middle totals, the
  *suertes* [@web:https://paolocanettieri.wordpress.com/article/alfonso-x-libro-de-los-juegos-vyvpjuoxc2n0-98/].
  The two Dice squares may therefore form a pair: a low *azar* at 28, which is bad, and a good
  *suerte* at 53, which poverty spoils (our observation). Bidwell-Steiner notices a snag: {q|bidwell-steinerFilosofiaCortesanaAlfonso2024|pdf10|204|Los números son 4, 4, 3, que, al sumar 11 parecen un error, pues solo faltan 10 para ganar.}
  ("The numbers are 4, 4, 3, which, adding up to 11, look like a mistake, since only 10 are
  needed to win") [@bidwell-steinerFilosofiaCortesanaAlfonso2024, 204 n.38]. Under Barros's
  exact-throw rule, a throw of eleven from 53 would pass the palm at 63 by one and bounce the
  player back to 62, with a stake to pay (our observation)
  [@barrosFilosofiaCortesanaMoralizada1588, pdf 68–69].

  Of the goose game's two Dice squares, 53 is the one whose number Barros kept; in the goose game a
  first throw of 4 + 5 jumped there [@luceroFilosofiaCortesanaJuego2020, 122]. Barros kept the
  number but not the mechanism: his Dice show three dice, not two, and are not tied to the
  first-throw rule [@luceroFilosofiaCortesanaJuego2020, 124]. In Madrigal's rules the players throw two dice, or six *arenillas* (dice marked on one face only) [@debarrosFilosofiaCortesana1587, pdf 11; @web:https://dle.rae.es/arenilla]; G and C speak of *el dado* [@sanchezEdicionesAntiguasFilosofia2016oct16, 189; @barrosFilosofiaCortesanaMoralizada1588, pdf 66]. The three painted dice are a picture of a throw, not the
  equipment of play (our observation).
variants: |
  - The throw of eleven is not in the first edition. G reads, in Lucero's collation, "the Dice,
    fifty-three, but since for the poor man no throw is good". M adds "where there is a throw of
    eleven, which is usually good and welcome for the one who plays". C follows M
    [@sanchezEdicionesAntiguasFilosofia2016oct16, 184; @barrosFilosofiaCortesanaMoralizada1588,
    pdf 52].
  - In G, Poverty stands at 59, not 60: {q|luceroTableroJuego15882019|pdf9|204|Y de la Pobreza, que está a cincuenta y nueve, vuelve a la Suerte cincuenta y tres, y danle limosna}
    [@luceroTableroJuego15882019, 204; @sanchezEdicionesAntiguasFilosofia2016oct16, 180, 189]. M,
    C and the board put it at 60 [@sanchezEdicionesAntiguasFilosofia2016oct16, 180; board].
  - Order of the verse: G has "Cualquier suerte es de tristeza / en la casa do hay pobreza"
    [@sanchezEdicionesAntiguasFilosofia2016oct16, 187]. M and C reverse the lines
    [@wilsonCervantesItemEmmanuel1968, 371; @barrosFilosofiaCortesanaMoralizada1588, pdf 53]. The
    board keeps G's order [board; @rodriguezmansillaGRABADOPICARAJUSTINA2019, 190]. The fixed
    English translation follows the book's (M and C) order.
  - Alms: C has only "they give him alms" [@barrosFilosofiaCortesanaMoralizada1588, pdf 68]. M says one stake from each player [@debarrosFilosofiaCortesana1587, pdf 12]. The label at 60 reads *Limosina et alla
    sorte·53* [board].
  - The plate reads *pouertade*, not Collar's *pobertade* [board].
readings: |
  Zollinger notes that the three dice give eleven points, but that the square's verse undercuts
  the good throw [@GameGooseLargest]. Collar de Cáceres, as noted above, finds only the saying here, and chance appearing {q|decaceresTableroItalianoFilosofia2009|pdf16|96|de nuevo en su forma hostil} (again in its hostile form) [@decaceresTableroItalianoFilosofia2009, 96].
  Bidwell-Steiner sees 53 as another chance to reach the goal with one good throw, and flags the
  eleven as an apparent mistake [@bidwell-steinerFilosofiaCortesanaAlfonso2024, 204]. She also
  writes that the poor player must give alms at 60. Madrigal's rules say the alms come from the other players, as Rodríguez Mansilla reports [@bidwell-steinerFilosofiaCortesanaAlfonso2024, 204; @debarrosFilosofiaCortesana1587, pdf 12; @rodriguezmansillaGRABADOPICARAJUSTINA2019, 190]. The La Bella Donna blog
  renders the verse "All luck is sadness (ends badly) in the house where there is poverty"
  [@TranslationFilosofiaCortesana].
