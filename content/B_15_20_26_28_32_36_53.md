=== id: sq15
title_en: The Pass of Hope
title_es: Passo de la esperança
board_it: Passo della speranza
kind: marked
rule_short: Don't stop here: pay one stake, move on to 26, the Favourite, and pay one more there.
see: |
  A bearded man in a short tunic stands on a brick bridge of two round arches, with water
  pouring out through the arches beneath his feet. He holds up his right hand, and a string of
  coins falls from it; in his left hand he keeps a purse or bag [board]. The numeral 15 is cut
  on either side of his head [board]. Under the picture are the heading *Passo della
  speranza*, an Italian couplet and a Spanish couplet: "No hope is good in any wise / that in
  another's pleasure lies" [board]. A separate box in the white gutter inside the ring, facing
  the square, carries the rule: *Al priuato ·nº· 26. Paga*, "To the Favourite, no. 26. Pay"
  [board].
why: |
  This is the first thing that happens to the new office-seeker after he starts out. He tells
  his friends about his suit, and they praise his merits so lavishly that he comes to rate them
  above what he had even hoped for. He "pays the toll", asks who has influence with the man he
  needs, learns that it is the *privado* (the Favourite), and goes to the Favourite's house to
  ask for help. Because he is new to suing, having to beg hurts him, and he says the verse to
  himself [@barrosFilosofiaCortesanaMoralizada1588, pdf 32–34]. Hope, in other words, costs
  money, and it carries you straight to the Favourite's door. The verse gives the lesson: hope
  that hangs on someone else's will is worth nothing.

  The rule follows the story. According to the 1588 rules, a player who reaches the Pass of Hope
  goes on to the Favourite at 26, paying one stake "for each"
  [@barrosFilosofiaCortesanaMoralizada1588, pdf 66–67]. The Madrid rules of Pedro Madrigal
  (1587) spell this out as one stake for each of the two houses [@millanFilosofiaCortesanaAlonso1996,
  470–71; @GameGooseLargest]. Madrigal's worked example shows the rule in use. Pedro, standing
  on 6, throws 9 and lands on 15. He reads the label above the square and pays one stake to the
  pot "for the good hope that offers itself". When he puts his marker on 26 he pays another
  "for the favour he hopes for" [@GameGooseLargest].
context: |
  The square adapts the bridge of the Game of the Goose. On goose boards the bridge stood at
  square 6, and the rule there was to pay and jump ahead: {q|woodChancingItPrint2019|pdf9|458|at number six, where there is a bridge, one pays a toll and advances to the twelfth space}
  [@woodChancingItPrint2019, 458]. The goose game reached Philip II's court shortly before Barros wrote. In 1585 the
  king's jester Gonzalo de Liaño complained to Francesco de' Medici about the new Tuscan game,
  in which, among other things, {q|infantesPinturaQueSe2010|pdf11|135|dan en una puente y meten un real}
  ("they land on a bridge and put in a real") [@infantesPinturaQueSe2010, 134–35]. Lucero
  traces Barros's square to that bridge: it {q|luceroFilosofiaCortesanaJuego2021juin20|pdf13|144|procede más o menos remotamente de la oca original, de ahí que sea preciso pagar el portazgo sobre el Puente}
  ("derives more or less remotely from the original goose game, hence one must pay the toll
  over the Bridge") [@luceroFilosofiaCortesanaJuego2021juin20, 144]. Barros moved the bridge
  from 6 to 15 and made the jump lead to 26 instead of 12 (our observation). On goose boards 26
  is one of the two Dice squares, reached by a lucky first throw of 6 and 3
  [@luceroFilosofiaCortesanaJuego2020, 122; @decaceresTableroItalianoFilosofia2009, 102 n.68].
  Barros's shortcut therefore ends on what had been a lucky square (our observation).

  *Portazgo* was the charge paid for passing a given point on a road, and the word comes from the
  older form *portadgo* [@web:https://dle.rae.es/portazgo]. Barros's first edition, printed by the
  widow of Alonso Gómez, spells it *portadgo*
  [@sanchezEdicionesAntiguasFilosofia2016oct16, 183]. In Barros the toll is a figure of speech:
  the text does not say who is paid. Lucero ties it to Liberality, the first of the four "means"
  Barros names. Liberality has no square of its own but runs through the whole game, and on it
  rests the hope the suitor places in his goal [@luceroFilosofiaCortesanaJuego2021juin20, 144].

  Emblem books usually showed hope as a ship. Alciato's *Spes proxima* has a storm-tossed ship
  saved by the twin stars Castor and Pollux
  [@web:https://www.emblems.arts.gla.ac.uk/alciato/emblem.php?id=A21a043]. Barros's bridge
  breaks with that image, and his text does not describe the picture
  [@decaceresTableroItalianoFilosofia2009, 94]. Collar de Cáceres reads the bridge as the passage
  one must take to reach the far bank: {q|decaceresTableroItalianoFilosofia2009|pdf14|94|El puente así mostrado es el paso obligado hacia otra orilla, que representa la esperanza en prosperar.}
  [@decaceresTableroItalianoFilosofia2009, 94]. Asking "who has influence with so-and-so" is how
  patronage worked in the courts Biagioli studies. There a patron was often a broker between a
  client and a higher source of power, and getting access to him was more than a matter of
  knowing whom to ask [@biagioliGalileoCourtierPractice1993, 19].
variants: |
  - Spelling of the toll: *portadgo* in G, *portazgo* in M and C
    [@sanchezEdicionesAntiguasFilosofia2016oct16, 183; @barrosFilosofiaCortesanaMoralizada1588, pdf 33].
    Lucero lists this among the errata M corrected
    [@sanchezEdicionesAntiguasFilosofia2016oct16, 183]. The dictionary gives *portadgo* as the older
    form of the word [@web:https://dle.rae.es/portazgo], so G's form may be old-fashioned rather
    than wrong (our observation).
  - In M the suitor visits "friends and patrons" (*amigos y valedores*)
    [@millanFilosofiaCortesanaAlonso1996, 477 n.73; @GameGooseLargest]. C has "friends" only
    [@barrosFilosofiaCortesanaMoralizada1588, pdf 33].
  - Payment: C says one stake "por cada vna" (for each), which leaves open what is being counted
    [@barrosFilosofiaCortesanaMoralizada1588, pdf 67]. M says "por cada una de las dos" (for
    each of the two houses) [@GameGooseLargest; @millanFilosofiaCortesanaAlonso1996, 470–71].
  - Rule label: the Naples board reads *Al priuato ·nº· 26. Paga* [board]. In M's worked example
    Pedro reads the label on the lost Madrid board; the modernized transcription gives it as
    "Al privado. un. 26. pi." [@GameGooseLargest]. That may be a misreading of *nº* and *pa[ga]*
    (our observation; uncertain).
  - Verse: the book and the board agree except for spelling (*ajena* in the book, *agena* on the
    board) [@barrosFilosofiaCortesanaMoralizada1588, pdf 34; board]. The Italian, *Nulla
    speranza per buona s'intende / che dalla volontà d'altrui dipende*, has the same sense
    [board]. The plate reads *per buona* where Collar prints *por buona*
    [board; @decaceresTableroItalianoFilosofia2009, 94].
readings: |
  Readers disagree about what the man is doing with the coins. Collar de Cáceres has him throwing
  coins into the water, as one "paying the toll", and reads the square as paying for favours and
  buying another man's will [@decaceresTableroItalianoFilosofia2009, 94]. Bidwell-Steiner sees
  the purse in his left hand and its contents slipping through the fingers of his right. For her
  the newcomer has to buy his "friends'" goodwill with a toll before he can move on to the
  Favourite [@bidwell-steinerFilosofiaCortesanaAlfonso2024, 200]. Zollinger describes a new
  courtier on a bridge with his hands full of money [@GameGooseLargest].

  Martínez Millán, who had not seen the board, read the square through the emblem of the ship
  in a storm as a hopeful image [@millanFilosofiaCortesanaAlonso1996, 477]. Collar rejects that
  reading because the board shows a bridge and a man paying
  [@decaceresTableroItalianoFilosofia2009, 104 n.104]. Seville places at the bridge the bribe of
  "first fruits" and quotes the verse that belongs to square 26
  [@sevilleImportanceTextPrinted2026, 73]. In Barros's text both belong to the Favourite's house
  (our observation) [@barrosFilosofiaCortesanaMoralizada1588, pdf 34]. The La Bella Donna blog
  renders the heading "Steps of Hope" and *privato* as "Private"
  [@TranslationFilosofiaCortesana].
barros_pdf: 32-34, 66-67
sources: barrosFilosofiaCortesanaMoralizada1588, GameGooseLargest, millanFilosofiaCortesanaAlonso1996, woodChancingItPrint2019, infantesPinturaQueSe2010, luceroFilosofiaCortesanaJuego2021juin20, luceroFilosofiaCortesanaJuego2020, decaceresTableroItalianoFilosofia2009, sanchezEdicionesAntiguasFilosofia2016oct16, biagioliGalileoCourtierPractice1993, bidwell-steinerFilosofiaCortesanaAlfonso2024, sevilleImportanceTextPrinted2026, TranslationFilosofiaCortesana, web:dle.rae.es/portazgo, web:emblems.arts.gla.ac.uk (Alciato A21a043)

=== id: sq20
title_en: Diligence
title_es: Diligencia
board_it: Diligentia
kind: marked
rule_short: No penalty. Players sent back from "I Thought…" (55) must start their diligence again here.
see: |
  A large beetle, a scarab or dung beetle, seen from above, pushes a big round ball that stands
  up behind it like a disc. It stands on the strip of ground that runs between the balusters
  [board]. The numeral 20 is below it. Under the picture are the heading *Diligentia* and the
  two couplets. The Spanish reads, in English: "For all the world's labour and care, / it's dung
  and rubbish everywhere" [board]. The square has no rule label [board].
why: |
  Barros brings in Diligence only after the square of "I Thought…" (55), the idle man who assumes
  someone else will do the work. That man is told to go back and be diligent, and to watch how
  the beetle does it. With more load than strength, the beetle struggles to push a ball of dung to
  its hole. Everything men pursue is dung in the end, Barros says, and the beetle shows how hard
  the world works and for what [@barrosFilosofiaCortesanaMoralizada1588, pdf 49–50]. So the
  square has two faces. It holds up the beetle's effort as a model, and it judges the prize
  worthless.

  Earlier, in the House of Fortune, Barros rejects the saying that diligence is the mother of good
  fortune. Diligence, he says, is Fortune's daughter, nursed at her breast
  [@barrosFilosofiaCortesanaMoralizada1588, pdf 45–46]. In play, Diligence has no effect of its
  own. It is the place the rules send a player from "I Thought…"
  [@barrosFilosofiaCortesanaMoralizada1588, pdf 68].
context: |
  Diligence is one of the four "means" Barros names for the suitor's career, along with
  Liberality, Flattery and Labour [@barrosFilosofiaCortesanaMoralizada1588, pdf 24]. Lucero
  shows that the board is built on pairs of opposites, and that this pair is the plainest:
  {q|lucerosanchezAsnoPenseQue2019|pdf3|163|La contraposición más característica en la Filosofía cortesana atañe a Pensé Que y Diligencia}
  [@lucerosanchezAsnoPenseQue2019, 163]. The penalty sends the lazy ass of square 55 back to
  the busy beetle [@lucerosanchezAsnoPenseQue2019, 175].

  The beetle had a long record in emblem books, and it could mean very different things. Juan de
  Borja's *Empresas morales* made it a "valiant warrior" (*Strenuus bellator*) that should shame
  the proud [@luceroIdeaTrabajoFilosofia2019, 830]. Collar de Cáceres adds Pierio Valeriano,
  among whose many meanings is the labour and diligence of a man working alone. He also notes
  darker uses: the blind man in Horozco and the dishonest man in Lorenzo de Zamora
  [@decaceresTableroItalianoFilosofia2009, 94; 104 n.106]. Lucero traces to Valeriano the old
  belief that the beetle rolls its ball from the dung of an ox or an ass. Covarrubias's dictionary
  later repeated it, adding that the ball imitated the shape and motion of the heavens
  [@lucerosanchezAsnoPenseQue2019, 176]. Bidwell-Steiner points to Horapollo's *Hieroglyphica*
  (I.10), where the scarab, thought to be born without a mother, stands for the only-begotten
  [@bidwell-steinerFilosofiaCortesanaAlfonso2024, 204].

  Barros's verse turns the image towards contempt of the world (*de contemptu mundi*). Lucero
  finds that surprising after Borja, and compares it with the Inn of the goose game, which
  scholars also read as a rejection of worldly goods [@luceroIdeaTrabajoFilosofia2019, 830;
  @luceroFilosofiaCortesanaJuego2021juin20, 144 n.15].
variants: |
  - Verse: the 1588 book spells it *vasura* [@barrosFilosofiaCortesanaMoralizada1588, pdf 50],
    and the board *basura* [board]. Wilson reads M as *Quanto trabaja y procura*
    [@wilsonCervantesItemEmmanuel1968, 371]. Martínez Millán prints *Quanto trabajo*, probably a
    slip [@millanFilosofiaCortesanaAlonso1996, 480].
  - The Italian couplet differs in sense: *Quanto il mondo si travaglia / cosa vana è come
    paglia*, "however much the world toils, it is a vain thing, like straw" [board]. It swaps dung
    for straw, and *travaglia* echoes *travaglio*, the Labour squares (our observation). The plate
    reads *Quanto il mondo*. Collar prints *Quanto el mondo* and the La Bella Donna blog *Quando*
    [board].
  - No differences between G and M are recorded for this passage in the sources we cite.
readings: |
  Collar de Cáceres calls the emblem ambiguous. On the positive side it praises effort and
  diligence. On the negative side it calls the world rubbish and suggests that effort alone does
  not win [@decaceresTableroItalianoFilosofia2009, 94]. Lucero places the square firmly in the
  contempt of worldly goods. He adds that the suitor must still be diligent, because diligence is
  what can bend the will of a patron or of Fortune [@luceroIdeaTrabajoFilosofia2019, 830–31].
  Bidwell-Steiner reads the Horapollo source, a beetle that breeds with no female, as a first
  sign that Barros's court is imagined as a world of men only
  [@bidwell-steinerFilosofiaCortesanaAlfonso2024, 204]. The La Bella Donna blog describes a
  "purple beetle" [@TranslationFilosofiaCortesana], a colour the black-and-white engraving does
  not have (our observation).
barros_pdf: 45-46, 49-50, 68
sources: barrosFilosofiaCortesanaMoralizada1588, lucerosanchezAsnoPenseQue2019, luceroIdeaTrabajoFilosofia2019, luceroFilosofiaCortesanaJuego2021juin20, decaceresTableroItalianoFilosofia2009, bidwell-steinerFilosofiaCortesanaAlfonso2024, wilsonCervantesItemEmmanuel1968, millanFilosofiaCortesanaAlonso1996, TranslationFilosofiaCortesana

=== id: sq26
title_en: The Favourite
title_es: Casa del priuado
board_it: Il priuato
kind: marked
rule_short: No charge if you land here directly. A player sent on from the Pass of Hope (15) pays his second stake here.
see: |
  Two men in cloaks. On the left, a man in a cap steps forward holding up a round dish or bowl in
  both hands; what it holds cannot be made out. He offers it to a bearded man on the right, who
  stands in a doorway and reaches out for it [board]. The numeral 26 is cut by the doorway.
  Under the picture are the heading *Il priuato* and the two couplets. The Spanish reads, in
  English: "Don't seek another's hand, my friend, / unless your own comes full to lend" [board].
  A small box in the white gutter carries the single word *Paga.*, "Pay" [board].
why: |
  Having reached the Favourite's house, the suitor offers him "the first fruits of his estate" to
  win a friendly hearing. He has first waited for the right moment. Experience teaches him how
  much the gift matters, and he leaves saying the verse [@barrosFilosofiaCortesanaMoralizada1588,
  pdf 34]. The gift buys a hearing, not yet the favour itself (our reading).

  In play the Favourite costs money. The board says "Pay"
  [board]. The rules make the Pass of Hope lead here with a payment at each house
  [@barrosFilosofiaCortesanaMoralizada1588, pdf 66–67; @GameGooseLargest]. Collar de Cáceres
  reads the board's *Paga* as applying whether the player arrives straight from the track or
  from square 15 [@decaceresTableroItalianoFilosofia2009, 94]. Competition works here too. In
  Madrigal's worked example Rodrigo reaches 15, goes on to 26, finds Pedro there and takes his
  place, paying two stakes. Pedro goes back to the square Rodrigo came from and pays one stake
  for going back [@GameGooseLargest].
context: |
  A *privado* was not just any powerful man. Biagioli notes that the favourite was typical of
  baroque courts, and that {q|biagioliGalileoCourtierPractice1993|pdf338|323|In Spain, the title was privado and represented a specific role within court hierarchy.}
  [@biagioliGalileoCourtierPractice1993, 323 n.50]. Bidwell-Steiner places the beginnings of the
  system of royal favourites (*validos*) in Philip II's centralized Madrid court
  [@bidwell-steinerFilosofiaCortesanaAlfonso2024, 209]. In Barros's text the suitor asks who has
  influence with "so-and-so" and is told it is the *privado*
  [@barrosFilosofiaCortesanaMoralizada1588, pdf 33]. The Favourite here is the man who has the ear
  of whoever grants the favour (our reading). Barros dedicated the book to one such man, Philip
  II's secretary Mateo Vázquez de Leca. Lucero writes that Vázquez had won the king's *privanza*
  (his confidence) by the early 1580s [@luceroDEDICATORIAFILOSOFIACORTESANA2019, 37]. Martínez
  Millán calls him indispensable for obtaining any royal favour at the time
  [@millanFilosofiaCortesanaAlonso1996, 462 n.5].

  Barros also wrote a short manuscript piece, *Discurso y difinición del privado*, kept among
  Vázquez's papers. It is a paraphrase of book 8 of Aristotle's *Nicomachean Ethics*
  [@luceroDEDICATORIAFILOSOFIACORTESANA2019, 42; @millanFilosofiaCortesanaAlonso1996, 465 n.16].
  In it he writes that {q|luceroDEDICATORIAFILOSOFIACORTESANA2019|pdf10|42|es casi una misma cosa la privanza que alcanza el menor con el mayor que el amistad que se traba entre los iguales}
  ("the favour the lesser wins with the greater is almost the same thing as the friendship formed
  between equals") [@luceroDEDICATORIAFILOSOFIACORTESANA2019, 42]. Collar de Cáceres finds this
  cynical, because Barros also argues that friendship based on usefulness lasts longest
  [@decaceresTableroItalianoFilosofia2009, 84].

  The gift of first fruits belongs to an economy of gifts that historians of court patronage
  describe. Writing about Medici Florence, not Madrid, Biagioli argues that {q|biagioliGalileoCourtierPractice1993|pdf54|39|Gift-giving was the best investment for a client because the patron was bound by his status to reciprocate gifts in proportion to his own (rather than the client's) status.}
  [@biagioliGalileoCourtierPractice1993, 39]. Barros's verse makes the same calculation from the
  client's side: go to the great with your hands full (our observation).
variants: |
  - Rule: the board's *Paga.* stands on its own [board]. The books name a payment here only as
    part of the move from 15, "for each" (C) or "for each of the two" (M)
    [@barrosFilosofiaCortesanaMoralizada1588, pdf 67; @GameGooseLargest]. In M's worked example
    Pedro pays at 26 "for the favour he hopes for" [@GameGooseLargest].
  - Verse: the book and the board agree except for spelling (*ajena* in the book, *agena* on the
    board) [@barrosFilosofiaCortesanaMoralizada1588, pdf 34; board]. The plate reads *No
    pidas*. The La Bella Donna blog's "No(n) pidas" is not supported [board]. The Italian, *Non
    cercar mano aliena / se la tua non serà piena*, "do not seek another's hand if yours will
    not be full", has the same sense [board].
  - Prose: C reads *aguardado coyuntura, y como de la esperiencia*. The modernized transcription
    of M reads *aguardando coyuntura, como de la experiencia*. The difference may be the
    transcriber's [@barrosFilosofiaCortesanaMoralizada1588, pdf 34].
readings: |
  Collar de Cáceres reads the square bluntly: only money opens the door to advancement by this
  route. He calls the disillusion it teaches {q|decaceresTableroItalianoFilosofia2009|pdf14|94|un verdadero ejercicio de realismo}
  ("a true exercise in realism") [@decaceresTableroItalianoFilosofia2009, 94]. Martínez Millán
  sees the suitor arriving submissive and ready to serve even with his own goods. For him this
  made the courtier poor and servile, always acting to please his superiors, as Antonio de
  Guevara had already complained [@millanFilosofiaCortesanaAlonso1996, 477–78]. Bidwell-Steiner
  identifies the man in the doorway, from his posture and decorated cap, as a wealthy merchant;
  she compares a Neapolitan merchant in Abraham de Bruyn's costume book of 1577. For her,
  generosity here has changed from noble display into a climber's calculated investment
  [@bidwell-steinerFilosofiaCortesanaAlfonso2024, 200–201].

  Lucero sums up the square as handing over the first fruits to one's patron
  [@luceroFilosofiaCortesanaJuego2021juin20, 144]. The La Bella Donna blog describes "a man
  passing a bag to another" [@TranslationFilosofiaCortesana]. The plate shows a dish or bowl
  [board].
barros_pdf: 33-34, 66-67
sources: barrosFilosofiaCortesanaMoralizada1588, GameGooseLargest, decaceresTableroItalianoFilosofia2009, biagioliGalileoCourtierPractice1993, bidwell-steinerFilosofiaCortesanaAlfonso2024, luceroDEDICATORIAFILOSOFIACORTESANA2019, millanFilosofiaCortesanaAlonso1996, luceroFilosofiaCortesanaJuego2021juin20, TranslationFilosofiaCortesana

=== id: sq28
title_en: The Dice
title_es: Los dados
board_it: Sorte
kind: dice
rule_short: No effect if you land here. Players from What Will They Say? (36) are sent back here.
see: |
  Three dice lie on a small table covered with a dotted cloth that has a scalloped fringe. Their
  upper faces show one, two and three pips [board]. The numeral 28 is beside them. Under the
  picture are the heading *Sorte* (Italian for "chance" or "throw") and the two couplets. The
  Spanish reads, in English: "If suing brings no luck your way, / the lucky throw turns losing
  play" [board]. The square has no rule label [board].
why: |
  Barros ties this square to What Will They Say? (36). The suitor who has "served" the fear of
  gossip goes back to the Dice at 28 "to seek another *suerte* of negotiating": another throw,
  another way of trying his luck. But for the unlucky man, who meets with *azar* in everything
  he puts his hand to, the best throw of all would be a quick disillusion. The dice, Barros
  says, are painted showing "a low *azar*" [@barrosFilosofiaCortesanaMoralizada1588, pdf
  37–38]. The board's dice make 1 + 2 + 3 = 6, a poor, low throw (our observation). The rules
  give no effect to landing here directly. The square is a place the game sends you to
  [@barrosFilosofiaCortesanaMoralizada1588, pdf 66–69; @decaceresTableroItalianoFilosofia2009,
  94].
context: |
  Two dice words carry the square. *Suerte* was a throw of the dice, and also a person's lot or
  luck. Bidwell-Steiner notes that the term hovers between fortune and destiny
  [@bidwell-steinerFilosofiaCortesanaAlfonso2024, 202]. *Azar* comes from the Arabic *zahr*,
  "die". Its oldest recorded sense is the unlucky face of a die, or an unlucky throw, first
  attested in Alfonso X's book of games (1283)
  [@web:https://bibliamedieval.es/bibliateca.es/corominas/DATA/HTML/azar.html]. In the
  three-dice game Alfonso's book calls *azar*, the extreme totals, 3–6 and 15–18, were *azares*,
  and the middle totals, 7–14, were *suertes*
  [@web:https://paolocanettieri.wordpress.com/article/alfonso-x-libro-de-los-juegos-vyvpjuoxc2n0-98/].
  If Barros had that vocabulary in mind, "a low *azar*" would be a total from 3 to 6, and the
  board's 1-2-3 makes exactly 6 (our observation; the sources we cite do not make this link).
  The verse turns on the same word: when suing brings no luck, the *suerte* becomes an *azar*.

  On goose boards the Dice squares stood at 26 and 53. A player who threw 9 on his first turn
  went straight to one of them, 26 for 6 + 3 and 53 for 4 + 5. The rule stopped a first-throw 9
  from winning at once by jumping from goose to goose, and a player who later landed on either
  Dice square moved "from dice to dice" [@luceroFilosofiaCortesanaJuego2020, 122–23]. Lucero
  counts the Dice, with Death, among the squares the goose game owed to its arithmetic
  [@luceroFilosofiaCortesanaJuego2020, 124 n.14]. In that game they were "mechanical" squares
  that moved the player; Lucero still classes Barros's Dice that way: {q|luceroFilosofiaCortesanaJuego2021juin20|pdf4|135|las casas de carácter mecánico, como los Dados o los Bueyes (que sustituyen a las ocas), implican un desplazamiento en el jugador que arriba a una de ellas}
  [@luceroFilosofiaCortesanaJuego2021juin20, 135]. But he also shows that Barros cut them loose
  from that arithmetic: {q|luceroFilosofiaCortesanaJuego2020|pdf8|124|los Dados no conservan su posición estándar (se hallan en las casas 28 y 53) y no se representan con el valor supuesto que remite a la regla de la tirada inicial}
  [@luceroFilosofiaCortesanaJuego2020, 124]. Barros's rules attach no move to landing on 28 or 53
  and have no first-throw rule (our observation)
  [@barrosFilosofiaCortesanaMoralizada1588, pdf 66–69; @GameGooseLargest]. Collar de Cáceres says so outright: the square carries no
  penalty, and Barros does not use the goose rule that jumps between the two Dice squares
  [@decaceresTableroItalianoFilosofia2009, 94–95]. So here the Dice only receive players sent
  back from other squares.

  Lucero also reads the throw of the dice as the court's own logic. The suitor's fate is {q|sanchezFILOSOFIACORTESANAALONSO|pdf26|26|identificado con la azarosa tirada de dados, y esta con la más o menos caprichosa voluntad de los señores}
  ("identified with the chancy throw of the dice, and this with the more or less capricious
  will of the lords") [@sanchezFILOSOFIACORTESANAALONSO, 26].
variants: |
  - Verse: the book reads *La suerte se buelue azar* [@barrosFilosofiaCortesanaMoralizada1588, pdf
    38]. The board reads *la suerte buelue acar*, with no *se* and no cedilla visible under the
    *c* in either photograph [board]. Collar de Cáceres supplies "(se)" and prints *açar*, as the
    La Bella Donna blog does [board; @decaceresTableroItalianoFilosofia2009, 94].
  - The Italian couplet shifts the sense: *A'cui fortuna è avara / la sorte divien zara*, "for one
    to whom Fortune is miserly, the throw turns *zara*" [board]. It blames a stingy Fortune rather
    than bad luck in suing (our observation).
  - M's prose for this passage matches C's [@GameGooseLargest;
    @barrosFilosofiaCortesanaMoralizada1588, pdf 37–38]. The sources we cite record no G reading.
readings: |
  Zollinger describes the square as {q|GameGooseLargest|pdf0|web|une chance mineure, les trois dés offrant 1, 2 et 3 points}
  ("a minor chance, the three dice showing 1, 2 and 3") and notes that the verse distinguishes
  fortune, throw and chance [@GameGooseLargest]. Collar de Cáceres takes the Dice as an image of
  chance, with {q|decaceresTableroItalianoFilosofia2009|pdf14|94|No hay penalización; sólo un fin didáctico.}
  ("no penalty, only a teaching purpose") [@decaceresTableroItalianoFilosofia2009, 94]. Méndez
  Filesi, who had not seen the board, thought the square came from the goose game's Dice at 26
  but reversed, so that players are sent to it rather than from it [@GameGooseLargest].
  Bidwell-Steiner notes that Barros's text moves straight on to False Friendship: the dice come
  down on the side of bad luck [@bidwell-steinerFilosofiaCortesanaAlfonso2024, 202]. The La
  Bella Donna blog renders the Spanish verse "luck becomes a hazard"
  [@TranslationFilosofiaCortesana].

=== id: sq32
title_en: The Well of Oblivion
title_es: Pozo del oluido
board_it: Pozzo del oblio
kind: marked
rule_short: Miss one turn; pay one stake to each player and two to the pot, for the ropes.
see: |
  A round brick well-head, drawn in perspective, with a winch frame and crank handle on its
  right. A man inside the shaft, visible from the waist up, grips the rope with both hands and
  tries to haul himself out [board]. The numeral 32 is cut beside the winch. The heading
  *Pozzo del oblio* and the couplets run along the outer, left-hand side of the square [board].
  A separate two-line box in the gutter gives the rule in both languages, the only bilingual rule
  label on the board: *Per le fune ·1· et ·2· fermesi / Para sogas ·1· y ·2· estese*, "For the
  ropes, 1 and 2; let him stay" [board; @GameGooseLargest].
why: |
  Barros explains the well by the forgetfulness of the powerful. Because they take on so many
  affairs, they forget even those they are most obliged to, and so the Well of Oblivion stands at
  32 as the place "where they throw them" [@barrosFilosofiaCortesanaMoralizada1588, pdf 34–35].
  A player who falls in needs "ropes of liberality" to climb out. That means winning over the
  people who have influence with his patron, so that they remind the patron of his suit. So,
  having sat forgotten for one round, he pays one stake to each player and two to the pot
  [@barrosFilosofiaCortesanaMoralizada1588, pdf 35–36]. The rules say the same: one to each player
  and two to the pot "for ropes", having gone one round without playing
  [@barrosFilosofiaCortesanaMoralizada1588, pdf 67]. The board's label compresses this into "for
  the ropes, 1 and 2; stay" [board; @decaceresTableroItalianoFilosofia2009, 104 n.108]. The
  verse names the fault: "The ingrate lets oblivion hold / every good he got, untold."
context: |
  On goose boards the well stood at square 31 [@luceroFilosofiaCortesanaJuego2020, 123 n.12;
  @decaceresTableroItalianoFilosofia2009, 102 n.68]. There {q|woodChancingItPrint2019|pdf9|458|one pays another fine and remains until another player lands on the space}
  [@woodChancingItPrint2019, 458]. The version played at Philip II's court in 1585 had two wells:
  {q|infantesPinturaQueSe2010|pdf11|135|ay dos pozos} ("there are two wells")
  [@infantesPinturaQueSe2010, 135]. Barros moved the well by one square (our observation) and changed its rule.
  His player is not rescued by the next person to fall in. He loses a round and has to pay his way
  out, which Collar de Cáceres finds more logical for a court
  [@decaceresTableroItalianoFilosofia2009, 95]. Collar also rejects Martínez Vázquez de Parga's
  positive reading of the goose-game well [@decaceresTableroItalianoFilosofia2009, 104 n.109].
  Lucero lists the well among the squares Barros took over almost literally from the goose game
  [@luceroFilosofiaCortesanaJuego2021juin20, 144 n.15].

  The payment reverses the Pass of Hope. There the suitor paid for hope. Here he pays the other
  players, standing in for the go-betweens at court, to be remembered (our observation). The
  sources we cite name no emblem-book model for the well. Rodríguez Mansilla links the forgetting
  to the "river of Oblivion" on which idlers drift in the engraving for *La pícara Justina* (1605),
  a parody of Barros's board [@rodriguezmansillaGRABADOPICARAJUSTINA2019, 190–91].
variants: |
  - Verse: the book reads *El ingrato echa en oluido / Quanto bien ha recebido* (C; M reads the
    same, per Collar) [@barrosFilosofiaCortesanaMoralizada1588, pdf 35;
    @decaceresTableroItalianoFilosofia2009, 104 n.107]. The board's Spanish is *Pone el ingrato en
    oluido / quanto bien hae recibido* [board]. The Italian is *Pone in oblio l'ingrato / tutto il
    ben che li fu dato*, "the ingrate puts into oblivion all the good that was given him" [board].
    The fixed English translation follows the book.
  - Rule: the books list the payments and the missed round
    [@barrosFilosofiaCortesanaMoralizada1588, pdf 35–36, 67]. The board gives only "for the
    ropes, 1 and 2; stay" [board].
  - Prose: C reads *auiendo estado oluidado vna mano*. The modernized transcription of M reads
    *habiendo estado olvidado dando una mano*. This may be a reading from another witness, or an
    editor's emendation [@barrosFilosofiaCortesanaMoralizada1588, pdf 35].
  - The board reads *hae recibido*, not *recebido* as the La Bella Donna blog prints it, and
    *sogas*, not *sogar* [board].
readings: |
  Bidwell-Steiner sees in the picture {q|bidwell-steinerFilosofiaCortesanaAlfonso2024|pdf7|201|un hombre en porte de ahorcado aferrado desesperadamente a la cuerda del pozo, con el agua literalmente hasta el cuello}
  ("a man like a hanged man, clinging desperately to the rope of the well, with the water
  literally up to his neck"). She reads the double payment as the court's give-and-take
  multiplied: the fallen courtier pays his equals to remind the patron of him, and pays the pot as
  well [@bidwell-steinerFilosofiaCortesanaAlfonso2024, 201]. Wilson, working from M, summarizes:
  pay the others to remind the favourite you exist, pay twice as much into the pool, and lose a
  turn [@wilsonCervantesItemEmmanuel1968, 368]. Collar de Cáceres identifies those thrown in the
  well as people the favourites forget, even the ones they are most obliged to
  [@decaceresTableroItalianoFilosofia2009, 95]. The La Bella Donna blog translates the label "By
  the rope. Stop on 1 and 2" [@TranslationFilosofiaCortesana]. The rules show that the numbers
  are payments, not squares [@barrosFilosofiaCortesanaMoralizada1588, pdf 67].

  The sources do not settle when the player pays: C's prose has him pay "having been forgotten one
  round", which could mean after sitting out rather than on arrival (our observation)
  [@barrosFilosofiaCortesanaMoralizada1588, pdf 35].

=== id: sq36
title_en: What Will They Say?
title_es: Que diran
board_it: Che diranno
kind: marked
rule_short: Go back to 28, the Dice, and pay one stake.
see: |
  A man in a cap and cloak sits on the ground with his cheek resting on his hand, a pose of
  melancholy. Beside him stands a large lopped tree stump with one cut-off branch [board]. The
  heading *Che diranno* and the couplets are in a box outside the square's outer edge. The
  Spanish reads, in English: "Who serves 'What Will They Say?' / must take whatever they pay"
  [board]. A box along the inner edge carries the rule *Alla sorte ·n·° 28 ·Paga*, "To the
  Dice, no. 28. Pay" [board].
why: |
  Barros's suitor has pressed on through Labour until vexations and disillusions bring him to the
  fear of "what will they say". The fear comes from seeing his ruin. His hopes no longer have the
  shape he first gave them, and his money is running out before he can finish what he began. He
  would go home, but he dreads what people will say if he returns empty-handed after staking so
  much. The fear keeps him undecided until he resolves to shake it off and do the best he can.
  The verse is there to push him to that decision [@barrosFilosofiaCortesanaMoralizada1588, pdf
  36–37]. He then goes back to the Dice at 28 to try another *suerte*, another throw or way of
  pressing his suit [@barrosFilosofiaCortesanaMoralizada1588, pdf 37].

  The rules send the player from 36 back to 28 [@barrosFilosofiaCortesanaMoralizada1588, pdf 67].
  The board adds "Pay" [board]. That fits the closing clause of the rules, a stake paid "for each time he goes back, of whatever kind it may be" [@barrosFilosofiaCortesanaMoralizada1588, pdf 68–69]. In the Naples text the clause follows the overshoot rule, but Madrigal's exemption for Poverty implies that it covers every backward move [@debarrosFilosofiaCortesana1587, pdf 12]; Zollinger reads as applying to every backward move [@GameGooseLargest].
context: |
  Lucero shows how long the idea stayed with Barros. More than ten years later his *Proverbios
  morales* (1598) still name the fear as a key to court life: {q|luceroFilosofiaCortesanaJuego2021juin20|pdf12|143|«Ni faltará a quien condene / el miedo de qué dirán»}
  ("nor will there be lacking those who condemn / the fear of what they'll say")
  [@luceroFilosofiaCortesanaJuego2021juin20, 143]. Anti-court writers had long said that a man
  could not leave the court without the success he came for. Lucero cites Castillejo's *Aula de
  cortesanos* and Sebastián de Covarrubias's emblem of the courtier who never leaves, *Tanta est
  fallatia tecti* [@luceroFilosofiaCortesanaJuego2021juin20, 143]. In his reading the square
  stands for a stillness born not of idleness but of the frustration of a goal not reached
  [@lucerosanchezAsnoPenseQue2019, 163].

  The board pictures a posture familiar from images of melancholy, but Collar de Cáceres denies
  that the figure stands for the creative, Saturnine temperament. It shows inaction caused by a
  paralysing fear of other people's opinion [@decaceresTableroItalianoFilosofia2009, 95]. Barros's
  text does not describe this picture at all [@luceroTableroJuego15882019, 201 n.9]. The sources
  we cite do not comment on the stump.

  The square's number came from the goose game. In the classic layout 36 is one of the goose
  squares, and Lucero points out that its presence stopped Barros from spacing the Labour squares
  evenly [@luceroFilosofiaCortesanaJuego2020, 126].
variants: |
  - Rule: the books send the player to 28 and say nothing of a payment at this point
    [@barrosFilosofiaCortesanaMoralizada1588, pdf 67; @GameGooseLargest]. The board adds *Paga*
    [board].
  - Verse: the board has *sireue* (*sic*) for the book's *sirue* [board;
    @barrosFilosofiaCortesanaMoralizada1588, pdf 37]. The Italian is *Chi serv' à quel che diranno
    / pigli il soldo che li danno*, "who serves 'what they will say', let him take the pay they
    give him" [board]. *Soldo* is also a soldier's wage (our observation).
  - Prose: C misprints *le na faltando* for M's *le va faltando*
    [@barrosFilosofiaCortesanaMoralizada1588, pdf 36].
  - The sources we cite record no G reading for this square.
readings: |
  Bidwell-Steiner reads the square through the honour-and-shame code of Mediterranean societies,
  drawing on the anthropologists Peristiany and Pitt-Rivers. The work, she argues, makes all effort
  worthless without society's approval, and so {q|bidwell-steinerFilosofiaCortesanaAlfonso2024|pdf7|201|este pasaje contradice las interpretaciones que sitúan el texto en una vertiente neoestoica}
  ("this passage contradicts the readings that place the text in a Neostoic line")
  [@bidwell-steinerFilosofiaCortesanaAlfonso2024, 201]. Lucero stresses the player's dilemma:
  he cannot go back, and the court becomes a labyrinth
  [@luceroFilosofiaCortesanaJuego2021juin20, 143]. Collar de Cáceres, as above, rejects a reading
  through melancholy [@decaceresTableroItalianoFilosofia2009, 95]. Wilson calls the square "Fear
  of censure" [@wilsonCervantesItemEmmanuel1968, 368]. The La Bella Donna blog describes "a man
  sitting next to a chopped down tree" [@TranslationFilosofiaCortesana].
barros_pdf: 36-37, 67-69
sources: barrosFilosofiaCortesanaMoralizada1588, GameGooseLargest, luceroFilosofiaCortesanaJuego2021juin20, lucerosanchezAsnoPenseQue2019, decaceresTableroItalianoFilosofia2009, luceroTableroJuego15882019, luceroFilosofiaCortesanaJuego2020, bidwell-steinerFilosofiaCortesanaAlfonso2024, wilsonCervantesItemEmmanuel1968, TranslationFilosofiaCortesana

=== id: sq53
title_en: The Dice (the throw of eleven)
title_es: Los dados / la suerte
board_it: Sorte
kind: dice
rule_short: No effect here. Players sent from Poverty (60) arrive with one stake of alms from each player.
see: |
  Three dice lie on a dice-tray, a board with a raised rim and turned posts at the corners, drawn in
  perspective. Their upper faces show four, four and three pips: a throw of eleven [board]. The
  numeral 53 is below. Above the square's outer edge are the heading *Sorte* and the couplets.
  The Italian reads *Ogni sorte in mal fin cade / ove alberga povertade*, "every throw comes to a
  bad end where poverty lodges" [board]. The Spanish reads *Qual quier suerte es de tristeza / en
  la casa do ay pobreza* [board]. The square has no rule label [board].
why: |
  This square receives the suitor ruined by the House of Poverty (60). He is given alms so that
  he can go and seek another *suerte* of negotiating, and he goes to the Dice at 53. There, Barros
  says, is "a throw of eleven", which is usually a good and welcome throw for the player. But for
  the poor man no throw is good, even one he chooses. Hence the verse: "In a house where poverty
  lies, / every throw's a throw of sighs" [@barrosFilosofiaCortesanaMoralizada1588, pdf 52–53].

  The rules send the player from Poverty to 53, and "they give him alms"
  [@barrosFilosofiaCortesanaMoralizada1588, pdf 68]. Madrigal's rules specify one stake from each
  player, and exempt this move from the usual payment for going back [@GameGooseLargest]. The rules
  attach nothing to landing on 53 directly (our observation)
  [@barrosFilosofiaCortesanaMoralizada1588, pdf 66–69]. Collar de Cáceres likewise finds neither
  penalty nor favour here, only the saying [@decaceresTableroItalianoFilosofia2009, 96].
context: |
  Why is eleven "good"? Lucero, in a note to his edition, explains that eleven is the winning
  number in many betting games [@bidwell-steinerFilosofiaCortesanaAlfonso2024, 204 n.38]. In the
  three-dice game Alfonso X's book calls *azar*, eleven falls among the middle totals, the
  *suertes* [@web:https://paolocanettieri.wordpress.com/article/alfonso-x-libro-de-los-juegos-vyvpjuoxc2n0-98/].
  The two Dice squares may therefore form a pair: a low *azar* at 28, which is bad, and a good
  *suerte* at 53, which poverty spoils (our observation). Bidwell-Steiner notices a snag: {q|bidwell-steinerFilosofiaCortesanaAlfonso2024|pdf10|204|Los números son 4, 4, 3, que, al sumar 11 parecen un error, pues solo faltan 10 para ganar.}
  ("The numbers are 4, 4, 3, which, adding up to 11, look like a mistake, since only 10 are
  needed to win") [@bidwell-steinerFilosofiaCortesanaAlfonso2024, 204 n.38]. Under Barros's
  exact-throw rule, a throw of eleven from 53 would pass the palm at 63 by one and bounce the
  player back to 62, with a stake to pay (our observation)
  [@barrosFilosofiaCortesanaMoralizada1588, pdf 68–69].

  Of the goose game's two Dice squares, 53 is the one whose number Barros kept; in the goose game a
  first throw of 4 + 5 jumped there [@luceroFilosofiaCortesanaJuego2020, 122]. Barros kept the
  number but not the mechanism: his Dice show three dice, not two, and are not tied to the
  first-throw rule [@luceroFilosofiaCortesanaJuego2020, 124]. The players themselves throw two
  dice, or six *arenillas* (dice marked on one face only) [@GameGooseLargest;
  @web:https://dle.rae.es/arenilla]. The three painted dice are a picture of a throw, not the
  equipment of play (our observation).
variants: |
  - The throw of eleven is not in the first edition. G reads, in Lucero's collation, "the Dice,
    fifty-three, but since for the poor man no throw is good". M adds "where there is a throw of
    eleven, which is usually good and welcome for the one who plays". C follows M
    [@sanchezEdicionesAntiguasFilosofia2016oct16, 184; @barrosFilosofiaCortesanaMoralizada1588,
    pdf 52].
  - In G, Poverty stands at 59, not 60: {q|luceroTableroJuego15882019|pdf9|204|Y de la Pobreza, que está a cincuenta y nueve, vuelve a la Suerte cincuenta y tres, y danle limosna}
    [@luceroTableroJuego15882019, 204; @sanchezEdicionesAntiguasFilosofia2016oct16, 180, 189]. M,
    C and the board put it at 60 [@sanchezEdicionesAntiguasFilosofia2016oct16, 180; board].
  - Order of the verse: G has "Cualquier suerte es de tristeza / en la casa do hay pobreza"
    [@sanchezEdicionesAntiguasFilosofia2016oct16, 187]. M and C reverse the lines
    [@wilsonCervantesItemEmmanuel1968, 371; @barrosFilosofiaCortesanaMoralizada1588, pdf 53]. The
    board keeps G's order [board; @rodriguezmansillaGRABADOPICARAJUSTINA2019, 190]. The fixed
    English translation follows the book's (M and C) order.
  - Alms: C has only "they give him alms" [@barrosFilosofiaCortesanaMoralizada1588, pdf 68]. M
    says one stake from each player [@GameGooseLargest]. The label at 60 reads *Limosina et alla
    sorte·53* [board].
  - The plate reads *pouertade*, not Collar's *pobertade* [board].
readings: |
  Zollinger notes that the three dice give eleven points, but that the square's verse undercuts
  the good throw [@GameGooseLargest]. Collar de Cáceres calls it "hostile chance", a square with
  neither penalty nor favour, only the saying [@decaceresTableroItalianoFilosofia2009, 96].
  Bidwell-Steiner sees 53 as another chance to reach the goal with one good throw, and flags the
  eleven as an apparent mistake [@bidwell-steinerFilosofiaCortesanaAlfonso2024, 204]. She also
  writes that the poor player must give alms at 60. The rules say the other players give them to
  him, as Rodríguez Mansilla reports [@bidwell-steinerFilosofiaCortesanaAlfonso2024, 204;
  @GameGooseLargest; @rodriguezmansillaGRABADOPICARAJUSTINA2019, 190]. The La Bella Donna blog
  renders the verse "All luck is sadness (ends badly) in the house where there is poverty"
  [@TranslationFilosofiaCortesana].
barros_pdf: 52-53, 68-69
