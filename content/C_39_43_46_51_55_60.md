=== id: sq39
title_en: False Friendship
title_es: Falsa amistad
board_it: Falsa amicitia
kind: marked
rule_short: Go back to 7, the Prodigal, and pay one stake.
see: |
  A fox lies on its back with its legs in the air and its tail curled under it, playing dead. Five birds have come to it: two have landed on its raised paws, and three more fly down from above, near the engraved number 39 [board]. The rule label above the square, in Italian only, reads "Al prodigo n.° 7 Paga" ("To the Prodigal, no. 7. Pay") [board]. Below the square are the heading "Falsa amicitia" and the verse in Italian and Spanish; in English: "The wise, when wronged, give thanks instead: / that's how their business gets ahead." [board].
why: |
  By square 39 the suitor has passed the Dice at 28 and now meets false friends. Barros compares them to the fox that plays dead to catch the birds that come to peck at it: they make themselves humble and easy to deal with and promise impossible things until they have taken what they can [@barrosFilosofiaCortesanaMoralizada1588, pdf 38–39]. His advice is not to break with them. The suitor should hide what he knows, avoid turning friends into open enemies and look content with their goodwill, which is what the verse says: thank them for the injuries [@barrosFilosofiaCortesanaMoralizada1588, pdf 39].
  The penalty sends you back to 7, the Prodigal, because Barros's remedy is to start spending again, lavishly, even on the people you trusted least; at first everyone seems suspect, he says, until worse ones turn up [@barrosFilosofiaCortesanaMoralizada1588, pdf 39–40]. Square 7 shows a pelican giving its entrails, which Barros glosses as its estate, to cats instead of its young [@barrosFilosofiaCortesanaMoralizada1588, pdf 40]. The "Paga" on the board means paying one stake into the pot [@decaceresTableroItalianoFilosofia2009, 103].
context: |
  The fox that feigns death to catch birds is an old animal fable. Bidwell-Steiner traces it to the *Physiologus* and to the Spanish *Libro de los gatos* (exemplum 50) [@bidwell-steinerFilosofiaCortesanaAlfonso2024, 202]; Lucero notes that the tale is less common than is often assumed and that its antecedent is the *Physiologus* [@luceroFilosofiaCortesanaJuego2021juin20, 142].
  Emblem books used false friendship as a warning. Joannes Sambucus's "Fictus Amicus" (*Emblemata*, Antwerp 1564) shows two men shaking hands, one of them wearing a fox skin, and the 1716 edition of Ripa gives "Falsa amicizia" a hyena [@decaceresTableroItalianoFilosofia2009, 96–97, 104]. Hernando de Soto, a close friend of Barros, has an emblem "Amicus infidus", and Antonio de Guevara wrote that what a courtier most needs is one faithful friend who will rebuke rather than flatter him [@luceroFilosofiaCortesanaJuego2021juin20, 142]. Lucero calls false friendship an anti-court commonplace [@luceroFilosofiaCortesanaJuego2021juin20, 142].
  Barros also wrote on friendship himself. His short *Discurso y difinición del privado*, kept among Mateo Vázquez's papers, paraphrases book 8 of Aristotle's *Nicomachean Ethics* and argues that the favour a lesser man wins from a greater is almost the same thing as friendship between equals [@luceroDEDICATORIAFILOSOFIACORTESANA2019, 40, 42]. Collar de Cáceres calls this view cynical [@decaceresTableroItalianoFilosofia2009, 84]. Martínez Millán describes dissimulation as the basic rule of court life, citing Guevara among others [@millanFilosofiaCortesanaAlonso1996, 477–478]. Half a century later Saavedra Fajardo still accepted dissimulation in a ruler, but through the arts of the serpent, not those of the fox [@luceroFilosofiaCortesanaJuego2021juin20, 142].
variants: |
  Board: the Spanish verse is engraved "Dondo gra[cia]s por agrauios / negocian los honbres sabios", with "Dondo" a slip for "Dando" and "gras" carrying an abbreviation stroke [board]. Collar also reads "Dondo (sic)" [@decaceresTableroItalianoFilosofia2009, 95]; Zollinger, as reproduced on giochidelloca.it, prints "Donda" [@GameGooseLargest]. The Italian verse, "Rendon gratie per aggrauij / quando negotian gli sauij", turns the saying round: they give thanks for injuries when the wise do business [board].
  Books: C prints "Dando gracias por agrauios; / Negocian los hombres sabios." [@barrosFilosofiaCortesanaMoralizada1588, pdf 39], and M has the same couplet [@debarrosFilosofiaCortesana1587, pdf 7; @wilsonCervantesItemEmmanuel1968, 370]. In the prose, C reads "hasta cojer lo que pueden" and "hasta que se topan otros peores" where the modernized M text reads "puedan" and "se topan con otros peores" [@barrosFilosofiaCortesanaMoralizada1588, pdf 39–40; @debarrosFilosofiaCortesana1587, pdf 7].
  Rule: the board adds "Paga"; the rules at the end of C say only that from 39 one goes back to the Prodigal, at 7 [@barrosFilosofiaCortesanaMoralizada1588, pdf 67; board].
readings: |
  Collar de Cáceres takes the fox as the suitor himself: {q|decaceresTableroItalianoFilosofia2009|pdf16|96|La zorra es el Engaño, pero no el ajeno sino el que ha de ejercer el propio aspirante para el logro de aquello que pretende} (the fox is Deceit, not other people's but the deceit the suitor himself must practise to get what he seeks). He sees this as the reverse of emblem books that warn against false friends [@decaceresTableroItalianoFilosofia2009, 96].
  Lucero reads the square at first sight as a warning about other people, but also the other way round, as a lesson in discreet (not prudent) means such as dissimulation, {q|luceroFilosofiaCortesanaJuego2021juin20|pdf11|142|incluso convertirse en la raposa, si es menester, ser cazador y no presa} (even becoming the fox, if need be: hunter, not prey). He links the square to Barros's two decades at court [@luceroFilosofiaCortesanaJuego2021juin20, 142].
  Bidwell-Steiner calls the animal a vixen and counts her among the board's three female figures, all negative or ambiguous (with Flattery's siren and Fortune) [@bidwell-steinerFilosofiaCortesanaAlfonso2024, 202]. Wilson describes the fox as shamming sleep [@wilsonCervantesItemEmmanuel1968, 368], whereas Barros's text has it playing dead [@barrosFilosofiaCortesanaMoralizada1588, pdf 38]. The La Bella Donna blog took it for a wolf [@TranslationFilosofiaCortesana].

=== id: sq43
title_en: The Change of Ministers
title_es: Mudança de ministros
board_it: Mutanza d'ministri
kind: marked
rule_short: Go back to 10, Flattery, and pay one stake.
see: |
  Two bearded men in long gowns stand face to face. The man on the left, in a cloak, grips a tall staff that stands upright between them; the man on the right, in a buttoned gown, reaches his right hand towards it and holds his hat in his left (our observation) [board]. Collar de Cáceres reads the staff as a baton of command being handed over [@decaceresTableroItalianoFilosofia2009, 96]. The rule label along the inner edge, in Italian only, reads "Alla adulatione n.° 10 Paga" ("To Flattery, no. 10. Pay") [board]. Outside the square are the heading "Mutanza d'ministri" and the verse in Italian and Spanish; in English: "Who narrows all his hope to one / must bear the blow when change has come." [board].
why: |
  Here the suitor has spent all his strength winning over some important man, the minister who handles his business and on whom he was counting, and then that minister is moved, or the suitor is; left alone and without a welcome, he can only say the verse [@barrosFilosofiaCortesanaMoralizada1588, pdf 41–42]. The rule sends him back to 10, Flattery, to bow to whoever now holds the post [@barrosFilosofiaCortesanaMoralizada1588, pdf 42].
  Lucero points out that the backward move makes sense as a story: the man who suffers a change of ministers must begin flattering a new patron, so he goes from 43 back to 10, and the reverse would make no sense [@luceroFilosofiaCortesanaJuego2020, 125]. He reads the verse as a reproach: {q|luceroFilosofiaCortesanaJuego2021juin20|pdf14|145|se reprocha al solicitante haberse atenido a un solo valedor} (the suitor is blamed for relying on a single patron).
context: |
  Barros dedicated the book to Mateo Vázquez de Leca, Philip II's secretary [@barrosFilosofiaCortesanaMoralizada1588, pdf 7], who had become secretary in 1573 [@wilsonCervantesItemEmmanuel1968, 365, 367]. Martínez Millán places Barros in the "castellanista" faction that formed round Vázquez after Cardinal Diego de Espinosa's death in 1572. It was opposed to a Rome-leaning "papista" group that included Don Juan of Austria, the secretary Antonio Pérez and the Princess of Éboli [@millanFilosofiaCortesanaAlonso1996, 463–464].
  That rivalry ended in the murder in Madrid of Don Juan's envoy and the fall of Antonio Pérez. Bidwell-Steiner attributes the plot to the castellanistas led by Vázquez, and follows Lucero in reading Barros's book as part of that contest [@bidwell-steinerFilosofiaCortesanaAlfonso2024, 210], and Wilson says Vázquez probably helped bring about Pérez's disgrace [@wilsonCervantesItemEmmanuel1968, 365, 367]. An encyclopedia entry dates the murder of the envoy, Juan de Escobedo, to 31 March 1578, the arrest of Pérez and the princess to 28 July 1579, and Pérez's flight to Aragon to April 1590 [@web:https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antonio_P%C3%A9rez_(statesman)]. So when Barros wrote, the most spectacular change of ministers of the reign was within recent memory, and its winner was his dedicatee (our observation).
  By 1587 Vázquez's own star was falling. Lucero dates his decline to about 1585, made worse by the creation of the Junta de Noche [@luceroDEDICATORIAFILOSOFIACORTESANA2019, 37]. Martínez Millán says the faction peaked after 1578, was strengthened by the union with Portugal in 1580, declined from 1586 to 1590, and lost most of its members to death in the early 1590s [@millanFilosofiaCortesanaAlonso1996, 464]. Vázquez died in 1591 [@luceroDedicatoriaProverbiosMorales2019, 109 n. 21]. Barros later saw another change of this kind at first hand: his patron García de Loaysa was pushed aside by Philip III and Lerma within days of the new reign in 1598 [@luceroDedicatoriaProverbiosMorales2019, 112].
  Biagioli describes the same pattern at the papal court, where each new pope brought his own nephews and clients to power [@biagioliGalileoCourtierPractice1993, 316]. He quotes Panfilo Persico's remark that at Rome {q|biagioliGalileoCourtierPractice1993|pdf335|320|everybody turns himself toward the rising sun, leaving the setting one}. Biagioli also argues that big leaps in status could come only from a single great patron [@biagioliGalileoCourtierPractice1993, 29], which is exactly the dependence Barros's verse warns against (our observation).
variants: |

Collar prints "di mutanza" and "Quien", and Zollinger, as reproduced on giochidelloca.it, "Che limita su speranza" [board; @decaceresTableroItalianoFilosofia2009, 96; @GameGooseLargest].
  Books: C prints "Quien limita su esperapca / Sufra el golpe de mudança", a misprint [@barrosFilosofiaCortesanaMoralizada1588, pdf 42]; M reads "Quien limita su esperanza, sufra el golpe de mudanza" [@debarrosFilosofiaCortesana1587, pdf 7]. Lucero records that G gives a square number as the numeral "43" where M spells out "cuarenta y tres casas" [@sanchezEdicionesAntiguasFilosofia2016oct16, 186]; he does not say where, but in M that phrase occurs only in this passage (our observation).
  Rule: the board adds "Paga"; the rules at the end of C say only that from 43 one goes back to Flattery, at 10 [@barrosFilosofiaCortesanaMoralizada1588, pdf 67; board].
  Numbering: Biagioli, working from Geoffrey Parker, calls square 43 "Your patron dies" [@biagioliGalileoCourtierPractice1993, 35]; in the 1588 book and on the board the patron dies at 46, and 43 is the Change of Ministers [@barrosFilosofiaCortesanaMoralizada1588, pdf 41, 44; board].
readings: |
  Collar de Cáceres describes the image as {q|decaceresTableroItalianoFilosofia2009|pdf16|96|un hombre que entrega un bastón, simbolizando el relevo en el mando} (a man handing over a staff, standing for the change of command) and calls the square a particularly serious setback [@decaceresTableroItalianoFilosofia2009, 96]. Lucero pairs it with the Death of the Patron as the board's two critical moments, both of which punish reliance on one patron [@luceroFilosofiaCortesanaJuego2021juin20, 145]. Seville reads the move to 10 as going back to flatter the incoming minister [@sevilleImportanceTextPrinted2026, 73].
  The engraving leaves open who is who. The man holding his hat may be the suitor making his bow to the new holder of the staff, as Barros's rule requires, or the outgoing minister (our observation). The La Bella Donna blog calls the scene "two men meeting" [@TranslationFilosofiaCortesana].

=== id: sq46
title_en: The Death of the Patron
title_es: Muerte del valedor
board_it: Morte de lo ualitore
kind: marked
rule_short: Your patron has died: go back to the start and begin again, paying one stake.
see: |
  A bearded man in a broad-brimmed hat and a short cloak walks away from an empty funeral bier with one hand raised to his face. The bier is a coffin-shaped frame covered with a pall marked with a cross, propped at a slant on its two carrying poles (our observation of the details) [board]. Collar de Cáceres identifies the man as the blind man of the verse [@decaceresTableroItalianoFilosofia2009, 96]. The engraving shows him with no stick or guide (our observation). The rule label along the inner edge, in Italian only, reads "In camincia de nuouo ·Paga·" ("Begin anew. Pay") [board]. Outside the square are the heading "Morte de lo ualitore" and the verse in Italian and Spanish; in English: "Who trusts in men is left, you'll find, / without a guide, like one gone blind." [board].
why: |
  Just when the suitor expected to reach the goal of his desire, the man who most favoured him dies. From square 46 he goes back in tears to begin the game again and look for another patron's favour [@barrosFilosofiaCortesanaMoralizada1588, pdf 44]. The verse explains why: a man who puts his trust in men is left like a blind man with no one to lead him [@barrosFilosofiaCortesanaMoralizada1588, pdf 44]. The hand at the man's face may show those tears (our observation).
  This is the harshest square on the board: everything gained is lost. Collar de Cáceres writes {q|decaceresTableroItalianoFilosofia2009|pdf16|96|Pero el mayor revés está en la Muerte del Valedor} (but the greatest setback is the Death of the Patron). Lucero stresses that here {q|luceroFilosofiaCortesanaJuego2021juin20|pdf14|145|Aquí la muerte solo admite una interpretación recta, no simbólica, como en el juego de la oca.} (death allows only a literal reading, not a symbolic one, as in the Game of the Goose).
context: |
  In the Game of the Goose that Barros adapted, square 58 is Death, which sends the player back to the start [@decaceresTableroItalianoFilosofia2009, 96, 102; @sevilleImportanceTextPrinted2026, 73]. Lucero explains Death's fixed place at 58 as a remedy for a flaw in the goose game's numbers. He argues that Barros, by turning Death into the death of a patron and moving it to 46, dropped that numerical logic without even raising the problem [@luceroFilosofiaCortesanaJuego2020, 123–124].
  Biagioli's study of Galileo shows how real this square was. He writes that {q|biagioliGalileoCourtierPractice1993|pdf49|34|deaths of great patrons—especially of monarchs like popes, who were not members of a hereditary dynasty—were perceived by contemporaries as major patronage crises.} He describes clients' careers as paths broken by such crises [@biagioliGalileoCourtierPractice1993, 30]. After Cosimo II died in 1621, for instance, Galileo could not be sure that the new ruler would confirm his post [@biagioliGalileoCourtierPractice1993, 279], and the mathematician Federico Commandino twice lost papal patronage when his pope died [@biagioliGalileoCourtierPractice1993, 318]. Biagioli cites Barros's game, through Geoffrey Parker, as proof that the pattern was well known: {q|biagioliGalileoCourtierPractice1993|pdf50|35|those who landed on square 43 ("Your patron dies") had to go back to the start.}
  Barros's own career followed this square. His patron Mateo Vázquez died in 1591 [@luceroDedicatoriaProverbiosMorales2019, 109 n. 21]. His next patron, García de Loaysa, archbishop of Toledo and dedicatee of the *Proverbios morales*, was sidelined in 1598 and died in February 1599. Lucero reads this as Barros's own Death of the Patron, which {q|luceroDedicatoriaProverbiosMorales2019|pdf20|120|devuelve a Barros a la casilla de salida de su tablero de la Filosofía cortesana} (sends Barros back to the starting square of his board) [@luceroDedicatoriaProverbiosMorales2019, 112].
  Collar found no model for this emblem in the emblem books and takes it to be Barros's own invention. He notes that a bier appears on another Italian board, the *Gioco del Barone* (square 55) [@decaceresTableroItalianoFilosofia2009, 96, 104]. The verse recalls Jeremiah 17:5, "Cursed is the man that trusteth in man" (our observation; none of the sources we cite makes this link).
variants: |
  Board: the rule is engraved "In camincia de nuouo ·Paga·" ("camincia", with a, in both photographs), while Collar, Zollinger and the La Bella Donna blog all print "Incomincia" [board; @decaceresTableroItalianoFilosofia2009, 96; @GameGooseLargest; @TranslationFilosofiaCortesana]. The Italian verse is engraved "L'huom ch'in huomo si confida / resta cieco senza guida" (Collar "chi in", the blog "cieca"); the Spanish reads "El hombre que en ho[m]bres fia / queda qual ciego sin guia" [board].
  Books: C has the same Spanish couplet as the board [@barrosFilosofiaCortesanaMoralizada1588, pdf 44], and so does M [@debarrosFilosofiaCortesana1587, pdf 7]. G misprints the square's name as "Muerte del velador", which M corrects to "valedor" [@sanchezEdicionesAntiguasFilosofia2016oct16, 183]. Lucero lists the suitor's return "with tears" as an M addition [@sanchezEdicionesAntiguasFilosofia2016oct16, 184]; C keeps it [@barrosFilosofiaCortesanaMoralizada1588, pdf 44].
  Rule: the board adds "Paga"; the rules at the end of C say only that from 46 one begins the game again [@barrosFilosofiaCortesanaMoralizada1588, pdf 68; board].
  Numbering: Biagioli, working from Parker, puts the patron's death at square 43 [@biagioliGalileoCourtierPractice1993, 35]. Barros's text and the board both give 46 [@barrosFilosofiaCortesanaMoralizada1588, pdf 44, 68; board].
readings: |
  Collar de Cáceres sees the square as the suitor's greatest reverse and a sign of Barros's disillusion: all the work done so far has been for nothing [@decaceresTableroItalianoFilosofia2009, 96]. Lucero takes it literally and pairs it with the Change of Ministers, since in both the suitor is blamed for relying on one patron [@luceroFilosofiaCortesanaJuego2021juin20, 145]. Seville compares the rule to the Goose game's death square [@sevilleImportanceTextPrinted2026, 73]. Méndez Filesi, writing before the board was found, made the same comparison with the Goose game's Death at 58 [@GameGooseLargest]. Biagioli treats the square as the emblem of a patronage crisis, though his square number differs from Barros's [@biagioliGalileoCourtierPractice1993, 35]. The La Bella Donna blog renders *valedor* as "helper" and the image as "a man at a grave" [@TranslationFilosofiaCortesana].

=== id: sq51
title_en: The House of Fortune
title_es: Casa de la fortuna
board_it: Casa della fortuna
kind: marked
rule_short: Fortune smiles: throw the dice again (you play twice).
see: |
  Fortune stands with one foot on a sphere: a nude young woman with wings and a single face [board; @decaceresTableroItalianoFilosofia2009, 96]. With her left hand she holds a long banderole that curls down to the ground, inscribed in Italian "cambio et / muto il consiglio" ("I change and alter counsel") [board]. Her right hand rests on the rim of a spoked wheel mounted on a post, and behind the wheel a man tumbles head-first with his arms hanging down (our observation of the hand) [board]. A tuft of hair seems to blow up from the crown of her head, beside the engraved number 51 (our observation) [board]. Below are the heading "Casa della fortuna" and the verse in Italian and Spanish; in English: "All things are at the disposition / of Fortune and of God's permission." [board]. The rule label reads "Giuoca ·2· uolte" ("Play twice") [board].
why: |
  After adverse fortune comes good fortune, Barros writes: no one is so unlucky that she never comes to his door to help him, if he knows how to seize the moment [@barrosFilosofiaCortesanaMoralizada1588, pdf 44–45]. Her motto, in the book "I exchange and alter counsel.", means that she disposes our plans and guides our actions, deciding whether we make our efforts or leave them undone. It follows, he says, that diligence is not the mother of good luck, as people claim, but her daughter, nursed at her breast [@barrosFilosofiaCortesanaMoralizada1588, pdf 45–46]. Then he corrects himself: properly speaking there is no Fortune, only a disposition of the will of God, whose purpose is to teach us to bear our troubles with patience [@barrosFilosofiaCortesanaMoralizada1588, pdf 46]. Hence the verse joins Fortune and (divine) permission.
  The player who lands here plays twice, like a lucky man, to test whether he really is lucky, since too much luck often harms someone who does not know how to use it. From 51, Barros says, some throws can win the pot and others lose it or put it off [@barrosFilosofiaCortesanaMoralizada1588, pdf 47]. In his list of the suitor's hazards he names "fortune ill used" [@barrosFilosofiaCortesanaMoralizada1588, pdf 24]. Lucero thinks Fortune sits at 51 so that a double six on the extra throw can reach 63 [@luceroFilosofiaCortesanaJuego2020, 125].
context: |
  The board's Fortune combines two traditions, according to Collar de Cáceres: the medieval wheel of Fortune that lifts some and throws down others, as in Gregor Reisch's *Margarita philosophica* (1503), and the Renaissance nude Fortune standing on a sphere, often winged and often merged with Occasion [@decaceresTableroItalianoFilosofia2009, 96]. Collar and Lucero also see the whole board's spiral of balusters as an unrolled wheel of Fortune [@decaceresTableroItalianoFilosofia2009, 88, 90; @luceroFilosofiaCortesanaJuego2021juin20, 147].
  The Madrigal edition (M) added a separate woodcut of Fortune, with a long description; Collar de Cáceres calls it the only image in the book's text, though M's title page also carries Vázquez's coat of arms [@luceroDEDICATORIAFILOSOFIACORTESANA2019, 38; @decaceresTableroItalianoFilosofia2009, 90]. It is an oval framed by the motto NO SERIA FORTVNA SI FVESE SIEMPRE VNA ("Were she always one and same, / she would not deserve the name."). It shows her with two faces, young, nude and winged, on a sphere, holding a palm and an ox-yoke (victory and defeat) and a rattle and a sword (joy and rigour), with a forelock [@decaceresTableroItalianoFilosofia2009, 90; @wilsonCervantesItemEmmanuel1968, 366, 368–369]. Barros explains each element. The globe is the universe she rules; she is nude because she acts without consideration; her arms hold up the weak and throw down the proud; she is young because her strength never fails; her wings let her fly from her favourites; her two faces are kind to some and harsh to others. The palm and yoke show that yesterday's victor may be today's slave, and the *sonaja* (a jingling rattle) and the sword show death in the midst of pleasure [@barrosFilosofiaCortesanaMoralizada1588, pdf 61–65; @debarrosFilosofiaCortesana1587, pdf 10–11]. Wilson found most of these attributes in the mythographers Pérez de Moya and Baltasar de Vitoria, but {q|wilsonCervantesItemEmmanuel1968|pdf8|369|the yoke and the timbrel, however, seem unusual}. For Collar, {q|decaceresTableroItalianoFilosofia2009|pdf10|90|Esta imagen de la Fortuna (fig. 11) con dos rostros es no obstante más propia del siglo XV} (this two-faced image is more typical of the fifteenth century), as in Hans Burgkmair's woodcut for Pistorius's *Dialogus de Fato et Fortuna* [@decaceresTableroItalianoFilosofia2009, 90]. Barros owned Garimberto's book on "changeable Fortune" (Salamanca 1572) and probably Petrarch's *Remedies against Both Kinds of Fortune* [@decaceresTableroItalianoFilosofia2009, 90].
  Fortune and Providence: Martínez Millán reads Barros's correction (no Fortune, only God's will) as a Counter-Reformation Christianizing of the pagan goddess [@millanFilosofiaCortesanaAlonso1996, 480]. Lucero calls it a resignation between Senecan and Christian, and recalls, after Badillo, Christian writers' fear that Fortune might displace Providence [@luceroFilosofiaCortesanaJuego2021juin20, 147]. Biagioli notes that Fortune and the dangers of court intrigue had been favourite themes of court treatises since the fifteenth century [@biagioliGalileoCourtierPractice1993, 324].
  Occasion: the board's top-right corner shows Occasion, a half-reclining woman, bare to the waist, with a single lock of hair and the motto "No me pierdas" ("Don't lose me.") [board]; Collar de Cáceres describes her as seated and clothed [@decaceresTableroItalianoFilosofia2009, 93]. Barros describes her as a woman with a handful of hair on her forehead and a bald head [@barrosFilosofiaCortesanaMoralizada1588, pdf 58]. In M he gives the forelock to Fortune too, as a warning to seize the chance before she turns and shows her bald head [@debarrosFilosofiaCortesana1587, pdf 10]. Collar traces the merger of the two figures to Alciato's emblem "In occasionem", with its razor, and to Panofsky's account of their fusion [@decaceresTableroItalianoFilosofia2009, 93]. Lucero observes that {q|luceroFilosofiaCortesanaJuego2021juin20|pdf15|146|Fortuna y Ocasión truecan sus atributos de antiguo} (Fortune and Occasion have long exchanged attributes). A seventeenth-century poem quoted by Biagioli shows clients rushing to Rome after a papal election to seize the waving forelock of the one who flees [@biagioliGalileoCourtierPractice1993, 34–35].
variants: |
  Banner: the board has the Italian "cambio et muto il consiglio" [board]. Collar misreads it as "Cambio e moto" [@decaceresTableroItalianoFilosofia2009, 96]. The book gives the words in Spanish, as "a letter in her hand": "Yo trueco, y mudo el co[n]sejo" in C [@barrosFilosofiaCortesanaMoralizada1588, pdf 45], "Yo trueco y mudo el consejo" in M [@debarrosFilosofiaCortesana1587, pdf 8]. Collar cites the book as "Yo trueco y mudo consejo", without the article [@decaceresTableroItalianoFilosofia2009, 104]. On the board the banner is in Italian only, while the square's verse is in both languages (our observation) [board].
  Verse: both engraved first lines carry a colon-like mark, "Tutto :sta" and "Todo :esta" [board]; C prints "Todo esta a dispusicion, / De fortuna, y permission." [@barrosFilosofiaCortesanaMoralizada1588, pdf 46].
  Fortune and God: where M and C say there is no Fortune "but a disposition of the will of God", G says "but the permission of God" [@sanchezEdicionesAntiguasFilosofia2016oct16, 184–185].
  The book's Fortune image: G has neither the woodcut nor its description [@sanchezEdicionesAntiguasFilosofia2016oct16, 188–189]; M adds both [@luceroDEDICATORIAFILOSOFIACORTESANA2019, 38]. C keeps the description but, in the Vienna copy, prints no picture (our observation from the transcription) [@barrosFilosofiaCortesanaMoralizada1588, pdf 60–65]. C also lacks three passages of M: the ball on which she stands, which easily turns top to bottom; a longer account of her two faces (the proud forget her and credit their own worth); and the paragraph on her forelock [@barrosFilosofiaCortesanaMoralizada1588, pdf 61, 63; @debarrosFilosofiaCortesana1587, pdf 10].
  Board against book: the board's Fortune has one face and no palm, yoke, rattle or sword, and adds the wheel with the falling man [board; @decaceresTableroItalianoFilosofia2009, 90, 96]. Rodríguez Mansilla and Zollinger both note that she is not the Fortune of the woodcut [@rodriguezmansillaGRABADOPICARAJUSTINA2019, 191; @GameGooseLargest]. Bidwell-Steiner, citing Lucero, calls Fortune's iconography one of the most significant variants between the editions [@bidwell-steinerFilosofiaCortesanaAlfonso2024, 203].
readings: |
  Collar de Cáceres reads the wheel behind the figure as the threat of her other side: {q|decaceresTableroItalianoFilosofia2009|pdf16|96|Detrás aparece la rueda, con un hombre que cae, expresión de la amenaza de su lado adverso, siempre factible y en esta ocasión ausente.} (behind her the wheel with a falling man expresses the threat of her adverse side, always possible but absent this time). Bidwell-Steiner describes Fortune as turning the wheel with her right hand, which agrees with the engraving, and holding a banner she quotes in the book's Spanish words [@bidwell-steinerFilosofiaCortesanaAlfonso2024, 203]. She reads the yoke of the book's woodcut as turning triumph into defeat, which makes the suitor a Sisyphus [@bidwell-steinerFilosofiaCortesanaAlfonso2024, 205].
  Lucero sees a contradiction at the heart of the work: a book that praises work runs on dice, and {q|luceroIdeaTrabajoFilosofia2019|pdf9|829|Entonces, Fortuna siempre vence.} (then Fortune always wins). Martínez Millán, who never saw a board, put Fortune in a supposed fourth corner of the sheet [@millanFilosofiaCortesanaAlonso1996, 475]; on the Naples board she is square 51 [board].
  On the rule, Wilson could not follow Barros's Spanish and suggested, tentatively, that two throws totalling exactly twelve win, while more send you back to pay [@wilsonCervantesItemEmmanuel1968, 368]. Lucero and Bidwell-Steiner point to a double six, 51 + 12 = 63 [@luceroFilosofiaCortesanaJuego2020, 125; @bidwell-steinerFilosofiaCortesanaAlfonso2024, 203]. The La Bella Donna blog translates the verse as "All things are possible with fortune and permission" [@TranslationFilosofiaCortesana].

=== id: sq55
title_en: "I Thought…"
title_es: El penseque
board_it: Pensar che
kind: marked
rule_short: Go back to 20, Diligence, and pay one stake.
see: |
  An ass lies couched on the ground with its legs folded under it, head up and ears pricked [board]. Above it, in a small cartouche, is the heading "Pensar che" ("to think that…"), and below, the verse in Italian and Spanish; in English: "From "I Thought…" good fortune flies, / and what he has soon fades and dies." [board]. The rule label along the inner edge, in Italian only, reads "Alla diligentia n·° 20 ·Paga" ("To Diligence, no. 20. Pay") [board].
why: |
  "Pensé que", "I thought that…", is the excuse of the man who failed to act. Coming straight after Fortune, the square answers her: Fortune has a hand in which efforts we choose, Barros says, but we must not leave everything to her [@barrosFilosofiaCortesanaMoralizada1588, pdf 47–48]. Complacent half-heartedness is the idler's shield, above all for those who do nothing in their own business and expect others to do it. Because carelessness comes easily to the lucky, the "penseque" is painted at 55 as an ass lying down, since the man who says "who would have thought it?" and fails to see what is coming is like one [@barrosFilosofiaCortesanaMoralizada1588, pdf 48–49].
  The careless man is sent back to 20, Diligence, to learn from the dung-beetle that pushes a ball of dung bigger than its strength towards its hole [@barrosFilosofiaCortesanaMoralizada1588, pdf 49–50]. Lucero calls the contrast between "I Thought…" and Diligence the most characteristic opposition in the work [@lucerosanchezAsnoPenseQue2019, 163].
context: |
  The name comes, Lucero argues, from a satirical tradition of burlesque family trees of Foolishness. "Pensé que" or "Penseque" was already a set phrase for fools, first found in a *Genealogía de la Necedad* of the early sixteenth century [@lucerosanchezAsnoPenseQue2019, 167]. Juan Pérez de Moya's *Filosofía secreta* (1585), the author best represented in Barros's library, tells it in its chapter on the descendants of dullards: {q|lucerosanchezAsnoPenseQue2019|pdf8|168|Dicen que el Tiempo Perdido se casó con la Ignorancia, y hubieron un hijo, que se llamó Pensequé} (they say that Lost Time married Ignorance, and they had a son called I-Thought-That). Pensequé in turn married Youth and fathered "I didn't know", "I didn't think", "I didn't look into it" and "Who would have said?", and the line ends in poverty, the hospital and death [@lucerosanchezAsnoPenseQue2019, 167–168]. Lucero notes that some versions of the family tree include Poverty and "What Will They Say?", two of Barros's own squares [@lucerosanchezAsnoPenseQue2019, 168].
  The ass was joined to the name in proverbs: Juan de Mal Lara (1568) already made Pensé Que the son of Lost Time and Ignorance, and Gonzalo Correas later recorded "Penseque, asneque y burreque, todos son hermanos" ("I-thought-that, ass-that and donkey-that are all brothers") [@lucerosanchezAsnoPenseQue2019, 171]. For Correas the phrase belonged to fools, said to people who excuse their carelessness in important business with "I didn't think" [@lucerosanchezAsnoPenseQue2019, 171].
  Lucero argues that the ass's usual meanings (stupidity, stubbornness, lust, patient labour) do not include laziness, and that Barros inverts the image of the ass as a working animal to set it against the ox of Labour [@lucerosanchezAsnoPenseQue2019, 163, 165, 167]. A lying beast does not always mean sloth: Juan de Borja's ox lying on hay, "Quies bona", stands for rest earned in old age [@lucerosanchezAsnoPenseQue2019, 165]. The word had a long life after Barros, in *La pícara Justina*, Gracián, Quevedo and Tirso's play *El castigo del penseque* [@lucerosanchezAsnoPenseQue2019, 172–173].
variants: |
  Board: the heading reads "Pensar che" (infinitive, "to think that") while the Italian verse begins "Dal pensai che," ("from I-thought-that") [board]. Zollinger, as reproduced on giochidelloca.it, normalizes the verse to "Dal pensar che" and prints "tiene" for the engraved "tien" [board; @GameGooseLargest]. The Italian second line, "fugge et quella che tien poco li dura", says that what he has lasts him little, where the Spanish says it does not last [board]. The Spanish verse is engraved "Del pense que huye uentura / y la que tiene no dura" [board].
  Books: C prints "Del penseque huy[e] ventura: / Y la que tiene no dura.", with letters lost to damage [@barrosFilosofiaCortesanaMoralizada1588, pdf 49]. G has a shorter gloss. M adds the warning not to trust everything to Fortune, the idler's "shield" and the words "and does not foresee it", and C follows M [@sanchezEdicionesAntiguasFilosofia2016oct16, 185–186; @barrosFilosofiaCortesanaMoralizada1588, pdf 48–49]. Smaller C–M differences: "escudo de holgazanes" against "de los holgazanes", "otros las haran" against "lo harán" [@barrosFilosofiaCortesanaMoralizada1588, pdf 48; @debarrosFilosofiaCortesana1587, pdf 8].
  Rule: the board adds "Paga"; the rules at the end of C say only that from 55 one goes back to Diligence, at 20 [@barrosFilosofiaCortesanaMoralizada1588, pdf 68; board].
readings: |
  Collar de Cáceres reads the square as sloth, one of the seven deadly sins: {q|decaceresTableroItalianoFilosofia2009|pdf17|97|La torpe Acidia (casa 55), pecado de los capitales} (clumsy Sloth, square 55, one of the capital sins). He compares Ripa's image of Sloth, which can be a woman lying beside a lying ass, and a drawing attributed to Guido Reni inscribed "Acidia vestita di nero" [@decaceresTableroItalianoFilosofia2009, 97].
  Lucero doubts this. Ripa's first edition is later than Barros's book, and it is unclear whether Ripa's sloth is the woman, the ass or both [@lucerosanchezAsnoPenseQue2019, 166]. Lucero himself infers laziness from the posture: {q|lucerosanchezAsnoPenseQue2019|pdf4|164|Holgazán, suponemos, porque está tumbado.} (Idle, we suppose, because it is lying down). His conclusion is that the tradition stresses foolishness over laziness, and that Barros alone reverses the proportions, making idleness primary while keeping the lack of foresight in view [@lucerosanchezAsnoPenseQue2019, 175]. He also finds it striking that Occasion reclines on the 1588 board, though Barros's text does not require it [@lucerosanchezAsnoPenseQue2019, 172].
  Bidwell-Steiner calls the ass an embodiment of procrastination and, citing Lucero's edition, traces its iconography to Aesop [@bidwell-steinerFilosofiaCortesanaAlfonso2024, 203]; in his article on the square, Lucero says the ass is not lazy in Aesop [@lucerosanchezAsnoPenseQue2019, 165]. The La Bella Donna blog left the verse untranslated and took the animal for a horse [@TranslationFilosofiaCortesana].

=== id: sq60
title_en: Poverty
title_es: Pobreza
board_it: Casa d'la pouerta
kind: marked
rule_short: Go back to 53, the Dice; each other player gives you one stake as alms.
see: |
  Three dead trees, with no leaves and bare, twisted branches, grow from the strip of ground at the square's outer edge towards the centre of the board [board]. Outside the square are the heading "Casa d'la pouerta" ("House of Poverty") and the verse in Italian and Spanish; in English: "Want dries up the sap, and so / favour's root can no more grow." [board]. The rule label along the inner edge, in Italian only, reads "Limosina et alla sorte·53" ("Alms, and to the Dice, 53"), and unlike most penalty squares it has no "Paga" [board].
why: |
  With time, deceit and delay the suitor comes to poverty. Barros shows it as land so dry that the trees have neither leaf nor fruit, to show the passage from the spring of plenty to the winter of need [@barrosFilosofiaCortesanaMoralizada1588, pdf 50–51]. Above all, poverty acts on the patron. As soon as he sees that his suitor has become poor, his words dry up, though words are leaves blown by the wind, and his deeds even more, though deeds are the fruit of what we owe one another. While the suitor still had leaves and fruit to win goodwill it was the other way round; now the watering has stopped and everything has withered [@barrosFilosofiaCortesanaMoralizada1588, pdf 51–52]. Hence the verse about sap and root.
  He is given alms so that he can go and look for another *suerte* of business, a word that means both a way of trying his luck and a throw of the dice. So he goes to the Dice at 53, where there is a throw of eleven, usually a good one, but for a poor man no throw is good [@barrosFilosofiaCortesanaMoralizada1588, pdf 52–53]. The dice engraved at 53 show 4, 4 and 3 [board]. The verse there reads: "In a house where poverty lies, / every throw's a throw of sighs." [@barrosFilosofiaCortesanaMoralizada1588, pdf 53].
context: |
  Emblem books had their own images of poverty. Alciato's emblem 120 shows a man whose winged arm tries to lift him while a stone tied to his other hand holds him down, and Ripa describes Poverty as a woman lying on dry branches; Barros chose the leafless tree instead [@decaceresTableroItalianoFilosofia2009, 96].
  Poverty was a real danger for suitors: Martínez Millán notes that the cost of life at court ruined many of them [@millanFilosofiaCortesanaAlonso1996, 481]. Lucero observes that the man who reaches Poverty will find no powerful person to pity him [@luceroFilosofiaCortesanaJuego2021juin20, 144]. With "What Will They Say?", Poverty produces the idleness and paralysis that are the opposite of Diligence [@luceroFilosofiaCortesanaJuego2021juin20, 146]. Elsewhere he suggests that it may follow from liberality spent without control, or from the vices of idleness [@lucerosanchezAsnoPenseQue2019, 163]. Collar de Cáceres calls the square the reverse of square 7, the Prodigal, with the same moral: {q|decaceresTableroItalianoFilosofia2009|pdf16|96|Viene a ser el reverso de lo mostrado en la casa 7, con indéntica enseñanza moral.} (it is the reverse of square 7, with the same moral lesson; "indéntica" as printed).
  The alms rule has a real-life echo. Collar links it to Barros's later concern with public poor relief [@decaceresTableroItalianoFilosofia2009, 96]. Barros wrote a letter of approval for Cristóbal Pérez de Herrera's *Discursos del amparo de los legítimos pobres* (1598), a proposal for poor relief [@decaceresTableroItalianoFilosofia2009, 82]. The idea that poverty is born of idleness, in one of the labour verses M added ("From idleness springs poverty, / and out of labour, prosperity."), appears again in Barros's praise of *Guzmán de Alfarache* [@sanchezEdicionesAntiguasFilosofia2016oct16, 187].
variants: |
  59 or 60: the first edition (G) twice places Poverty at 59 [@luceroTableroJuego15882019, 204]. M and C put it at 60, which Lucero calls {q|sanchezEdicionesAntiguasFilosofia2016oct16|pdf12|180|la corrección del número de casilla de la Pobreza, donde se prefiere M y es tal y como aparece en la pintura de Cartaro que acompaña a esta edición de 1588} (the correction of Poverty's square number, where M is preferred, just as it appears on Cartaro's board issued with this 1588 edition). Because G repeats the number, Lucero holds that it is not a slip and infers a lost first board with Poverty at 59. He admits that the discrepancy {q|luceroTableroJuego15882019|pdf9|204|propone varias preguntas y deja abiertas todas las respuestas} (raises several questions and leaves all the answers open) [@luceroTableroJuego15882019, 204]. On Cartaro's board square 59 is blank (our observation) [board].
  Alms: C's rules say only "and they give him alms", as G does [@barrosFilosofiaCortesanaMoralizada1588, pdf 68; @luceroTableroJuego15882019, 204]. M specifies one stake from each player, and exempts the player on Poverty from the payment for going back: instead of paying, he receives a stake from everyone [@debarrosFilosofiaCortesana1587, pdf 12; @sanchezEdicionesAntiguasFilosofia2016oct16, 189]. The board's label has no "Paga", which fits that exemption (our observation) [board]. In the prose, C has "Mandanle dar limosna", M "Mándale" [@barrosFilosofiaCortesanaMoralizada1588, pdf 52; @debarrosFilosofiaCortesana1587, pdf 9].
  Verse: the board reads "Pobreza seca el humor / dela raiz del fauor", the Italian "Pouertà secca l'humore / alla radice del fauore" [board]; C prints "Pobreza seca el vmor / De la rayz del fauor." [@barrosFilosofiaCortesanaMoralizada1588, pdf 52]. Collar gives "de la raíz de fauor" and the heading as "Casa del pouertà"; the plate has "del fauor" and "Casa d'la pouerta" [@decaceresTableroItalianoFilosofia2009, 96; board]. Zollinger drops "et" from the rule label [@GameGooseLargest; board].
  The verse of 53, where the poor player lands: G and the board put "Any throw is sad" first, while M and C reverse the lines [@sanchezEdicionesAntiguasFilosofia2016oct16, 187; @rodriguezmansillaGRABADOPICARAJUSTINA2019, 190; @barrosFilosofiaCortesanaMoralizada1588, pdf 53; board].
readings: |
  Bidwell-Steiner reads the rule as ordering the poor player to give alms, and finds the remedy contradictory [@bidwell-steinerFilosofiaCortesanaAlfonso2024, 204]. Wilson and Rodríguez Mansilla read it as the other players giving him alms [@wilsonCervantesItemEmmanuel1968, 368; @rodriguezmansillaGRABADOPICARAJUSTINA2019, 190], and M's rules say so explicitly [@debarrosFilosofiaCortesana1587, pdf 12]. Seville stresses that Poverty at once dries up the support the suitor had been receiving [@sevilleImportanceTextPrinted2026, 73]. Collar reads the square as the reverse of the Prodigal [@decaceresTableroItalianoFilosofia2009, 96]. The La Bella Donna blog translated the rule label as "Beg for luck at n.53" and *favor* as "luck" [@TranslationFilosofiaCortesana].
