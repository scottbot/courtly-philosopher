## A. Scrollytelling introduction (landing page)

### madrid-1587
visual: whole board, slowly zooming to the title "FILOSOFIA CORTESANA / DE ALONSO DE BARROS" along the top margin.

Madrid, 1587. The court of Philip II had been settled in the town since 1561, and the king lived in the Alcázar [@web:https://www.museodelprado.es/en/learn/encyclopedia/voice/alcazar-de-madrid-real/cd2eb3b7-3aa7-45f9-9ce5-0ed711622f08]. Because the king was the source of favour, people came to court to ask for it [@millanFilosofiaCortesanaAlonso1996, 465–66]. These petitioners were the *pretendientes*, people who spent years, and money, pressing for an office, a pension or a *merced* (a grant) that was always scarcer than the people who wanted it [@luceroIdeaTrabajoFilosofia2019, 823, 829]. Geoffrey Parker, followed by Fernando Collar de Cáceres, named the two obsessions of Philip II's courtiers {q|decaceresTableroItalianoFilosofia2009|pdf2|82|aburrimiento y promoción} (boredom and promotion) [@decaceresTableroItalianoFilosofia2009, 82]. Collar de Cáceres says the book and the game answered both [@decaceresTableroItalianoFilosofia2009, 82].

### barros
visual: lower-right margin, the line "CRIADO DEL REY NR̃O S.ᵒʳ" under the title's end, then "Con su priuilegio".

The man who made it signed himself *criado del Rey*, the king's servant [board] [@sanchezEdicionesAntiguasFilosofia2016oct16, 174, 179]. Alonso de Barros came from a Segovian family in royal service; in 1563, on his father's death, he succeeded him as *aposentador*, one of the officials who found lodgings for the court, and held the post for four decades [@luceroDEDICATORIAFILOSOFIACORTESANA2019, 34]. He wanted more. From the early 1580s he sent the king memorials listing his services [@luceroDEDICATORIAFILOSOFIACORTESANA2019, 34]. He was a client of Mateo Vázquez de Leca, Philip II's secretary, to whom he dedicated this book [@sanchezFILOSOFIACORTESANAALONSO, 22]. Ernesto Lucero sums up his method: {q|luceroDEDICATORIAFILOSOFIACORTESANA2019|pdf1|33|Alonso de Barros concibió la escritura como un servicio cortesano.} (Barros treated writing as a service at court.)

### goose-arrives
visual: square 15, the bridge (Passo della speranza), a survival of the Bridge of the Game of the Goose.

The game he borrowed had just arrived. In 1617 Pietro Carrera wrote that the Game of the Goose was invented in Florence and that Grand Duke Francesco de' Medici sent it to Philip II [@sevilleImportanceTextPrinted2026, 69]. A letter of 24 August 1585, written at Monzón by the king's jester Gonzalo de Liaño to the grand duke, curses a servant of the Medici agent Luis Dovara, who brought a devilish game {q|infantesPinturaQueSe2010|pdf11|135|que se juega con dos dados} (played with two dice) and complains that the prince, the infanta and Luis Tristán have won forty escudos from him [@infantesPinturaQueSe2010, 134–35]. The king wrote to a daughter that her sister had become a gambler at the new game [@infantesPinturaQueSe2010, 134 n. 22; @woodChancingItPrint2019, 461].

### moral-map
visual: square 4, the yoked oxen under a festoon of fruit, with the verse panel beneath.

Barros kept the race of the goose game (63 squares on a spiral, an exact throw to finish) and changed its meaning [@luceroFilosofiaCortesanaJuego2020, 125]. The geese became yoked oxen, the square of Labour [@luceroFilosofiaCortesanaJuego2020, 124]. Death became the death of one's patron [@luceroFilosofiaCortesanaJuego2020, 124]. The other squares became the chances of a petition at court: hope, flattery, false friends, a change of ministers, the patron's death, poverty [@luceroFilosofiaCortesanaJuego2021juin20, 135, 149]. For Barros the 63 squares are the years of life that a petition consumes [@luceroFilosofiaCortesanaJuego2021juin20, 135]. Lucero calls it {q|luceroFilosofiaCortesanaJuego2020|pdf1|117|la primera tematización metafórica del juego de la oca de que tenemos constancia} (the first metaphorical, themed version of the goose game on record) [@luceroFilosofiaCortesanaJuego2020, 117].

### sheet-and-book
visual: whole board, with a small book (the 1588 duodecimo) sliding in beside it.

It came in two parts. The royal licence of 9 February 1587 says that Barros had composed {q|luceroTableroJuego15882019|pdf3|198|una pintura intitulada Filosofía cortesana, con ciertas diferencias de figuras y letras, que se contiene en un pliego grande} (a picture called *Filosofía cortesana*, with various figures and verses, contained on a large sheet) and had moralized it in a separate text [@luceroTableroJuego15882019, 198]. The sheet was the board; the little book gave the rules and explained each picture [@luceroTableroJuego15882019, 197–98]. Barros's second edition says the sheet travelled apart from the book, to be spread on a table or pasted onto one [@debarrosFilosofiaCortesana1587, pdf 11]. E. M. Wilson put it plainly: {q|wilsonCervantesItemEmmanuel1968|pdf8|369|You improve yourself morally while you gamble.}

### reading-a-square
visual: square 26, Il priuato (the Favourite): two cloaked men, one offering a dish; the "Paga." label in the gutter; the two couplets.

Each square with a picture works like an emblem, the picture-and-verse form popular in the period. Lucero maps the parts: the square's name is the motto, the picture is the image, and the verses under it, together with Barros's prose in the book, are the explanation [@luceroFilosofiaCortesanaJuego2020, 117]. Take square 26. The heading reads *Il priuato*, the Favourite [board]. A man offers something to another at a doorway [board]; Barros says it is the first fruits of the petitioner's estate, offered in return for a hearing [@decaceresTableroItalianoFilosofia2009, 94]. The Spanish verse: "Don't seek another's hand, my friend, / unless your own comes full to lend" [board]. A small label above says *Paga*: pay [board] [@decaceresTableroItalianoFilosofia2009, 94].

### three-editions
visual: text: title page of the 1588 book (FILOSOFIA CORTESANA MORALIZADA … En Napoles por Iosep Cacchij), then the 1587 title page reproduced by Wilson.

The book went through three editions in little more than a year [@sanchezEdicionesAntiguasFilosofia2016oct16, 177]. The first (G) came from the widow of Alonso Gómez in Madrid, with a colophon of 17 February 1587 [@sanchezEdicionesAntiguasFilosofia2016oct16, 179, 189, 191]. Pedro Madrigal reprinted it the same year (M), adding sonnets by Cervantes and Pedro Liñán de Riaza, Vázquez's coat of arms, a woodcut of Fortune and a worked example of play [@luceroDEDICATORIAFILOSOFIACORTESANA2019, 38–40]. Joseph Cacchij printed the third (C) in Naples in 1588 [@luceroDEDICATORIAFILOSOFIACORTESANA2019, 47]. One copy of each is known: Lisbon, Cambridge, Vienna [@sanchezEdicionesAntiguasFilosofia2016oct16, 176–77, 180]. The Madrid boards are lost; Lucero argues that the first had Poverty on square 59, not 60 [@luceroTableroJuego15882019, 204].

### naples-cartaro
visual: bottom-right signature "Marius·Cartarius·Jnc·Neap·1588·", then the Naples privilege line "Con priuilegio di Sua Ecc.ª per X. / anni nel Regno di Napoli".

The board that survives was engraved in Naples in 1588 by Mario Cartaro, a printmaker from Viterbo who had worked in Rome and moved to Naples to make maps of the kingdom [board] [@woodChancingItPrint2019, 462; @sevilleImportanceTextPrinted2026, 70]. Its verses are in Italian and Spanish, and it carries its own ten-year privilege for the Kingdom of Naples [@decaceresTableroItalianoFilosofia2009, 86; @luceroTableroJuego15882019, 203 n. 14]. It is a single sheet of etching and engraving, 531 × 404 mm [@woodChancingItPrint2019, 463]. The only impression recorded in the sources we cite is in the British Museum (1869,0410.2463), bought in 1869 from Edward Daniell [@sevilleImportanceTextPrinted2026, 71; @web:https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/P_1869-0410-2463-]. Scholars identified it in 2009 and 2010 [@infantesPinturaQueSe2010, 130, 133].

### the-pot
visual: the centre: the palm under its gate at square 63, then the man at the palm holding a fish, one shoe left behind.

Money was part of the game. In M each player puts one stake, of an agreed value, into the pot; players pay again at certain squares, pay each time they are sent back, and the one who lands exactly on 63 takes everything [@debarrosFilosofiaCortesana1587, pdf 11–12]. Lucero reads the pot as the court's favours, which {q|luceroFilosofiaCortesanaJuego2021juin20|pdf10|141|son limitados y no alcanzan para todos} (are limited and not enough for everyone) [@luceroFilosofiaCortesanaJuego2021juin20, 141]. The winner's square shows a palm and a man who has caught a fish and lost a shoe: nothing is won without cost [@decaceresTableroItalianoFilosofia2009, 97; board].

### cervantes
visual: the centre sea (Mare di soffrimento / Mar de suffrimiento): ships, the angler with his rod, the cartouche.

Cervantes wrote a sonnet for the second edition, while he was trying to win Mateo Vázquez's favour [@sanchezEdicionesAntiguasFilosofia2016oct16, 180–81]. It praises a book that joins pleasure and profit in a cheerful game, and it pictures the reader as a sailor [@wilsonCervantesItemEmmanuel1968, 366]: {q|bidwell-steinerFilosofiaCortesanaAlfonso2024|pdf3|197|El que navega por el golfo insano/ del mar de pretensiones vera al punto/ del cortesano laberintio el hilo} (whoever sails the mad gulf of the sea of petitions will at once see the thread through the courtly labyrinth) [@bidwell-steinerFilosofiaCortesanaAlfonso2024, 197]. At the centre of the board lies the Sea of Suffering, with its verse: "Who seeks advancement must endure, / as birth makes dying just as sure" [board] [@decaceresTableroItalianoFilosofia2009, 99].

### enter
visual: bottom-left corner: the entrance arch with "Guarda / l'fine", the swan on the skull blowing its trumpet ("Noscete ipsum"), and square 1, the peacock with its tail spread.

The way in is a gate [board]. Above it a swan stands on a skull and blows a trumpet that says "Know thyself"; between the pillars is written "Look to the end" [board] [@decaceresTableroItalianoFilosofia2009, 93]. Barros explains that the beginner enters by the Gate of Opinion, deceived by their own good opinion of themselves, and struts like the peacock on the first square until time tells them to look at their feet [@decaceresTableroItalianoFilosofia2009, 93]. Its verse: "Reason looks down to the feet; / Opinion, to the wheel's conceit" [board]. Now enter through the Gate of Opinion.

## B. About pages

### about-barros

#### Alonso de Barros (c. 1540/52 – 1604)

Alonso de Barros came from a Segovian family tied to royal service [@luceroDEDICATORIAFILOSOFIACORTESANA2019, 34]. His father was Diego López de Orozco, an attendant and *aposentador* of Charles V, and his mother Elvira de Barros; Alonso took his mother's surname [@decaceresTableroItalianoFilosofia2009, 82; @luceroIdeaTrabajoFilosofia2019, 828]. He was unmarried and left his estate to a nephew [@decaceresTableroItalianoFilosofia2009, 82, 100 n. 11].

#### Office

In 1563, when his father died, Barros succeeded him as *aposentador*, one of the officials who arranged lodging for the court, a post much needed once the court had settled in Madrid [@luceroDEDICATORIAFILOSOFIACORTESANA2019, 34]. Lucero counts more than four decades in the post, from 1563 to 1604 [@sanchezFILOSOFIACORTESANAALONSO, 22]; Wilson, following Pérez Pastor, dates the appointment to 1569 [@wilsonCervantesItemEmmanuel1968, 369]. Before settling in Madrid he served at the taking of the Peñón de Vélez de la Gomera (1564), in Corsica and at the relief of Malta (1565) [@decaceresTableroItalianoFilosofia2009, 82; @wilsonCervantesItemEmmanuel1968, 369].

He asked for more and was often refused. He was proposed in 1582 for a post of *contador* in Guatemala and not appointed; in 1583 the king declined to name him assayer of the Segovia mint, a post left vacant by the death of his brother-in-law [@decaceresTableroItalianoFilosofia2009, 99–100 nn. 10–11; @millanFilosofiaCortesanaAlonso1996, 463 n. 9]. His memorials, as Lucero reads them, cite his years of service and his soldiering, not his books [@luceroDEDICATORIAFILOSOFIACORTESANA2019, 34]. In 1587, weeks after the licence to print this book, he petitioned for the *escribanía mayor de rentas* of the district of Santo Domingo de Silos, and received it in September [@luceroDEDICATORIAFILOSOFIACORTESANA2019, 35–36]. Lucero sees Mateo Vázquez behind the grant but does not claim that the book alone won it [@luceroDEDICATORIAFILOSOFIACORTESANA2019, 35–36]. Martínez Millán dated the same grant to October 1590 [@millanFilosofiaCortesanaAlonso1996, 463].

#### Patrons

Barros dedicated the *Filosofía cortesana* to Mateo Vázquez de Leca, Philip II's secretary and the leader of the court faction historians call *castellanista* [@luceroDEDICATORIAFILOSOFIACORTESANA2019, 37, 42; @millanFilosofiaCortesanaAlonso1996, 463–64]. From the second edition on, Vázquez's coat of arms stood on the title page [@luceroDEDICATORIAFILOSOFIACORTESANA2019, 38–40]. Vázquez died in 1591 [@luceroDedicatoriaProverbiosMorales2019, 109 n. 21]. In 1598 Barros dedicated his second book to García de Loaysa Girón, tutor of the future Philip III, who became archbishop of Toledo that summer, lost his court offices within days of the new reign and died in February 1599 [@luceroDedicatoriaProverbiosMorales2019, 104, 111–12]. When Barros asked leave to pass his post of *aposentador* to Diego López de Burgos, a man not of his family, the king refused [@luceroDedicatoriaProverbiosMorales2019, 119]. Lucero reads the episode through Barros's own board: the Death of the Patron sends the player back to the start [@luceroDedicatoriaProverbiosMorales2019, 120].

#### Writings

Barros published two books of his own: the *Filosofía cortesana* (1587) and the *Proverbios morales* (Madrid: Luis Sánchez, 1598) [@luceroDEDICATORIAFILOSOFIACORTESANA2019, 34]. The *Proverbios* hold 1,063 proverbs in octosyllabic verse [@luceroDedicatoriaProverbiosMorales2019, 102] and were reprinted many times, also as *Perla de proverbios morales* and *Heráclito de Alonso de Barros*, and in French [@decaceresTableroItalianoFilosofia2009, 82, 100 n. 12; @wilsonCervantesItemEmmanuel1968, 363]. For two centuries the two books were confused, until Pérez Pastor and later Wilson separated them [@sanchezEdicionesAntiguasFilosofia2016oct16, 169–72; @millanFilosofiaCortesanaAlonso1996, 465 n. 17]. Barros also wrote a printed *Memorial sobre el reparo de la milicia* on veterans' relief, and a short manuscript *Discurso y difinición del privado*, which treats the favour of a lesser person with a greater as a form of friendship [@decaceresTableroItalianoFilosofia2009, 82, 84; @luceroDEDICATORIAFILOSOFIACORTESANA2019, 40, 42]. He wrote paratexts for friends: the *Elogio* of the author in Mateo Alemán's *Guzmán de Alfarache* (1599), a letter in Cristóbal Pérez de Herrera's *Amparo de pobres* (1598) and praise for Hernando de Soto's *Emblemas moralizadas* (1599) [@sanchezFILOSOFIACORTESANAALONSO, 22; @decaceresTableroItalianoFilosofia2009, 82].

#### Friends

Alemán called Barros his "otro yo" (other self) in his prologue to the *Proverbios* [@luceroDedicatoriaProverbiosMorales2019, 103]. With Alemán and above all Pérez de Herrera, Barros took part in the reform of poor relief in the Madrid parish of San Martín [@luceroDedicatoriaProverbiosMorales2019, 109]. Soto, a *contador* and emblem writer, exchanged praise with him; Lope de Vega contributed a poem to the *Proverbios* [@luceroDedicatoriaProverbiosMorales2019, 103, 116]. Collar de Cáceres calls him a court bureaucrat with literary interests, close to Soto and Alemán [@decaceresTableroItalianoFilosofia2009, 82]. At his death his library held 151 volumes, studied by Trevor Dadson [@decaceresTableroItalianoFilosofia2009, 83].

#### What the sources do not settle

- **Birth.** Nicolás Antonio gives Segovia, c. 1552 [@wilsonCervantesItemEmmanuel1968, 363]. Collar de Cáceres argues for c. 1542, since Barros was already appointed in 1563 and fighting in 1564–65 [@decaceresTableroItalianoFilosofia2009, 82, 99 n. 7]. Dadson proposed 1540 or 1545 [@sanchezEdicionesAntiguasFilosofia2016oct16, 175 n. 23]; Díaz Gito writes c. 1541–45 [@gitoTodaVidaEs2020, 184].
- **Death.** Collar de Cáceres gives the night of 17 August 1604, in Madrid, with the will opened on 18 August [@decaceresTableroItalianoFilosofia2009, 82, 100 n. 11]; Wilson, after Pérez Pastor, gives 18 August 1604 [@wilsonCervantesItemEmmanuel1968, 369].
- **The Silos office:** 1587 (Lucero, Collar de Cáceres) or 1590 (Martínez Millán), as above.
- **Authorship.** Martínez Millán and José Luis Gonzalo Sánchez-Molero suggested the work came out of Vázquez's circle; Lucero holds Barros's authorship beyond doubt while allowing that friends may have read the text [@luceroDEDICATORIAFILOSOFIACORTESANA2019, 41].

### about-court

#### A court that stayed put

Philip II settled his court in Madrid in 1561 and made the Alcázar his principal residence [@web:https://www.museodelprado.es/en/learn/encyclopedia/voice/alcazar-de-madrid-real/cd2eb3b7-3aa7-45f9-9ce5-0ed711622f08]. A fixed court drew people who wanted something from the king, because the king was the source of *gracia* (grace, favour) [@millanFilosofiaCortesanaAlonso1996, 465–66]. Collar de Cáceres, after Geoffrey Parker, names the courtiers' two obsessions as boredom and promotion [@decaceresTableroItalianoFilosofia2009, 82].

#### Pretendientes and mercedes

A *pretendiente* (petitioner, office-seeker) was, in the 1737 dictionary of the Spanish Academy, one who seeks or solicits; the laws it cites required court petitioners to register their presence [@web:https://webfrl.rae.es/DA_DATOS/TOMO_V_HTML/PRETENDIENTE_006558.html]. The Latin equivalents it gives, *Petitor. Candidatus. Procus.*, span the word's range: a claimant, a candidate for office, and a wooer, so the same word could name someone courting a lady and someone courting the king [@web:https://webfrl.rae.es/DA_DATOS/TOMO_V_HTML/PRETENDIENTE_006558.html]. English "suitor" carries the same double sense, but most readers now hear only the second; this edition says "petitioner", which keeps the asking and loses the courtship. What they sought was a *merced*: an office, a pension or another grant [@luceroIdeaTrabajoFilosofia2019, 823]. Lucero stresses that a *merced* was a gift of grace, not a wage owed for service, and that it was always scarce [@luceroIdeaTrabajoFilosofia2019, 823, 829]. By 1593 the deceived petitioner was a stock figure of satire, as in Góngora's letrilla "Un buhonero ha empleado" [@cansecoLUCEROErnestoEd2021, 430].

Petitions reached the king in writing. Petitioners sent *memoriales*, the councils answered with *consultas*, and royal secretaries filtered what the king saw [@web:https://cedis.novalaw.unl.pt/wp-content/uploads/2023/01/14509_LA-DISTRIBUCION-DE-LA-GRACIA-DURANTE-LA-ANEXION-DE-PORTUGAL-RODRIGO-VAZQUEZ-DE-ARCE-1578-1583.pdf]. Barros's own file of petitions shows the procedure at work: in 1587 the king asked why the Silos post had been vacant so long and whether anyone else wanted it before granting it [@luceroDEDICATORIAFILOSOFIACORTESANA2019, 35–36].

#### Secretaries and favourites

Mateo Vázquez de Leca, the dedicatee, was secretary to Philip II from 1573 until his death in 1591 [@wilsonCervantesItemEmmanuel1968, 365, 367; @luceroDedicatoriaProverbiosMorales2019, 109 n. 21]. Lucero presents him as the model of the *letrado* who rose by effort, and as the patron of the *castellanista* party [@luceroDEDICATORIAFILOSOFIACORTESANA2019, 37, 42]. From the early 1580s he sought social standing to match his power, with a claimed descent from the Lecca of Corsica and the Colonna that rivals mocked; his power waned from about 1585, when the Junta de Noche was created [@luceroDEDICATORIAFILOSOFIACORTESANA2019, 37].

The *privado* (royal favourite) had no fixed office, only the king's ear. Bidwell-Steiner places Barros's court at the beginnings of the system of *validos* [@bidwell-steinerFilosofiaCortesanaAlfonso2024, 209–10]. On the board the Favourite is square 26, where the petitioner pays to be heard [board] [@decaceresTableroItalianoFilosofia2009, 94]. Barros's manuscript on the *privado* makes the favour of an inferior with a superior almost the same thing as friendship between equals; Collar de Cáceres finds this cynical [@luceroDEDICATORIAFILOSOFIACORTESANA2019, 42; @decaceresTableroItalianoFilosofia2009, 84].

#### Factions

Martínez Millán describes two groupings at Philip II's court. The *castellanistas*, formed after Cardinal Espinosa's death in 1572 under Vázquez's patronage, favoured an intransigent religious policy and Castilians in the central offices; they opposed the *papistas* around don Juan de Austria, Antonio Pérez and the princess of Éboli [@millanFilosofiaCortesanaAlonso1996, 463–64]. On his account the *castellanistas* peaked from 1578, gained with the union with Portugal in 1580 and declined in 1586–90 [@millanFilosofiaCortesanaAlonso1996, 464]. Martínez Millán assigned Barros to them from his two dedicatees [@millanFilosofiaCortesanaAlonso1996, 463]. Lucero accepts this and reads the board's praise of labour as the faction's ideology [@luceroFilosofiaCortesanaJuego2020, 118], though he notes that Cervantes and Liñán in the second edition's preliminaries show the factions were not sealed [@luceroDEDICATORIAFILOSOFIACORTESANA2019, 39]. Bidwell-Steiner prefers a broader explanation, a change in codes of court sociability, to a factional one [@bidwell-steinerFilosofiaCortesanaAlfonso2024, 195–96].

Barros's squares map onto this world. The Change of Ministers (43) sends the petitioner back to Flattery (10) to court the new minister [@luceroFilosofiaCortesanaJuego2020, 125]. The Death of the Patron (46) sends the petitioner back to the start [@luceroFilosofiaCortesanaJuego2021juin20, 145]. Lucero reads both as a warning against relying on a single patron [@luceroFilosofiaCortesanaJuego2021juin20, 145].

#### A comparative frame: Biagioli's patronage economy

Mario Biagioli's study of Galileo at the Medici and papal courts (1993) gives a vocabulary for the same system, though his evidence is Florentine and Roman and a generation later than Barros. For Biagioli {q|biagioliGalileoCourtierPractice1993|pdf19|4|patronage was an institution without walls} [@biagioliGalileoCourtierPractice1993, 4]; opting out of it was social suicide [@biagioliGalileoCourtierPractice1993, 16]. Careers ran in jumps, broken by crises that usually followed a patron's death [@biagioliGalileoCourtierPractice1993, 30, 34]. A prince's reward was not a debt: {q|biagioliGalileoCourtierPractice1993|pdf66|51|for a courtier to receive a reward from his prince was structurally analogous to a Christian's receiving grace from God} [@biagioliGalileoCourtierPractice1993, 51]. He quotes Matteo Pellegrini (1624), for whom it served the prince to keep everyone suspended between fear and hope [@biagioliGalileoCourtierPractice1993, 20]. Biagioli cites Barros's game itself, at second hand from Parker, as a court game in which a patron's death sent the player back to the start; he places that square at 43, where the 1588 board and the rules put it at 46 [@biagioliGalileoCourtierPractice1993, 35; board] [@luceroDedicatoriaProverbiosMorales2019, 120].

The parallel is ours to draw, not Barros's: Lucero's reading of the dice as the patron's will [@sanchezFILOSOFIACORTESANAALONSO, 26, 45] and Biagioli's reading of reward as grace describe the same structure, in which effort is necessary but not enough.

### about-goose

#### Origins

The origins of the Game of the Goose are obscure; the earliest references Seville cites are Italian, from the 1460s: a ban at Argenta in 1463 and a payment of 1465 to Borso d'Este's jester for playing it [@sevilleImportanceTextPrinted2026, 64]. Seville reads its numerology (63, the Grand Climacteric; series of nines) as a sign that it first represented the spiritual progress of a life [@sevilleImportanceTextPrinted2026, 64]. The earliest surviving boards are inlaid wooden gifts for princes [@sevilleImportanceTextPrinted2026, 64]; Lucero names a reversible wooden chess-and-goose board in the Metropolitan Museum of Art [@luceroFilosofiaCortesanaJuego2020, 120].

#### From Florence to Madrid

Pietro Carrera, *Il gioco de gli scacchi* (Militello, 1617), wrote that the game was invented in Florence and that Grand Duke Francesco de' Medici sent it to Philip II [@sevilleImportanceTextPrinted2026, 69]. His words, as the page appears in a photograph posted to the Tarot History Forum in 2013 (our transcription; long s given as s) [@TarotHistoryForum; @ref:carrera1617, 25]:

> *E chiaro che gli huomini spiritosi dopo la prima inuentione della cosa sù l'istesso fondamento aggiungendo, ò mutando, ritrouano altre inuentioni, come è noto essere auuenuto al ritrouamento del gioco dell'Oca ne' tempi de' nostri padri, perchè questo gioco essendosi ritrouato in Firenze, e piacendo sommamente parue à Francesco di Medici grã Duca di Toscana di mandarlo alla Maestà del Rè Filippo II. in Ispagna, oue publicato diè materia à' buoni ingegni di ritrouarne altri poco differenti dal primo, fra' quali vi è il gioco detto la Filosofia Corteggiana ritrouato da Alonso di Barros spagnuolo.*

(It is clear that clever men, after the first invention of a thing, by adding or changing on the same foundation, find other inventions, as is known to have happened with the finding of the Game of the Goose in our fathers' time: for this game having been devised in Florence, and pleasing greatly, Francesco de' Medici, Grand Duke of Tuscany, thought fit to send it to His Majesty King Philip II in Spain, where, once published, it gave good wits the matter to devise others little different from the first, among which is the game called the *Filosofia Corteggiana*, devised by Alonso de Barros, a Spaniard.) Carrera gives no firm date: Collar de Cáceres reports him as c. 1576, Infantes as c. 1567 [@decaceresTableroItalianoFilosofia2009, 85; @infantesPinturaQueSe2010, 134]. Domini observed that the gift was uncorroborated [@decaceresTableroItalianoFilosofia2009, 101 n. 36].

Two letters of 1585 support the first part of the story [@sevilleImportanceTextPrinted2026, 69]. On 24 August 1585, at Monzón, Gonzalo de Liaño ("Gonzalillo"), the king's jester, wrote to Francesco I cursing a servant of Luis Dovara, the Medici agent at the Spanish court, for bringing a game of the goose played with two dice; the prince, the infanta and Luis Tristán had won forty escudos from him [@infantesPinturaQueSe2010, 134–35]. The letter was found by Nieves Baranda [@infantesPinturaQueSe2010, 134]. Wood's text says the royal children lost the money, but the Spanish she quotes says Liaño lost it [@woodChancingItPrint2019, 470, 481]. Philip II wrote to a daughter that her sister had become a gambler at a new game brought by Gonzalillo [@infantesPinturaQueSe2010, 134 n. 22]. Bidwell-Steiner calls Liaño a *portero de cámara* [@bidwell-steinerFilosofiaCortesanaAlfonso2024, 196].

#### The standard game

Lucero reconstructs the rules then current [@luceroFilosofiaCortesanaJuego2020, 122–24]. Players race along a spiral of 63 squares by the throw of two dice [@luceroFilosofiaCortesanaJuego2020, 122; @woodChancingItPrint2019, 458]. A goose doubles the throw; the geese run in two series of nines, from 5 and from 9 [@luceroFilosofiaCortesanaJuego2020, 122]. A first throw of 9 goes at once to one of the two dice squares, 26 or 53, so that nobody can run the geese to victory on the first throw [@luceroFilosofiaCortesanaJuego2020, 122–23]. There are hazards: the Bridge (6), Inn (19), Well (31), Labyrinth (42), Prison (52) and Death (58), which sends the player back to the start [@decaceresTableroItalianoFilosofia2009, 102 n. 68; @woodChancingItPrint2019, 458]. Many hazards demand a payment to the pot [@sevilleImportanceTextPrinted2026, 66]. A player who lands on an occupied square displaces its occupant [@luceroFilosofiaCortesanaJuego2020, 122]. The last square must be reached exactly; an overthrow counts back [@woodChancingItPrint2019, 458].

#### What Barros kept and what he changed

Lucero lists what Barros kept: the 63 squares on a spiral, the rebound, the ban on two players sharing a square, mechanical squares, and payments to the pot or to other players [@luceroFilosofiaCortesanaJuego2020, 125].

What changed [@luceroFilosofiaCortesanaJuego2020, 124–25]:
- the geese became oxen, the squares of Labour, at 4, 12, 17, 23, 30, 34, 41, 48 and 57, in no numerical pattern; Barros says their placing keeps no order, because the matter has none;
- Death became the Death of the Patron and moved to 46;
- the Dice stand at 28 and 53, not 26 and 53, without the first-throw rule;
- Fortune (51) gives a second throw, so that a double six from 51 reaches 63.

Barros also dropped the Prison and the Labyrinth [@luceroFilosofiaCortesanaJuego2021juin20, 144 n. 15]. His Well of Oblivion costs a lost turn and payments, where the goose-game Well holds a player until another falls in [@decaceresTableroItalianoFilosofia2009, 95]. He did not use the goose rule of jumping from one dice square to the other [@decaceresTableroItalianoFilosofia2009, 95]. Lucero thinks Barros knew the goose game only partly, and emptied its numbering of symbolic sense {q|luceroFilosofiaCortesanaJuego2020|pdf10|126|por puro desconocimiento, a buen seguro} (out of plain ignorance, surely) [@luceroFilosofiaCortesanaJuego2020, 121, 126]; in the sequel he hedges: {q|luceroFilosofiaCortesanaJuego2021juin20|pdf4|135|probablemente por desconocimiento o quizá por interés} (probably out of ignorance, or perhaps by design) [@luceroFilosofiaCortesanaJuego2021juin20, 135].

#### Later themed geese

Carrera said that the game's publication in Spain led others to invent variants {q|sevilleImportanceTextPrinted2026|pdf8|69|a little different from the original, among which was the game known under the name of the Filosofia cortesana invented by Alonso de Barros of Spain} [@sevilleImportanceTextPrinted2026, 69]. Seville places Barros's game first among early themed variants; his next example is the *Jeu Royal de Cupidon* (Paris, c. 1640) [@sevilleImportanceTextPrinted2026, 68, 73]. In Rome, Altiero Gatti's *Gioco della scimia* (1588) replaced the goose with a monkey; Wood reads it as a parody of Barros's board [@woodChancingItPrint2019, 475]. Wood gives Lucchino Gargano's sheet (Rome, 1598) as the earliest surviving one titled a Game of the Goose [@woodChancingItPrint2019, 454]. Thematic geese later multiplied; Collar de Cáceres cites d'Allemagne's ten groups and boards of up to 300 squares [@decaceresTableroItalianoFilosofia2009, 86, 102 n. 55].

### about-editions

#### Three editions in a year

Three early editions are known, each in a single copy [@sanchezEdicionesAntiguasFilosofia2016oct16, 177]. Lucero Sánchez showed in 2016 that the Madrid edition of the widow of Alonso Gómez is the first and that Madrigal's is an expanded second edition in which Barros took part or consented [@sanchezEdicionesAntiguasFilosofia2016oct16, 169, 179, 191]. Among his reasons: G's first gathering, signed with pilcrows, was printed after the text, as the law of 1558 required of preliminaries, and its title page leaves the price blank because the official price (*tasa*) had not arrived in time; M prints the price on its title page [@sanchezEdicionesAntiguasFilosofia2016oct16, 179, 181]. C follows M throughout [@sanchezEdicionesAntiguasFilosofia2016oct16, 180 n. 42].

#### Ghosts

Tamayo de Vargas and Nicolás Antonio recorded an edition "por Alonso Gómez" of 1587; because Gómez had died in 1584, later bibliographers doubted it, and Ticknor's translators printed the date as 1567, a misprint that lived on in the bibliographies [@sanchezEdicionesAntiguasFilosofia2016oct16, 174–76]. Wilson in 1968 treated the Gómez imprint as a mistake [@wilsonCervantesItemEmmanuel1968, 364]. The Lisbon copy shows that the widow's shop printed it [@sanchezEdicionesAntiguasFilosofia2016oct16, 174–77]. An Indies licence of 17 November 1587 allowed Barros to print and sell "la dicha pintura y moralidad della" in the Americas; whether an Indies edition followed is not known from our sources [@decaceresTableroItalianoFilosofia2009, 84, 88].

#### Three boards, two lost

The licence covers a picture on a large sheet with a separate moralization [@luceroTableroJuego15882019, 198]. M says the picture travels apart from the book [@debarrosFilosofiaCortesana1587, pdf 11]. Neither Madrid copy keeps a board. Dadson took the lack of copies, and of boards, as a sign of heavy use [@sanchezEdicionesAntiguasFilosofia2016oct16, 177]; Lucero thinks loose sheets not bound with the text were simply lost [@luceroTableroJuego15882019, 198].

Lucero argues that each Madrid edition had its own board [@luceroTableroJuego15882019, 203]. G twice places Poverty at 59, not 60, and has different labour verses, so its board must have differed from Cartaro's [@luceroTableroJuego15882019, 203–04]; M's changed description of the central sea (it adds a patient angler) suggests a new plate, close to the Naples one but not identical [@luceroTableroJuego15882019, 205–06]. In his thesis the claim is more cautious: at least one board with small differences for G, and perhaps one very like Cartaro's for M [@sanchezFILOSOFIACORTESANAALONSO, 43–44]. Infantes, before G was found, thought the Naples board might copy a lost Madrid one [@infantesPinturaQueSe2010, 135].

#### This edition

Our text is a new diplomatic transcription of C, set beside a new transcription of Cartaro's board, with M's and G's readings reported where they differ; the switch between G, M and C changes the rules the game applies. See the editorial note (section D).

### about-board

#### Maker and making

The sheet is signed at lower right "Marius·Cartarius·Jnc·Neap·1588·": Mario Cartaro engraved it (*incidit*) in Naples in 1588 [board] [@decaceresTableroItalianoFilosofia2009, 86]. The British Museum catalogue, quoted by Seville, calls Cartaro an engraver, print dealer and publisher from Viterbo, born 1540/45, active in Rome from 1557 and in Naples by 1588 [@sevilleImportanceTextPrinted2026, 70 n. 16]. Wood says he moved to Naples in 1586 to complete maps of the kingdom [@woodChancingItPrint2019, 462]; the Treccani biography records that the Regia Camera of Naples employed him from 1586 to draw maps, and that he died in Naples on 16 April 1620 [@web:https://www.treccani.it/enciclopedia/mario-cartaro_(Dizionario-Biografico)/]. Zollinger describes him as the viceroy's cartographer [@GameGooseLargest]. Collar de Cáceres lists his Roman work: Vignola's façade for the Gesù, maps, celestial globes and religious prints [@decaceresTableroItalianoFilosofia2009, 102 n. 65]. Bartsch's catalogue of his prints does not include the board [@decaceresTableroItalianoFilosofia2009, 102 n. 64].

The technique is etching and engraving on a single sheet of paper, 531 × 404 mm [@woodChancingItPrint2019, 463; @web:https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/P_1869-0410-2463-]. Wood gives the usual size of printed boards as roughly 35–40 by 46–53 cm, printable on a royal folio and trimmed; this sheet is at the top of that range [@woodChancingItPrint2019, 479 n. 20]. Lucero notes that Italian engravers were prized for their etching, while Spanish emblem writers complained of Castilian block-cutters [@luceroDEDICATORIAFILOSOFIACORTESANA2019, 48].

#### Two privileges and three languages

The title runs along the top and ends at lower right: "Filosofia cortesana de Alonso de Barros, criado del Rey n[uest]ro S[eñ]or", with "Con su priuilegio" beneath [board]. A second line grants a privilege of His Excellency, the viceroy, for ten years in the Kingdom of Naples [board] [@GameGooseLargest]. The verses of the squares are in Italian above and Spanish below [@decaceresTableroItalianoFilosofia2009, 86, 91]. The rule labels above the hazards are in Italian, and only the Well's is in both languages [@GameGooseLargest; board]. Lucero lists what is in one language only: the corner mottoes in Spanish, the swan's Latin "Nosce te ipsum", the Italian "Guarda l'fine" at the gate, the title in Spanish [@luceroTableroJuego15882019, 203 n. 14]. He takes the bilingual text as aimed at the Spanish colony in Naples and at local courtiers, and as evidence that Cartaro copied an earlier print [@luceroTableroJuego15882019, 203 n. 14].

#### Layout

A gate at bottom left leads to square 1, the peacock; the numbers run counter-clockwise along the bottom, up the right side, across the top and down the left, then turn inward in a second ring to the palm at 63 [board] [@woodChancingItPrint2019, 462–63]. Balusters divide the bays, as on other Italian goose boards; Collar de Cáceres sees in the spiral of balusters the spokes of Fortune's wheel [@decaceresTableroItalianoFilosofia2009, 88, 90]. Each bay has a picture above and, where the square has one, a heading and two couplets below; lettering reads from outside the track, so the top row is upside down for a player seated at the foot of the sheet (our observation) [board]. The nine labour squares show yoked oxen under hanging fruit [@decaceresTableroItalianoFilosofia2009, 91]. Most other squares carry only a number and a plant (our observation) [board].

The four corners hold a dolphin wound round an anchor, "Date prisa a espacio" (make haste slowly), top left; a half-reclining woman with a single forelock, Occasion, "No me pierdas" (don't lose me), top right; a hand pointing to a clock, "Hasta la postrera" (until the last), bottom right; and the entrance gate with the swan on a skull, bottom left [board] [@decaceresTableroItalianoFilosofia2009, 92–93]. The engraved forms are "Dateprisa aespacio" and "Haesta la postrera"; Lucero and Collar de Cáceres print "Date prisa despacio", and Collar reads "Ha esta la postrera" [board] [@luceroTableroJuego15882019, 203 n. 14; @decaceresTableroItalianoFilosofia2009, 92–93]. Barros's text explains only three corner figures [@barrosFilosofiaCortesanaMoralizada1588, pdf 57]. The centre is the Sea of Suffering: ships, a galley, an angler, a net-fisher, fish and sea monsters [board] [@decaceresTableroItalianoFilosofia2009, 99]. Wood compares its sea monsters to those of maps, fitting Cartaro's trade [@woodChancingItPrint2019, 466]. Rodríguez Mansilla notes that the Italian goose boards he compares share the spiral but have no sea at the centre [@rodriguezmansillaGRABADOPICARAJUSTINA2019, 190].

#### Board and book

The board does not carry the rules in full: Lucero points out that Barros put no summary of rules on the sheet, so the book was needed [@luceroTableroJuego15882019, 202]. What the board has are short labels ("Paga", "Alla sorte n.º 28", "Giuoca 2 uolte") [board]. The two do not always agree. The Fortune on square 51 differs from the book's woodcut, and her banner reads "cambio et muto il consiglio" where the book's verse is "Yo trueco y mudo el consejo" [@rodriguezmansillaGRABADOPICARAJUSTINA2019, 191; @decaceresTableroItalianoFilosofia2009, 104 n. 113; board]. The Spanish lines of squares 10 and 53 stand in the reverse order to the book's [@decaceresTableroItalianoFilosofia2009, 104 n. 100; @rodriguezmansillaGRABADOPICARAJUSTINA2019, 190]. C's text never numbers the palm square; only the board gives it 63 [@barrosFilosofiaCortesanaMoralizada1588, pdf 53, pdf 68–69; board].

#### Provenance and rediscovery

The British Museum acquired the sheet in 1869: its record gives it as {q|britishMuseumRecord2012|pdf1||Purchased through Sotheby's (24-7.ii.1869)}, through the dealer Edward Daniell, from José de Salamanca y Mayol, Marquis of Salamanca, with José de Madrazo as an earlier owner [@britishMuseumRecord2012; @web:https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/P_1869-0410-2463-]. The record describes the lettering as {q|britishMuseumRecord2012|pdf1||numerous explanatory inscriptions in Italian and Spanish} and reads the signature "Mario Cartaro inc Neap 1588" [@britishMuseumRecord2012]; on the plate the name is Latinized, "Marius·Cartarius" [board]. It was thus in a Spanish collection before 1869 (our inference from the record). The giochidelloca.it catalogue gives it as a donation of Lady Charlotte Schreiber [@GameGooseLargest], which the Museum's own record does not support. Seville's registration number, 1869,0410.2463.+, sits next to Gargano's goose board of 1598, 1869,0410.2465.+ [@sevilleImportanceTextPrinted2026, 65, 71]. Zollinger reports that an Australian blogger put the sheet online in 2008 without knowing what it was [@GameGooseLargest]. Collar de Cáceres (2009) and Zollinger (2010) then identified and published it independently [@infantesPinturaQueSe2010, 130, 133; @luceroTableroJuego15882019, 202 n. 12]. It was shown in the Museum's 2017 display "Prints in Play" [@web:https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/P_1869-0410-2463-].

#### A coloured copy

A hand-coloured version of the sheet circulates online: it illustrates two 2013 posts on the *La Bella Donna* blog (its file is named for a class handout, "class-apa") and a 2013 thread on the Tarot History Forum [@FilosofiaCortesanaPath; @TarotHistoryForum]. It follows Cartaro's plate line for line, with flat modern colours on white paper; we take it for a modern colouring of a reproduction, not an early impression (our observation). The sources we cite record no coloured impression and no impression other than the British Museum's [@sevilleImportanceTextPrinted2026, 70; @britishMuseumRecord2012].

#### Among other boards

Seville calls it the unique example of its game sheet [@sevilleImportanceTextPrinted2026, 70]; Zollinger called it the oldest dated goose game known [@GameGooseLargest]. Gatti's *Gioco della scimia*, dated 1588, shares the spiral pergola of balusters [@decaceresTableroItalianoFilosofia2009, 86, 88]. Wood reads such Roman boards as systems of rules and as maps of journeys, whose moralizing works as an apology for gambling in a decade of papal edicts against dice; for her {q|woodChancingItPrint2019|pdf14|463|Each game therefore provides a pretext for gambling} [@woodChancingItPrint2019, 451, 463]. Few boards survive because they were pasted on tavern tables and replaced when worn; the surviving impressions she studied look unused [@woodChancingItPrint2019, 469, 479 n. 20]. Seville adds that an intaglio plate, unlike a woodcut, makes it easy to engrave rules on the sheet [@sevilleImportanceTextPrinted2026, 66].

### about-reception

#### Paratexts of 1587

The approval, dated 13 January 1587, is signed by Alonso de Ercilla, the poet of *La Araucana*; he judged the work ingenious, useful to the commonwealth as honest and pleasant entertainment, and the best of its kind he had seen [@wilsonCervantesItemEmmanuel1968, 367, 369]. Ercilla also appears on the endorsement of Barros's request for a privilege, which calls the work "un descurso de pretensores" [@luceroTableroJuego15882019, 200–01].

Pedro Liñán de Riaza's sonnet, in M, praises the book for trying {q|luceroDEDICATORIAFILOSOFIACORTESANA2019|pdf2|34|reducir a placer la pesadumbre / de pretensiones que consumen vidas} (to turn into pleasure the burden of petitions that consume lives) [@luceroDEDICATORIAFILOSOFIACORTESANA2019, 34]. Cervantes's sonnet, also first in M, compares the small book to a precious stone and promises the reader the thread through the courtly labyrinth [@wilsonCervantesItemEmmanuel1968, 366; @bidwell-steinerFilosofiaCortesanaAlfonso2024, 197]. Lucero reads it as Cervantes's third attempt to win Vázquez's favour, after his verse epistle from Algiers and *La Galatea*; he never won it [@sanchezEdicionesAntiguasFilosofia2016oct16, 180–81]. For two centuries the sonnet was the only reason critics noticed the book [@infantesPinturaQueSe2010, 128]: Fernández de Navarrete printed it in 1819, and Schevill and Bonilla printed it in 1922 from the Vienna copy of C [@wilsonCervantesItemEmmanuel1968, 363–64]. The printed line reads "laberintio" [@bidwell-steinerFilosofiaCortesanaAlfonso2024, 197]; Rodríguez Mansilla quotes it as "laberinto" [@rodriguezmansillaGRABADOPICARAJUSTINA2019, 192].

#### Early readers

Mateo Alemán, in his prologue to Barros's *Proverbios morales* (1598), says the *Filosofía cortesana* could also be called {q|sanchezEdicionesAntiguasFilosofia2016oct16|pdf2|170|desengaño de pretensores} (the disillusioning of office-seekers) [@sanchezEdicionesAntiguasFilosofia2016oct16, 170–71; @wilsonCervantesItemEmmanuel1968, 364]. Gutierre Marqués de Careaga, *Desengaño de Fortuna* (Madrid, 1612, fol. 225), quoted the labour couplets with approval [@wilsonCervantesItemEmmanuel1968, 370]; Pérez Pastor used that quotation to show that the book was not the *Proverbios* [@sanchezEdicionesAntiguasFilosofia2016oct16, 170, 174 n. 15]. Carrera (1617) names Barros as the inventor of a goose variant [@sevilleImportanceTextPrinted2026, 69; @decaceresTableroItalianoFilosofia2009, 85]. Collar de Cáceres infers from Carrera's wording that he knew the Naples edition [@decaceresTableroItalianoFilosofia2009, 85].

Fernando Rodríguez Mansilla argues that the title-page engraving of *La pícara Justina* (Medina del Campo, 1605), a border of 24 pictured squares round a ship of idlers, parodies Barros's board: 24 hours against 63 years, a cross against the palm, the river of oblivion against the sea of suffering, a sleeping Idleness against the oxen of labour [@rodriguezmansillaGRABADOPICARAJUSTINA2019, 183–84, 188–90]. He takes the joke as aimed at the same courtly readers who played Barros's game, not as an attack on it [@rodriguezmansillaGRABADOPICARAJUSTINA2019, 191–92]. He admits the eighteen-year gap as a possible objection [@rodriguezmansillaGRABADOPICARAJUSTINA2019, 185]. *Justina* also uses the word *penseque*, Barros's "I thought…" square; Lucero leaves open whether the game shaped the later use [@lucerosanchezAsnoPenseQue2019, 171–72].

#### Modern scholarship

- **E. M. Wilson (1968)** described the Emmanuel College copy of M, summarized the game in English and separated the book from the *Proverbios* [@wilsonCervantesItemEmmanuel1968, 363–71]. He admitted he could not follow every detail of the rules [@wilsonCervantesItemEmmanuel1968, 367].
- **Trevor J. Dadson (1987)** edited M with a facsimile and a conjectural board, and published Barros's library [@infantesPinturaQueSe2010, 128–30; @decaceresTableroItalianoFilosofia2009, 83].
- **Víctor Infantes** designed the 1987 board, wrote on the work as "emblemática sin emblemas" (1996), published the 1585 Liaño letter (2010) and returned to it in *Ludo ergo sum* (2014) [@infantesPinturaQueSe2010, 129–30, 134–35; @luceroTableroJuego15882019, 201; @sanchezEdicionesAntiguasFilosofia2016oct16, 177].
- **José Martínez Millán (1996)** read the book as a treatise on court behaviour and placed Barros in the *castellanista* faction; he had not seen a board [@millanFilosofiaCortesanaAlonso1996, 461 n. 4, 463–64].
- **Fernando Collar de Cáceres (2009)** published the Naples board, documented Barros's life and read the board's images against the emblem books [@decaceresTableroItalianoFilosofia2009, 81–104].
- **Manfred Zollinger (2010)** identified the board independently and described it with its rules [@GameGooseLargest; @infantesPinturaQueSe2010, 133].
- **Kelli Wood (2019)** set it among Roman printed gambling boards of 1588–1600 [@woodChancingItPrint2019, 453, 462–63].
- **Ernesto Lucero (2016–2021)** found the princeps, proposed the lost Madrid boards, and wrote on the dedications, labour, the "I thought…" square and the rules; his critical edition appeared in 2019 [@sanchezFILOSOFIACORTESANAALONSO, 12–13].
- **Marlen Bidwell-Steiner (2024)** read the game as casuistic practice and as a picture of an all-male court [@bidwell-steinerFilosofiaCortesanaAlfonso2024, 195–96, 208–11].
- **Adrian Seville (2026)** used it as his first case of a board game whose companion book carries its meaning [@sevilleImportanceTextPrinted2026, 70].

Lucero notes that modern critics neglected the book for the showy board [@luceroTableroJuego15882019, 198]. Biagioli (1993) cited the game at second hand from Geoffrey Parker, as {q|biagioliGalileoCourtierPractice1993|pdf50|35|a court game not unlike today's "Monopoly"} [@biagioliGalileoCourtierPractice1993, 35]; Wilson had compared it to Snakes and Ladders and Monopoly [@wilsonCervantesItemEmmanuel1968, 367]. Amateur English translations of the board circulated from 2013 on the La Bella Donna blog [@TranslationFilosofiaCortesana; @GiocoDellocaBella].

### about-rules

#### What we have to go on

Three texts give rules, and they differ. G and C end with a short section of rules, which Lucero finds laconic and in agreement on the concrete rules [@luceroTableroJuego15882019, 203 n. 13]. M replaces it with a longer "Declaración del juego y orden de jugarle", with a worked example for three players [@sanchezEdicionesAntiguasFilosofia2016oct16, 189–90]. The prose sections of all three also state rules square by square [@barrosFilosofiaCortesanaMoralizada1588, pdf 27–28, pdf 47–52]. The Naples board adds short Italian labels [board]. G is known to us only through Lucero's quotations and tables [@sanchezEdicionesAntiguasFilosofia2016oct16, 183–90; @luceroTableroJuego15882019, 204].

#### Rules common to all three

- Move by the points thrown; if two land on one square, the newcomer stays and the first takes the square the newcomer left [@barrosFilosofiaCortesanaMoralizada1588, pdf 66; @sanchezEdicionesAntiguasFilosofia2016oct16, 189]. Barros calls this "el vso de la competencia" (the custom of competition) [@barrosFilosofiaCortesanaMoralizada1588, pdf 28].
- Labour (the oxen, 4, 12, 17, 23, 30, 34, 41, 48, 57): move on again as many squares as the throw that brought you there [@barrosFilosofiaCortesanaMoralizada1588, pdf 66; board] [@luceroFilosofiaCortesanaJuego2020, 124].
- Pass of Hope (15): go on to the Favourite (26), paying one stake "for each" (M: "for each of the two") [@barrosFilosofiaCortesanaMoralizada1588, pdf 66–67; @debarrosFilosofiaCortesana1587, pdf 12]. Board: 15 "Al priuato n.º 26 Paga"; 26 "Paga" [board].
- Well of Oblivion (32): miss a turn, pay one stake to each player and two to the pot "para sogas" (for ropes) [@barrosFilosofiaCortesanaMoralizada1588, pdf 67]. Board: "Per le fune 1 et 2 fermesi / Para sogas 1 y 2 estese" [board].
- What Will They Say? (36) to the Dice (28); False Friendship (39) to the Prodigal (7); Change of Ministers (43) to Flattery (10); Death of the Patron (46): start the game again; House of Fortune (51): throw twice; "I Thought…" (55) to Diligence (20) [@barrosFilosofiaCortesanaMoralizada1588, pdf 67–68]. Board labels on 36, 39, 43, 46 and 55 add "Paga"; 51 reads "Giuoca 2 uolte" [board].
- Poverty to the Dice at 53, with alms [@barrosFilosofiaCortesanaMoralizada1588, pdf 68; @luceroTableroJuego15882019, 204]. Board: "Limosina et alla sorte 53" [board].
- Overshoot the palm: go back the excess, paying one stake "por cada vez que boluiere atras de qualquier suerte que sea" (each time one goes back, of whatever kind) [@barrosFilosofiaCortesanaMoralizada1588, pdf 68–69; @luceroTableroJuego15882019, 204].

#### G (Madrid, 1587, first edition)

Headed "Esto se juega en esta forma"; its first rule speaks of "el dado", the die [@sanchezEdicionesAntiguasFilosofia2016oct16, 189]. Poverty stands at 59 [@sanchezEdicionesAntiguasFilosofia2016oct16, 180 n. 43, 189; @luceroTableroJuego15882019, 204]. The poor player is given alms ("danle limosna"), with no amount [@luceroTableroJuego15882019, 204]. G has no sentence on how the game is won; its rules end at the overshoot clause, set in the shape of a goblet, and "Deo gratias" [@sanchezEdicionesAntiguasFilosofia2016oct16, 189–90].

#### M (Madrid, 1587, Madrigal)

M's Declaración adds [@debarrosFilosofiaCortesana1587, pdf 11–12; @GameGooseLargest; @millanFilosofiaCortesanaAlonso1996, 470–71]:
- the sheet is spread on a table or pasted to it;
- each player first puts one stake, of whatever value is agreed, into the pot;
- play is with two dice marked on every face from one to six, or with six *arenillas* marked on one face;
- two or more may play, as many as wish; each has a distinct marker (a ring, a two-real coin, a doubloon in the example) and plays in turn;
- a player displaced at the very start is left without a square;
- the poor player receives one stake from each other player, pays nothing for going back, and is the one exception to the overshoot charge;
- whoever lands exactly on 63, the palm, takes the whole pot, both the opening stakes and everything paid in during play.

The worked example applies these rules. Pedro, on 6, throws 9 to reach 15, reads "Al privado" above it, pays one stake and moves to 26, paying another. Rodrigo, on 5, throws 10, reaches 15, moves to 26, finds Pedro there and takes the square, paying two stakes. Pedro goes back to 5, where Rodrigo had been, paying one stake "porque volvió atrás" (because he went back) [@debarrosFilosofiaCortesana1587, pdf 12]. The example ends by saying the same holds for the other squares and for more players [@debarrosFilosofiaCortesana1587, pdf 12].

#### C (Naples, 1588, with Cartaro's board)

Headed "EL YVEGO SE juega en esta forma", it opens "EL Que lança el dado" (whoever throws the die) [@barrosFilosofiaCortesanaMoralizada1588, pdf 66]. Poverty is at 60, as in M and on the board [@barrosFilosofiaCortesanaMoralizada1588, pdf 68; board]. It gives alms without an amount, like G [@barrosFilosofiaCortesanaMoralizada1588, pdf 68]. It lacks M's rule for the start, M's exception for Poverty, and any rule for winning; the palm is never numbered in the text [@barrosFilosofiaCortesanaMoralizada1588, pdf 66–69]. On the board the palm's own motto, "Ne il poco ne lassai / Ni lo mucho ni la poco" (neither too much nor too little), states the exact-throw rule, as Barros explains in the prose [board] [@millanFilosofiaCortesanaAlonso1996, 481].

#### What this digital version has to decide

The sources leave gaps. For each, the options, what the sources say, and the default we propose.

1. **How many dice in G and C.** G and C say "el dado" [@sanchezEdicionesAntiguasFilosofia2016oct16, 189; @barrosFilosofiaCortesanaMoralizada1588, pdf 66]. M says two dice, or six *arenillas* [@debarrosFilosofiaCortesana1587, pdf 11]. M's own last rule also says "con los puntos del dado" while using two dice, so the singular can be generic (our observation) [@debarrosFilosofiaCortesana1587, pdf 12]. The 1585 Liaño letter describes the goose game at court as played with two dice [@infantesPinturaQueSe2010, 135]. Barros's Fortune rule, that two throws from 51 can win, fits two dice, with 12 the most points in one throw [@luceroFilosofiaCortesanaJuego2020, 125; @bidwell-steinerFilosofiaCortesanaAlfonso2024, 203]. The Dice squares picture three dice (1, 2, 3 at 28; 4, 4, 3 at 53), and Lucero notes that the goose game's dice squares show two [@luceroFilosofiaCortesanaJuego2020, 124; @GameGooseLargest; board]; no text makes the picture a rule for play. Options: one die; two dice. Proposed default: two dice in all three versions, with one die available as a variant for G and C. The *arenillas* are six markers marked on one face only [@web:https://dle.rae.es/arenilla]; no source we cite says how they were counted, so we do not implement them.
2. **The alms in G and C.** Both say only "danle limosna" [@luceroTableroJuego15882019, 204; @barrosFilosofiaCortesanaMoralizada1588, pdf 68]. M specifies one stake from each other player [@debarrosFilosofiaCortesana1587, pdf 12]. Martínez Millán, Wilson and Rodríguez Mansilla read the text as the others giving to the poor player; Bidwell-Steiner reads it as the poor player giving alms [@millanFilosofiaCortesanaAlonso1996, 481; @wilsonCervantesItemEmmanuel1968, 368; @rodriguezmansillaGRABADOPICARAJUSTINA2019, 190; @bidwell-steinerFilosofiaCortesanaAlfonso2024, 204]. M settles it for M. Options for G and C: one stake from each player (M's rule); one stake from the pot; no payment. Proposed default: M's rule.
3. **Which backward moves cost a stake.** All three versions charge one stake for going back "de cualquier suerte que sea" [@barrosFilosofiaCortesanaMoralizada1588, pdf 68–69]. The phrase can mean only the overshoot at the end, or any throw that sends a player back. M's example charges the displaced player one stake for going back [@debarrosFilosofiaCortesana1587, pdf 12], and M exempts the poor player from paying, which only makes sense if the move from Poverty to 53 would otherwise cost (our observation) [@debarrosFilosofiaCortesana1587, pdf 12]. The board prints "Paga" on every square that sends a player back (36, 39, 43, 46, 55) but not on Fortune, and prints it on the forward move 15–26 [board]; we read these labels as the same general rule, not an extra charge, because M's Declaración lists no separate payment for those squares (our observation) [@debarrosFilosofiaCortesana1587, pdf 12]. Options: (a) charge only the overshoot; (b) charge every backward move: overshoot, displacement, and the squares that send back; (c) as (b) plus a second charge on the labelled squares. Proposed default: (b) in all versions; in G and C, whether the poor player also pays for the move to 53 is not stated; we exempt that player, as M does.
4. **A special square sends you onto an occupied square.** For a forward jump, M's example settles it: Rodrigo, sent from 15 to 26, takes Pedro's square, and Pedro goes back to the square Rodrigo had left before his throw, paying a stake [@debarrosFilosofiaCortesana1587, pdf 12]. For a backward move (39 to 7, 43 to 10, 36 to 28, 55 to 20, 60 to 53, 46 to the start) the sources are silent. Options: (a) the general rule, with the displaced player moving to the square the mover occupied before the throw, which can move the displaced player forward; (b) the displaced player moves to the square the mover was sent from; (c) no displacement on backward moves, and both share the square. Proposed default: (a), which follows the wording of the rule and M's example; we flag it in play when it moves a player forward. The same choice applies when a labour move ends on an occupied square.
5. **Starting position.** M: a player displaced at the start "se ha de quedar sin casa", because the newcomer had no square before [@debarrosFilosofiaCortesana1587, pdf 12]. Players therefore begin off the track, before the gate (our reading) [@debarrosFilosofiaCortesana1587, pdf 12]. The gate itself carries no number; square 1 is the peacock [board]. C lacks M's clause [@barrosFilosofiaCortesanaMoralizada1588, pdf 66]. Proposed default: all markers start off the board at 0, in front of the gate; a player displaced from a square reached by an opening throw returns to 0; the Death of the Patron returns a player to 0.
6. **Purses.** Only M speaks of money at the start: one stake of an agreed value [@debarrosFilosofiaCortesana1587, pdf 11]. No source we cite gives a purse, a limit on debt, or what happens when a player cannot pay. Lucero observes that the game's slow progress limits its money consequences [@luceroFilosofiaCortesanaJuego2020, 126]. Options: an unlimited purse, with a running balance that may go negative; a fixed purse, with a bankrupt player out of the game; a fixed purse, with a bankrupt player continuing without paying. Proposed default: each player starts with 20 stakes and may go negative; the balance is shown, and the winner takes the pot.

Further points the sources leave open:

- **Fortune (51), "juega dos veces".** Two readings: one more throw, or two more throws. C's prose says the player who reaches Fortune plays twice, "estando el juego de manera, que co[n] dos suertes se gana la polla, y con las demas se pierde, o se dilata" (the game being so arranged that with two throws the pot is won, and with the others it is lost or put off) [@barrosFilosofiaCortesanaMoralizada1588, pdf 47]; Wilson could not follow the Spanish [@wilsonCervantesItemEmmanuel1968, 368]. Lucero reads it as one extra throw, placed at 51 so that a throw of twelve wins [@luceroFilosofiaCortesanaJuego2020, 125]; Collar de Cáceres reads the board's "Giuoca 2 uolte" as two additional throws [@decaceresTableroItalianoFilosofia2009, 96]. Our translator notes that on Cartaro's board exactly two throws win from 51: twelve, straight to the palm, and six, to the oxen at 57 and on by six more to 63 (our observation) [@barrosFilosofiaCortesanaMoralizada1588, pdf 47; board]. That reading makes "dos suertes" two winning throws, not two throws in a row. Our default: one extra throw at once; two extra throws can be chosen in the setup.
- **Poverty in G.** G puts Poverty at 59 while the only board shows it at 60 [@luceroTableroJuego15882019, 204; board]. Under G the game treats 59 as Poverty and 60 as a plain square, and marks the change on the board image.
- **Labour in G.** G had eight labour verses of its own, four of them not in M, and Lucero's thesis infers that G's labour squares were spread more evenly [@sanchezEdicionesAntiguasFilosofia2016oct16, 187–88; @sanchezFILOSOFIACORTESANAALONSO, 20]. G's positions are unknown; under G we keep Cartaro's labour squares and show G's verses in G's printed order.
- **The Well.** C says the player pays "auiendo estado sin [j]ugar vna mano" (having been a round without playing), which can mean that the player pays after missing their turn or on arrival [@barrosFilosofiaCortesanaMoralizada1588, pdf 67]. Proposed default: the player pays on arrival and misses their next turn.
- **Turn order.** M: "por su orden", and in the example Pedro plays first [@debarrosFilosofiaCortesana1587, pdf 11]. The rule that the youngest starts, given on the La Bella Donna blog, is not in the sources we cite [@TranslationFilosofiaCortesana].
- **Special squares reached by a rebound or a transfer.** No source says whether a square reached by counting back from 63, or by a transfer, takes effect. Proposed default: a square reached by rebound takes effect; a square reached by transfer does not (so 60 to 53 does not trigger another move).
- **Landing directly on the Favourite (26).** The board writes "Paga." at 26, but the rules charge a stake there only for the player who arrives from the Pass of Hope [@barrosFilosofiaCortesanaMoralizada1588, pdf 66–67; @debarrosFilosofiaCortesana1587, pdf 12]. We charge only in that case, and read the board's "Paga." as the second half of the label at 15 (our observation) [board].
- **Endless Labour.** If a Labour move reached by counting back from the palm would lead back to the same oxen (a throw of twelve from 57 returns to 57), the player stops on the oxen. The sources do not foresee the case; this is our decision.
- **What this edition implements.** Two dice (one die available); a stake for every move back, including being displaced (the overshoot only, as an option); the poor player receives one stake from each other player and pays nothing for the move to 53, in all three versions; players start before the gate, and a player displaced from a square reached by an opening throw returns there; each purse holds 20 stakes and may go below zero; a square reached by counting back from 63 takes effect, a square reached by being sent there does not; the winner takes the whole pot. The rules of the game engine are in `js/game.js`, with the source of each rule noted beside it.

## D. Editorial note: how this edition was made

**Who made it.** This edition was made by Claude, an AI model made by Anthropic, at the request of Scott B. Weingart; "we" in these pages means the edition. Every quotation in the notes was checked by program against the text of its source. The transcriptions, translations and interpretations have not been reviewed by a specialist in early modern Spanish or Italian, and the edition is a draft until they have been. None of the source material gathered for the project was used by Anthropic to train its models. See Credits and rights, below.

**Base texts.** The book is Barros's third edition, C (Naples: Joseph Cacchij, 1588), transcribed from the Google Books scan of the only copy recorded (Lucero 2016), Vienna, Österreichische Nationalbibliothek, 35 V 49 [@barrosFilosofiaCortesanaMoralizada1588]. We chose C because it was issued with the only board known to survive. The board is Mario Cartaro's sheet of 1588, British Museum 1869,0410.2463.+, transcribed from two photographs [board]. Readings of M (Madrid: Pedro Madrigal, 1587) come from a modernized transcription of that edition and from the plates in Wilson 1968; they are reported, not adopted [@debarrosFilosofiaCortesana1587; @wilsonCervantesItemEmmanuel1968, 366]. G (Madrid: widow of Alonso Gómez, 1587) is known to us only through Lucero's published collation, and its readings are cited from him [@sanchezEdicionesAntiguasFilosofia2016oct16, 183–90].

**Transcription.** The transcription of C is diplomatic. It keeps the spelling, u/v, i/j, accents, capitals and punctuation of the print. Long s is given as s; ligatures are resolved. Abbreviations are expanded in square brackets (q̃ as q[ue]; a tilde over a vowel as [n] or [m]). Word division is normalized where the print's spacing is ambiguous. Line breaks are not kept; paragraphs and verse lines are. Page starts, signatures, running heads and catchwords are marked. Doubtful readings are marked [?], illegible letters [illeg.], and misprints [sic]. Nothing is corrected or supplied from another edition, and recorded damage stays recorded. The board transcription follows the same rules. It also records the engraver's run-together words and the Italian above the Spanish in each square.

**Translation.** The English aims to be faithful first and readable second: nothing added, nothing dropped, every figure of speech kept, in modern, slightly elevated British English. Barros's long periods may be split, never merged or reordered. Words with double senses (*suerte*, a throw and a lot; *trabajo*, work and hardship; *cuesta*, a slope and "it costs"; *casa*, a house of the board and a household) are flagged in notes. Fixed terms are used throughout (*pretendiente*, petitioner; *valedor*, patron; *privado*, the Favourite; *tanto*, a stake; *polla*, the pot). Each verse has a fixed rhymed rendering, shown to players, and a literal one beneath it. Barros calls the squares of the board *casas*, houses; the translation of his text keeps his word, and everywhere else this edition says "square". It says "petitioner" for *pretendiente*, not "suitor", and "case" for a petitioner's *negocio*, not "business".

**The version switch.** Players can choose G, M or C. The board image is always Cartaro's, the only board known. Under G, Poverty stands on 59, not 60, and G's own couplets are shown for the eight Labour squares where its text differs from the board. Under M and C the rules are the same; under M, each square also shows the passages M adds or changes. Whichever edition is chosen, Barros's prose explanation of each square is quoted from C, because G's text is known to us only through Lucero's collation and M's only through a modernized transcription. M's "Declaración del juego" and its worked example are used under every edition to settle what G and C leave unsaid. Where all three are silent, the decisions set out in "How the game is played" apply; the setup screen and the rules panel link to them.
