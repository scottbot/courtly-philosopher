## A. Scrollytelling introduction (landing page)

<!-- Quotations in this section give the edition's English first, in quotation marks, and the original second, as a verified {q|…} in parentheses (editor's decision, LAY-06). The `visual:` lines are notes for the editor; the visuals themselves are set in js/story.js SCENES. -->

### madrid-1587
visual: whole board, slowly zooming to the title "FILOSOFIA CORTESANA / DE ALONSO DE BARROS" along the top margin.

The court of Philip II, king of Spain, had been settled in Madrid since 1561 [@web:https://www.redalyc.org/pdf/7370/737080494002.pdf, 2–3], and the king lived in its royal palace, the Alcázar [@web:https://www.museodelprado.es/en/learn/encyclopedia/voice/alcazar-de-madrid-real/cd2eb3b7-3aa7-45f9-9ce5-0ed711622f08]. The king was the source of favor, so people came to court to ask for it [@millanFilosofiaCortesanaAlonso1996, 465–66]. These petitioners were the *pretendientes*. What they pressed for was a *merced*, a grant made by the king's grace, not a wage owed for service, and always scarcer than the men who wanted it [@luceroIdeaTrabajoFilosofia2019, 823, 829]. Philip II's courtiers, as Fernando Collar de Cáceres puts it after the historian Geoffrey Parker, had among their obsessions boredom and promotion ({q|decaceresTableroItalianoFilosofia2009|pdf2|82|aburrimiento y promoción}) [@decaceresTableroItalianoFilosofia2009, 82]; the book and the game answered both. The game's title runs along the top of the sheet: *Filosofia cortesana de Alonso de Barros*{es} [board].

### barros
visual: lower-right margin, the line "CRIADO DEL REY NR̃O S.ᵒʳ" under the title's end, then "Con su priuilegio".

The man who made it signed himself *criado del Rey*{es}, the king's servant [board] [@sanchezEdicionesAntiguasFilosofia2016oct16, 174, 179]. Barros, a Segovian, took over his dead father's post of *aposentador*, one of the officials who found lodgings for the court, in 1563, and died in it in 1604 [@decaceresTableroItalianoFilosofia2009, 82; @luceroDEDICATORIAFILOSOFIACORTESANA2019, 34]. As his modern editor tells the story, the post never contented him: from the early 1580s he petitioned the king for more in written memorials [@luceroDEDICATORIAFILOSOFIACORTESANA2019, 34]. The book is dedicated to his patron, Mateo Vázquez de Leca, secretary to Philip II [@luceroDEDICATORIAFILOSOFIACORTESANA2019, 33–35; @sanchezFILOSOFIACORTESANAALONSO, 22]. Vázquez had made his own way up: E. M. Wilson records that he “was born in Algiers, where his mother was a Christian captive” [@wilsonCervantesItemEmmanuel1968, 365]. Ernesto Lucero, who edited the book in 2019, sums up Barros's method: “Alonso de Barros conceived of writing as a service at court” ({q|luceroDEDICATORIAFILOSOFIACORTESANA2019|pdf1|33|Alonso de Barros concibió la escritura como un servicio cortesano.}) [@luceroDEDICATORIAFILOSOFIACORTESANA2019, 33, 35 n. 3; @sanchezFILOSOFIACORTESANAALONSO, 18].

### what-for
visual: the gate and square 1 (js/story.js: square 1 and the gate).

Barros says what he meant in his preface to the reader: “my intention has been nothing but to show, in jest and in play, the earnest truths and misfortunes that follow long petitioning” ({q|debarrosFilosofiaCortesana1587|pdf3|pdf 3|mi intento no ha sido sino mostrar entre burla y juego las veras y desventuras que siguen a una larga pretensión}) [@debarrosFilosofiaCortesana1587, pdf 3; @barrosFilosofiaCortesanaMoralizada1588, pdf 17]. His dedication calls the book a teaching for anyone who seeks advancement, so that they “may know the beginnings, the means and the ends by which human petitions travel, and where they come to rest” ({q|luceroDEDICATORIAFILOSOFIACORTESANA2019|pdf3|35|sepan los principios, los medios y los fines por do caminan y vienen a parar las pretensiones humanas}) [@luceroDEDICATORIAFILOSOFIACORTESANA2019, 35; @barrosFilosofiaCortesanaMoralizada1588, pdf 19]. His friend the novelist Mateo Alemán wrote in 1598 that it could as well be called “the disillusioning of office-seekers” ({q|sanchezEdicionesAntiguasFilosofia2016oct16|pdf2|170|desengaño de pretensores}) [@sanchezEdicionesAntiguasFilosofia2016oct16, 170–71]. The board also says two things at once: its verses praise hard work, while its dice hand out luck. Lucero draws the conclusion: “Then Fortune always wins” ({q|luceroIdeaTrabajoFilosofia2019|pdf9|829|Entonces, Fortuna siempre vence.}) [@luceroIdeaTrabajoFilosofia2019, 829].

### goose-arrives
visual: square 15, the bridge (Passo della speranza), a survival of the Bridge of the Game of the Goose.

The game he borrowed was the Game of the Goose, a race. Players throw two dice and move their markers along a spiral of 63 squares; some squares help, some send a player back, and the first to land exactly on the last square takes the pot [@luceroFilosofiaCortesanaJuego2020, 122; @woodChancingItPrint2019, 458]. E. M. Wilson, who described Barros's game in English in 1968, compared it to Snakes and Ladders and to Monopoly [@wilsonCervantesItemEmmanuel1968, 367]. The goose game was new at the Spanish court; a writer of 1617 said it came from Florence, a gift of the grand duke of Tuscany to Philip II [@sevilleImportanceTextPrinted2026, 69]. In 1585 the king's jester complained in a letter that the prince and the infanta had won forty escudos from him at this devilish game, “played with two dice” ({q|infantesPinturaQueSe2010|pdf11|135|que se juega con dos dados}), and that “the king likes to see me stung” ({q|infantesPinturaQueSe2010|pdf11|135|gusta el rey de verme picado}) [@infantesPinturaQueSe2010, 134–35]. The bridge outlined here, square 15, is Barros's version of the goose game's Bridge, where a player paid a toll and moved on [@luceroFilosofiaCortesanaJuego2021juin20, 144; @woodChancingItPrint2019, 458].

### moral-map
visual: square 4, the yoked oxen under a festoon of fruit, with the verse panel beneath.

Barros kept the race of the goose game (63 squares on a spiral, an exact throw to finish) and changed its meaning [@luceroFilosofiaCortesanaJuego2020, 125]. The geese became yoked oxen, the square of Labor [@luceroFilosofiaCortesanaJuego2020, 124]. Death became the death of one's patron [@luceroFilosofiaCortesanaJuego2020, 124]. The other squares became the chances of a petition at court: hope, flattery, false friends, a change of ministers, the patron's death, poverty [@luceroFilosofiaCortesanaJuego2021juin20, 135, 149]. For Barros the 63 squares are the years of life that a petition consumes [@luceroFilosofiaCortesanaJuego2021juin20, 135]. Lucero calls the game “the first metaphorical, themed version of the goose game on record” ({q|luceroFilosofiaCortesanaJuego2020|pdf1|117|la primera tematización metafórica del juego de la oca de que tenemos constancia}) [@luceroFilosofiaCortesanaJuego2020, 117].

### sheet-and-book
visual: the title page and royal license of the 1588 book (book pages p007, p009).

It came in two parts. The royal license to print, of 9 February 1587, says that Barros had composed “a picture entitled *Filosofía cortesana*{es}, with a number of different figures and verses, contained on a large sheet” ({q|luceroTableroJuego15882019|pdf3|198|una pintura intitulada Filosofía cortesana, con ciertas diferencias de figuras y letras, que se contiene en un pliego grande}) [@luceroTableroJuego15882019, 198]. He had also, it says, “moralized it in a separate account” ({q|luceroTableroJuego15882019|pdf3|198|la habéis moralizado en una relación aparte}) [@luceroTableroJuego15882019, 198]: he had written a moral explanation of it. The sheet was the board; the little book gave the rules and explained each picture [@luceroTableroJuego15882019, 197–98]. Barros's second edition says the sheet traveled apart from the book, to be spread on a table or pasted onto one [@debarrosFilosofiaCortesana1587, pdf 11]. Kelli Wood shows that in Rome such printed sheets were gambled on by the nobility and in common drinking-houses alike, and she explains why so few survive: they “were pasted down on tables in taverns, gambled on, and then pasted over when they were worn out” [@woodChancingItPrint2019, 451, 469]. E. M. Wilson put it plainly: {q|wilsonCervantesItemEmmanuel1968|pdf8|369|You improve yourself morally while you gamble.}

### reading-a-square
visual: square 26, Il priuato (the Favorite): two cloaked men, one offering a dish; the "Paga." label in the gutter; the two couplets.

Each square with a picture works like an emblem, the picture-and-verse form popular in the period. Lucero maps the parts: the square's name is the motto, the picture is the image, and the verses under it, together with Barros's prose in the book, are the explanation [@luceroFilosofiaCortesanaJuego2020, 117]. Take square 26. The heading reads *Il priuato*, the Favorite [board]. A man offers something to another at a doorway [board]; Barros says it is the first fruits of the petitioner's estate, offered in return for a hearing [@decaceresTableroItalianoFilosofia2009, 94]. The Spanish verse below it, in English: “Before you ask another's hand, / fill yours, or empty-handed stand” [board]. A small label above says *Paga*: pay [board] [@decaceresTableroItalianoFilosofia2009, 94].

### three-editions
visual: Cervantes's sonnet and the first page of the rules in the 1588 book (book pages p015, p066).

The book was printed three times in little more than a year, which Lucero takes as a sign that it was popular: twice in Madrid in 1587, the second time with additions, among them a worked example of play and a sonnet by Cervantes, and once in Naples in 1588 [@sanchezEdicionesAntiguasFilosofia2016oct16, 177, 179, 191; @luceroDEDICATORIAFILOSOFIACORTESANA2019, 38–40, 47]. One copy of each edition is known, and no board for either Madrid edition; when you play, you can switch between the three versions [@sanchezEdicionesAntiguasFilosofia2016oct16, 176–77, 180; @luceroTableroJuego15882019, 202].

### naples-cartaro
visual: bottom-right signature "Marius·Cartarius.Jnc Neap·1588·", then the Naples privilege line "Con priuilegio di Sua Ecc.ª per ·X· / anni nel Regno di Napoli".

The board that survives was engraved in Naples in 1588 by Mario Cartaro, a printmaker from Viterbo, active first in Rome, who went to Naples in 1586 to work on its maps [board] [@woodChancingItPrint2019, 462; @sevilleImportanceTextPrinted2026, 70]. Naples then belonged to Philip II's crown and was governed for him by a viceroy, the “His Excellency” whose privilege, a ten-year right to print the sheet in the Kingdom of Naples, is engraved at its foot [board] [@luceroTableroJuego15882019, 202, 203 n. 14; @GameGooseLargest]. Its verses are in Italian and Spanish; Lucero thinks the bilingual board was meant for the Spaniards living in Naples and for the local courtiers [@decaceresTableroItalianoFilosofia2009, 86; @luceroTableroJuego15882019, 203 n. 14]. It is a single sheet, etched and engraved, 531 × 404 mm [@woodChancingItPrint2019, 463]. The only copy recorded in the sources we cite is in the British Museum (1869,0410.2463.+), which bought it in 1869 through the dealer Edward Daniell, from the collection of the Marquis of Salamanca [@britishMuseumRecord2012; @web:https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/P_1869-0410-2463-]. Scholars recognized what it was in 2009 and 2010 [@infantesPinturaQueSe2010, 130, 133].

### the-pot
visual: the center: the palm under its gate at square 63, then the man at the palm holding a fish, one shoe left behind.

Money was part of the game. In the second Madrid edition each player puts one stake, a sum agreed before play, into the pot; players pay again at certain squares and each time they are sent back, and the one who lands exactly on 63 takes everything [@debarrosFilosofiaCortesana1587, pdf 11–12]. Lucero reads the pot as the court's favors, which “are limited and not enough for everyone” ({q|luceroFilosofiaCortesanaJuego2021juin20|pdf10|141|son limitados y no alcanzan para todos}) [@luceroFilosofiaCortesanaJuego2021juin20, 141]. The winner's square shows a palm, and beside it stands a man who has caught a fish but left a shoe behind: nothing is won without cost [@decaceresTableroItalianoFilosofia2009, 97; board].

### cervantes
visual: the center sea (Mare di soffrimento / Mar de suffrimiento): ships, the angler with his rod, the cartouche.

Miguel de Cervantes, later the author of *Don Quixote*, wrote a sonnet for the second edition while he was trying to win Mateo Vázquez's favor; he never won it [@sanchezEdicionesAntiguasFilosofia2016oct16, 180–81 and n. 41]. The sonnet praises a book that joins pleasure and profit in a cheerful game [@wilsonCervantesItemEmmanuel1968, 366]. It pictures the reader as a sailor: “whoever sails the mad gulf of the sea of petitions will at once see the thread through the courtly labyrinth” ({q|bidwell-steinerFilosofiaCortesanaAlfonso2024|pdf3|197|El que navega por el golfo insano/ del mar de pretensiones vera al punto/ del cortesano laberintio el hilo}) [@bidwell-steinerFilosofiaCortesanaAlfonso2024, 197]. At the center of the board lies the Sea of Suffering, with its verse: “Who seeks advancement must endure, / as death for all the born is sure” [board] [@decaceresTableroItalianoFilosofia2009, 99].

### barros-board
visual: square 46, the Death of the Patron.

Barros played his own game for real. In 1582 he was passed over for an accounting post in Guatemala, and in 1583 he was refused the assayership of the Segovia mint [@decaceresTableroItalianoFilosofia2009, 82 and n. 10; @millanFilosofiaCortesanaAlonso1996, 463 n. 9]. In 1587, weeks after this book was approved, he asked for a revenue office at Silos, and the king wrote on the file: “Let me know why it has been vacant so long, whether I have been consulted on it before, and whether others ask for it” ({q|luceroDEDICATORIAFILOSOFIACORTESANA2019|pdf3|35|Avíseme cómo ha tanto tiempo que está vaca, y si se me ha consultado otras veces, y si la piden otros}) [@luceroDEDICATORIAFILOSOFIACORTESANA2019, 35 n. 6]. He got the post [@luceroDEDICATORIAFILOSOFIACORTESANA2019, 35–36]. Then his patrons died: Mateo Vázquez in 1591, García de Loaysa in 1599 [@luceroDedicatoriaProverbiosMorales2019, 109 n. 21, 112]. Lucero writes that the Death of the Patron “sends Barros back to the starting square of his own board” ({q|luceroDedicatoriaProverbiosMorales2019|pdf20|120|devuelve a Barros a la casilla de salida de su tablero de la Filosofía cortesana}) [@luceroDedicatoriaProverbiosMorales2019, 120]. In 1600, under the new king, he was refused leave to pass his post to a man outside his family [@luceroDedicatoriaProverbiosMorales2019, 119 and n. 48]. He died in 1604, still in the post he had inherited from his father [@decaceresTableroItalianoFilosofia2009, 82, 100 n. 11; @luceroDEDICATORIAFILOSOFIACORTESANA2019, 34].

### enter
visual: bottom-left corner: the entrance arch with "Guarda / l'fine", the swan on the skull blowing its trumpet ("Noscete ipsum"), and square 1, the peacock with its tail spread.

The way in is a gate [board]. Above it a swan stands on a skull and blows a trumpet that says “Know thyself”; between the pillars is written “Look to the end” [board] [@decaceresTableroItalianoFilosofia2009, 93]. Barros explains that the beginner enters by the Gate of Opinion, deceived by their own good opinion of themselves, and struts like the peacock on the first square until time tells them to look at their feet [@decaceresTableroItalianoFilosofia2009, 93]. Its verse: “Reason looks down to the feet; / Opinion, to the wheel's conceit” [board].

## B. About pages

### about-glance
title: At a glance

A short guide to the people, words, dates and places that come up across the edition. Most lines carry a source; the essays below say more.

#### Who's who, then

- **Alonso de Barros** (c. 1540/52–1604), the game's author: a royal *aposentador*, one of the officials who found lodgings for the court, from 1563 until his death [@luceroDEDICATORIAFILOSOFIACORTESANA2019, 34; @decaceresTableroItalianoFilosofia2009, 82].
- **Philip II**, king of Spain, at whose court in Madrid the game is set; he died on 13 September 1598 [@woodChancingItPrint2019, 461; @luceroDedicatoriaProverbiosMorales2019, 112 n. 28].
- **Mateo Vázquez de Leca** (1542–91), born in Algiers, Philip II's secretary from 1573, Barros's patron and the book's dedicatee [@wilsonCervantesItemEmmanuel1968, 365; @luceroDedicatoriaProverbiosMorales2019, 109 n. 21].
- **García de Loaysa Girón** (d. 1599), tutor of the future Philip III and, in 1598, archbishop of Toledo; Barros's second patron, to whom he dedicated his *Proverbios morales*{es} [@luceroDedicatoriaProverbiosMorales2019, 104, 111–12].
- **Antonio Pérez**, royal secretary, Vázquez's great rival, who fell from power [@luceroFilosofiaCortesanaJuego2021juin20, 137 n. 3; @bidwell-steinerFilosofiaCortesanaAlfonso2024, 210–11; @wilsonCervantesItemEmmanuel1968, 365].
- **Mario Cartaro** (Viterbo, 1540/45 – Naples, 1620), engraver, print dealer and mapmaker, who engraved the surviving board in Naples in 1588 [@sevilleImportanceTextPrinted2026, 70 n. 16; @web:https://www.treccani.it/enciclopedia/mario-cartaro_(Dizionario-Biografico)/].
- **Miguel de Cervantes**, later the author of *Don Quixote*, and **Pedro Liñán de Riaza**, poets who wrote sonnets for the second edition [@sanchezEdicionesAntiguasFilosofia2016oct16, 180–81 and n. 41; @luceroDEDICATORIAFILOSOFIACORTESANA2019, 38–39].
- **Alonso de Ercilla**, poet of *La Araucana*{es}, who approved the book for print [@wilsonCervantesItemEmmanuel1968, 367, 369].
- **Gonzalo de Liaño** (“Gonzalillo”), the king's jester, whose letter of 1585 shows the goose game at court [@infantesPinturaQueSe2010, 134–35].
- **Francesco de' Medici**, Grand Duke of Tuscany, said to have sent the goose game to Philip II [@sevilleImportanceTextPrinted2026, 69].
- **Pietro Carrera**, Sicilian writer on chess, who told that story in 1617 and named Barros's game [@sevilleImportanceTextPrinted2026, 69; @decaceresTableroItalianoFilosofia2009, 85].
- **Mateo Alemán**, author of the novel *Guzmán de Alfarache*{es}, who called Barros his “other self”; **Cristóbal Pérez de Herrera** and **Hernando de Soto**, friends of Barros and writers of his circle [@luceroDedicatoriaProverbiosMorales2019, 103, 109, 116].
- **Pedro, Diego and Rodrigo**, the three players of Barros's worked example in the second edition [@debarrosFilosofiaCortesana1587, pdf 12].
- The printers: **María Ruiz**, “the widow of Alonso Gómez” (first edition, Madrid 1587), **Pedro Madrigal** (second edition, Madrid 1587) and **Iosep Cacchij** (third edition, Naples 1588) [@sanchezEdicionesAntiguasFilosofia2016oct16, 174 n. 17, 176–79].

#### Who's who, now

- **E. M. Wilson** (1968) described the Cambridge copy and summarized the game in English [@wilsonCervantesItemEmmanuel1968, 363–71].
- **Trevor J. Dadson** (1987) edited the second edition, with a facsimile and a conjectural board designed by **Víctor Infantes**, who later wrote on the board and published the 1585 letter (2010) [@infantesPinturaQueSe2010, 128–35].
- **José Martínez Millán** (1996), a historian of the court, placed Barros in a court faction [@millanFilosofiaCortesanaAlonso1996, 463–64; @gitoTodaVidaEs2020, 185].
- **Fernando Collar de Cáceres** (2009) published the Naples board [@decaceresTableroItalianoFilosofia2009, 81–104].
- **Manfred Zollinger** (2010) identified the board independently [@GameGooseLargest].
- **Kelli Wood** (2019) set it among the printed gambling games of Rome [@woodChancingItPrint2019, 453].
- **Ernesto Lucero** (2016–21) showed that the Lisbon copy is a separate edition and argued that it is the first; he edited the book (2019) and has written a series of studies of it [@sanchezEdicionesAntiguasFilosofia2016oct16, 176–77, 179; @sanchezFILOSOFIACORTESANAALONSO, 12–13, 18].
- **Marlen Bidwell-Steiner** (2024) read the game as a lesson in casuistry and as a picture of a court of men [@bidwell-steinerFilosofiaCortesanaAlfonso2024, 195–96, 208].
- **Adrian Seville** (2026) used it to show how a game's book can carry its meaning [@sevilleImportanceTextPrinted2026, 70].

#### Words

- *aposentador*: a royal official who found lodgings for the court; Barros's post [@luceroDEDICATORIAFILOSOFIACORTESANA2019, 34].
- *arenillas*: six dice marked on one face only, which the second edition allows instead of two ordinary dice [@debarrosFilosofiaCortesana1587, pdf 11; @web:https://dle.rae.es/arenilla].
- **board**: the printed sheet the game is played on; for this game, Mario Cartaro's sheet of 1588, the only one known [@sevilleImportanceTextPrinted2026, 70].
- *castellanistas* and *papistas*: the two court factions historians describe under Philip II; Vázquez led the first [@millanFilosofiaCortesanaAlonso1996, 463–64].
- *consulta*: a council's written report to the king on a petition, on which he wrote his answer [@luceroDEDICATORIAFILOSOFIACORTESANA2019, 35–36].
- *criado del Rey*{es}: “the king's servant”, Barros's title on the board [board].
- *desengaño*: disillusion; Mateo Alemán called the book a *desengaño de pretensores*{es}, a disillusioning of office-seekers [@sanchezEdicionesAntiguasFilosofia2016oct16, 170–71].
- **edition**: one printing of a book. This edition calls the three early ones G (Madrid, 1587, the widow of Alonso Gómez; the first), M (Madrid, 1587, Pedro Madrigal; enlarged) and C (Naples, 1588, Iosep Cacchij; issued with Cartaro's board) [@sanchezEdicionesAntiguasFilosofia2016oct16, 177, 179].
- **emblem**: a motto, a picture and a verse, often with a prose commentary, that together teach a lesson; each pictured square of the board works like one [@luceroFilosofiaCortesanaJuego2020, 117].
- **escudo**, **ducat**, **real**, **maravedí**: coins and money of account of the time; the sources we cite do not give their values in 1587 (see “Playing for money”).
- **etching and engraving**: the two techniques used on Cartaro's metal plate, an intaglio plate rather than a woodcut [@woodChancingItPrint2019, 463; @sevilleImportanceTextPrinted2026, 66].
- **the Favorite** (*privado*): one close to power whose favor a petitioner must buy; on the board, square 26, where the petitioner pays for a hearing [@barrosFilosofiaCortesanaMoralizada1588, pdf 34; board]. Bidwell-Steiner places Barros's court at the beginnings of the system of *validos*, royal favorites [@bidwell-steinerFilosofiaCortesanaAlfonso2024, 209–10].
- **Game of the Goose**: a race game from Italy, for two dice, on a spiral of 63 squares, which Barros adapted [@luceroFilosofiaCortesanaJuego2020, 122; @sanchezFILOSOFIACORTESANAALONSO, 37].
- **impression**: one printed copy of a sheet.
- **license** and **privilege**: the royal permission to print and sell a work, granted here for ten years [@luceroTableroJuego15882019, 198].
- **memorial**: a written petition to the king [@luceroDEDICATORIAFILOSOFIACORTESANA2019, 34–35].
- *merced*: a grant made by the king's grace, not a wage owed for service [@luceroIdeaTrabajoFilosofia2019, 823, 829].
- **patron** (*valedor*): the protector who speaks for a petitioner.
- **petitioner** (*pretendiente*): one who seeks office or favor at court; the 1737 dictionary of the Spanish Academy also gives Latin *procus*{la}, a wooer, among its equivalents [@web:https://webfrl.rae.es/DA_DATOS/TOMO_V_HTML/PRETENDIENTE_006558.html].
- **pot** (*polla*): the money in the middle, which the winner takes; **stake** (*tanto*): one counter of the value agreed before play [@debarrosFilosofiaCortesana1587, pdf 11–12].
- *princeps*: the first edition.
- **square**: one of the 63 spaces of the board; Barros calls them *casas*, houses [@barrosFilosofiaCortesanaMoralizada1588, pdf 31].
- *suerte*: a throw of the dice, and a lot in life; Barros plays on both senses.
- *tasa*: the official price set for a book [@sanchezEdicionesAntiguasFilosofia2016oct16, 179, 181].
- **Tacitism**: the reading of the Roman historian Tacitus for lessons in politics; Lucero, after Antonio Álvarez-Ossorio, calls Barros's version a “reason of state of oneself” [@luceroFilosofiaCortesanaJuego2021juin20, 146].

### about-barros
title: Alonso de Barros

#### Alonso de Barros (c. 1540/52 – 1604)

Alonso de Barros came from a Segovian family tied to royal service [@luceroDEDICATORIAFILOSOFIACORTESANA2019, 34]. His father was Diego López de Orozco, an attendant and *aposentador* of Charles V, and his mother Elvira de Barros; Alonso took his mother's surname [@decaceresTableroItalianoFilosofia2009, 82; @luceroIdeaTrabajoFilosofia2019, 828]. He was unmarried and left his estate to a nephew [@decaceresTableroItalianoFilosofia2009, 82, 100 n. 11].

#### Office

In 1563, when his father died, Barros succeeded him as *aposentador*, one of the officials who arranged lodging for the court; Lucero notes that the court's recent settling in Madrid made the office especially necessary [@luceroDEDICATORIAFILOSOFIACORTESANA2019, 34]. Lucero counts more than four decades in the post, from 1563 to 1604 [@sanchezFILOSOFIACORTESANAALONSO, 22]; Wilson, following Pérez Pastor, dates the appointment to 1569 [@wilsonCervantesItemEmmanuel1968, 369]. Before settling in Madrid he served at the taking of the Peñón de Vélez de la Gomera (1564), in Corsica and at the relief of Malta (1565) [@decaceresTableroItalianoFilosofia2009, 82; @wilsonCervantesItemEmmanuel1968, 369].

He asked for more and was often refused. He was proposed in 1582 for a post of *contador* in Guatemala and not appointed; in 1583 the king declined to name him assayer of the Segovia mint, a post left vacant by the death of his brother-in-law [@decaceresTableroItalianoFilosofia2009, 82 and n. 10; @millanFilosofiaCortesanaAlonso1996, 463 n. 9]. His memorials, as Lucero reads them, cite his years of service and his soldiering, not his books [@luceroDEDICATORIAFILOSOFIACORTESANA2019, 34]. In 1587, weeks after the license to print this book, he petitioned for the *escribanía mayor de rentas*{es} of the district of Santo Domingo de Silos, and received it in September [@luceroDEDICATORIAFILOSOFIACORTESANA2019, 35–36]. His petition first named the wrong town, Santo Domingo de la Calzada, and was corrected between the lines; Lucero allows that the slip may be merely anecdotal [@luceroDEDICATORIAFILOSOFIACORTESANA2019, 36]. Lucero sees Mateo Vázquez behind the grant but does not claim that the book alone won it [@luceroDEDICATORIAFILOSOFIACORTESANA2019, 35–36]. Martínez Millán dated the petition to October 1590 and the grant after it [@millanFilosofiaCortesanaAlonso1996, 463].

#### Patrons

Barros dedicated the *Filosofía cortesana*{es} to Mateo Vázquez de Leca, Philip II's secretary and the leader of the court faction historians call *castellanista* [@luceroDEDICATORIAFILOSOFIACORTESANA2019, 37, 42; @millanFilosofiaCortesanaAlonso1996, 463–64]. In the second edition, Madrigal's, Vázquez's coat of arms stood on the title page; the Naples edition dropped it [@luceroDEDICATORIAFILOSOFIACORTESANA2019, 38–40; @sanchezEdicionesAntiguasFilosofia2016oct16, 180]. Lucero argues that the patronage ran both ways. From the early 1580s Vázquez had been turning his power into social standing, with a claimed descent from the Lecca of Corsica and the Colonna that his rivals attacked, and by about 1585, Lucero writes, his power was waning; the arms on the second edition belong to that campaign [@luceroDEDICATORIAFILOSOFIACORTESANA2019, 37 and n. 13, 40, 42, 49]. Lucero points out the paradox: the arms speak for nobility of blood, on the title page of a book whose board praises work [@luceroDEDICATORIAFILOSOFIACORTESANA2019, 49; @ramirezAlonsoBarrosFilosofia2020, pdf 2]. Vázquez signed himself “de Lecca y Colonna” from 1583, and Lucero thinks these Italian ambitions help explain why the book was reprinted in Naples [@luceroDEDICATORIAFILOSOFIACORTESANA2019, 48–49]. Bidwell-Steiner goes further: she calls Vázquez an Andalusian {q|bidwell-steinerFilosofiaCortesanaAlfonso2024|pdf16|210|de ascendencia pobre y muy probablemente judía} (of poor and very probably Jewish ancestry) and an archetypal social climber [@bidwell-steinerFilosofiaCortesanaAlfonso2024, 210]. That is her statement; the other sources we cite do not discuss his ancestry beyond the attacks of his rivals on his origins, which Lucero records [@luceroDEDICATORIAFILOSOFIACORTESANA2019, 37 n. 13]. Vázquez died in 1591 [@luceroDedicatoriaProverbiosMorales2019, 109 n. 21]. In 1598 Barros dedicated his second book to García de Loaysa Girón, tutor of the future Philip III, who became archbishop of Toledo that summer, lost his court offices within days of the new reign and died in February 1599 [@luceroDedicatoriaProverbiosMorales2019, 104, 111–12]. When Barros asked leave, in 1600, to pass his post of *aposentador* to Diego López de Burgos, a man not of his family, the king refused: {q|luceroDedicatoriaProverbiosMorales2019|pdf19|119|No conviene abrir puerta a esto cuando no es de padre a hijo, yerno o hermano} (it is not fitting to open the door to this unless it passes from father to son, son-in-law or brother) [@luceroDedicatoriaProverbiosMorales2019, 119 and n. 48]. Lucero reads Loaysa's death, which cost Barros the intercessor he had counted on, through Barros's own board: the Death of the Patron sends the player back to the start [@luceroDedicatoriaProverbiosMorales2019, 119–20].

#### Writings

Barros published two books of his own: the *Filosofía cortesana*{es} (1587) and the *Proverbios morales*{es} (Madrid: Luis Sánchez, 1598) [@luceroDEDICATORIAFILOSOFIACORTESANA2019, 34]. The *Proverbios* hold 1,063 proverbs in octosyllabic verse [@luceroDedicatoriaProverbiosMorales2019, 102] and were reprinted many times, also as *Perla de proverbios morales*{es} and *Heráclito de Alonso de Barros*{es}, and in French [@decaceresTableroItalianoFilosofia2009, 82, 100 n. 12; @wilsonCervantesItemEmmanuel1968, 363]. Their opening lines make a game the figure of life itself: {q|decaceresTableroItalianoFilosofia2009|pdf4|84|Aunque, si yo no me engaño, todos jugamos un juego} (although, unless I am mistaken, we all play one game) [@decaceresTableroItalianoFilosofia2009, 84; @gitoTodaVidaEs2020, 185]. From the early seventeenth century the two books were confused. Pérez Pastor gave two arguments for separating them; for Wilson the question was settled when Schevill and Bonilla printed Cervantes's sonnet from the Naples copy in 1922; and some writers still confused them in the twentieth century and after [@wilsonCervantesItemEmmanuel1968, 364–65; @sanchezEdicionesAntiguasFilosofia2016oct16, 169–72; @millanFilosofiaCortesanaAlonso1996, 465 n. 17]. Barros also wrote a printed *Memorial sobre el reparo de la milicia* on veterans' relief, and a short manuscript *Discurso y difinición del privado*, which treats the favor of a lesser person with a greater as a form of friendship [@decaceresTableroItalianoFilosofia2009, 82, 84; @luceroDEDICATORIAFILOSOFIACORTESANA2019, 40, 42]. He wrote paratexts for friends: the *Elogio* of the author in Mateo Alemán's *Guzmán de Alfarache*{es} (1599), a letter in Cristóbal Pérez de Herrera's *Amparo de pobres*{es} (1598) and praise for Hernando de Soto's *Emblemas moralizadas*{es} (1599) [@sanchezFILOSOFIACORTESANAALONSO, 22; @decaceresTableroItalianoFilosofia2009, 82].

#### Friends

Alemán called Barros his {q|luceroDedicatoriaProverbiosMorales2019|pdf16|116|otro yo} (other self) in his prologue to the *Proverbios* [@luceroDedicatoriaProverbiosMorales2019, 116]. With Alemán and above all Pérez de Herrera, Barros took part in the reform of poor relief in the Madrid parish of San Martín [@luceroDedicatoriaProverbiosMorales2019, 109]. Soto, a *contador* and emblem writer, exchanged praise with him; Lope de Vega contributed a poem to the *Proverbios* [@luceroDedicatoriaProverbiosMorales2019, 103, 116]. Collar de Cáceres calls him a court bureaucrat with literary interests, close to Soto and Alemán [@decaceresTableroItalianoFilosofia2009, 82]. At his death his library held 151 volumes, studied by Trevor Dadson [@decaceresTableroItalianoFilosofia2009, 83; @ref:dadson1987lib].

#### What the sources do not settle

- **Birth.** Nicolás Antonio gives Segovia, c. 1552 [@wilsonCervantesItemEmmanuel1968, 363]. Collar de Cáceres gives c. 1542, after Dadson and Cavillac [@decaceresTableroItalianoFilosofia2009, 82, 99 n. 7]; Wilson had argued from his service in 1564–65 that he was born before 1552 [@wilsonCervantesItemEmmanuel1968, 369]. Dadson proposed 1540 or 1545 [@sanchezEdicionesAntiguasFilosofia2016oct16, 175 n. 23]; Díaz Gito writes c. 1541–45 [@gitoTodaVidaEs2020, 184].
- **Death.** Collar de Cáceres gives the night of 17 August 1604, in Madrid, with the will opened on 18 August [@decaceresTableroItalianoFilosofia2009, 82, 100 n. 11]; Wilson, after Pérez Pastor, gives 18 August 1604 [@wilsonCervantesItemEmmanuel1968, 369].
- **The Silos office:** 1587 (Lucero, Collar de Cáceres) or 1590 (Martínez Millán), as above.
- **The assayership of 1583.** Martínez Millán writes that Barros sought the Segovia mint's assayership for his widowed sister; Collar de Cáceres calls that inexact and has him seek it for himself, as does Lucero [@millanFilosofiaCortesanaAlonso1996, 463; @decaceresTableroItalianoFilosofia2009, 82, 100 n. 11; @luceroDEDICATORIAFILOSOFIACORTESANA2019, 36 n. 10].
- **Authorship.** Martínez Millán and José Luis Gonzalo Sánchez-Molero suggested the work came out of Vázquez's circle; Lucero holds Barros's authorship beyond doubt while allowing that friends may have read the text [@luceroDEDICATORIAFILOSOFIACORTESANA2019, 41].

### about-court
title: The court of Philip II and its petitioners

#### A court that stayed put

Philip II settled his court in Madrid in 1561 [@web:https://www.redalyc.org/pdf/7370/737080494002.pdf, 2–3] and made the Alcázar his principal residence [@web:https://www.museodelprado.es/en/learn/encyclopedia/voice/alcazar-de-madrid-real/cd2eb3b7-3aa7-45f9-9ce5-0ed711622f08]. A fixed court drew people who wanted something from the king, because the king was the source of *gracia* (grace, favor) [@millanFilosofiaCortesanaAlonso1996, 465–66]. Collar de Cáceres, after Geoffrey Parker, names two of the courtiers' obsessions, boredom and promotion [@decaceresTableroItalianoFilosofia2009, 82; @ref:parker1979].

#### Pretendientes and mercedes

A *pretendiente* (petitioner, office-seeker) was, in the 1737 dictionary of the Spanish Academy, one who seeks or solicits; the laws it cites required court petitioners to register their presence [@web:https://webfrl.rae.es/DA_DATOS/TOMO_V_HTML/PRETENDIENTE_006558.html]. The Latin equivalents it gives, *Petitor. Candidatus. Procus.*{la}, span the word's range: a claimant, a candidate for office, and a wooer, so the same word could name someone courting a lady and someone courting the king [@web:https://webfrl.rae.es/DA_DATOS/TOMO_V_HTML/PRETENDIENTE_006558.html]. English “suitor” carries the same double sense, but most readers now hear only the second; this edition says “petitioner”, which keeps the asking and loses the courtship. What they sought was a *merced*, a grant; for Barros it was most often a royal office [@luceroIdeaTrabajoFilosofia2019, 823; @luceroDedicatoriaProverbiosMorales2019, 103]. Lucero stresses that a *merced* was a gift of grace, not a wage owed for service, and that it was always scarce [@luceroIdeaTrabajoFilosofia2019, 823, 829]. By 1593 the deceived petitioner was a stock figure of satire, as in Góngora's letrilla “Un buhonero ha empleado” [@cansecoLUCEROErnestoEd2021, 430].

Petitions reached the king in writing. A petitioner sent a *memorial*, a written petition; the king's councils put the matter to him in a *consulta*, a written report; and the king wrote his answer on the papers [@luceroDEDICATORIAFILOSOFIACORTESANA2019, 35–36 and nn. 6–7]. Barros's own file shows the procedure. In March 1587 he petitioned for a revenue office in the district of Santo Domingo de Silos, vacant since its holder's death in 1576. The king asked why it had been vacant so long, whether he had been consulted on it before, and whether others wanted it. The answer, a *consulta* of 10 September, was that nobody had asked for it until Barros did, but that a memorial had since come in from the *contador* Juan de la Peña Zorrilla, who asked for the post after more than twenty-five years of service. Barros had the post, with a salary of 20,021 maravedís. Lucero thinks that Peña Zorrilla, whom Barros probably knew as a fellow Segovian, in the end tipped the balance in Barros's favor, and sees the hand of Mateo Vázquez behind it [@luceroDEDICATORIAFILOSOFIACORTESANA2019, 35–36].

#### Secretaries and favorites

Mateo Vázquez de Leca, the dedicatee, was secretary to Philip II from 1573 until his death in 1591 [@wilsonCervantesItemEmmanuel1968, 365, 367; @luceroDedicatoriaProverbiosMorales2019, 109 n. 21]. Lucero presents him as the model of the *letrado*, the official who rose by learning and effort rather than by birth, and as the patron of the *castellanista* party, the court faction described below [@luceroDEDICATORIAFILOSOFIACORTESANA2019, 37, 42, 49]. From the early 1580s he sought social standing to match his power, with a claimed descent from the Lecca of Corsica and the Colonna that rivals mocked; his power waned from about 1585, Lucero writes, and still more with the creation of the committee of ministers called the Junta de Noche, which the sources we cite do not date [@luceroDEDICATORIAFILOSOFIACORTESANA2019, 37].

Bidwell-Steiner places Barros's court at the start of the system of *validos*, royal favorites who became, in her phrase, “potent masters of intrigues” (our translation) [@bidwell-steinerFilosofiaCortesanaAlfonso2024, 209–10]. On the board the Favorite is square 26, where the petitioner pays to be heard [board] [@decaceresTableroItalianoFilosofia2009, 94]. Barros's manuscript on the *privado* says: “I think that the favor the lesser wins with the greater is almost the same thing as the friendship struck between equals” (our translation); Collar de Cáceres finds this cynical [@luceroDEDICATORIAFILOSOFIACORTESANA2019, 42; @decaceresTableroItalianoFilosofia2009, 84].

#### Factions

Martínez Millán describes two groupings at Philip II's court. The *castellanistas*, formed after Cardinal Espinosa's death in 1572 under Vázquez's patronage, favored an intransigent religious policy and Castilians in the central offices; they opposed the *papistas*, the grouping around don Juan de Austria, Antonio Pérez and the princess of Éboli [@millanFilosofiaCortesanaAlonso1996, 463–64]. Bidwell-Steiner counts the contest between these two factions as the most famous intrigue of the reign; on her account it ended with don Juan's envoy murdered in Madrid and Antonio Pérez ruined [@bidwell-steinerFilosofiaCortesanaAlfonso2024, 210]. Wilson writes that Vázquez probably helped to bring about Pérez's disgrace, and Bidwell-Steiner that the plot was hatched by a group of *castellanistas* with Vázquez at their head [@wilsonCervantesItemEmmanuel1968, 365; @bidwell-steinerFilosofiaCortesanaAlfonso2024, 210]. The sources we cite do not date these events; [square 43, the Change of Ministers](atlas.html#43), returns to them. On Martínez Millán's account the *castellanistas* peaked from 1578, gained with the union with Portugal in 1580 and declined in 1586–90 [@millanFilosofiaCortesanaAlonso1996, 464]. Martínez Millán assigned Barros to them from his two dedicatees [@millanFilosofiaCortesanaAlonso1996, 463]. Lucero accepts this and reads the board's praise of labor as the faction's ideology [@luceroFilosofiaCortesanaJuego2020, 118], though he notes that Cervantes and Liñán in the second edition's preliminaries show the factions were not sealed [@luceroDEDICATORIAFILOSOFIACORTESANA2019, 39]. Bidwell-Steiner prefers a broader explanation, a change in codes of court sociability, to a factional one [@bidwell-steinerFilosofiaCortesanaAlfonso2024, 195–96].

Barros's squares map onto this world. The Change of Ministers (43) sends the petitioner back to Flattery (10) to court the new minister [@luceroFilosofiaCortesanaJuego2020, 125]. The Death of the Patron (46) sends the petitioner back to the start [@luceroFilosofiaCortesanaJuego2021juin20, 145]. Lucero reads both as a warning against relying on a single patron [@luceroFilosofiaCortesanaJuego2021juin20, 145].

#### Women and the court game

Bidwell-Steiner points out what is missing from Barros's court: “all the pleasurable leisure of earlier court literature, such as theater, music, artful conversation and, above all, women” (our translation). In Luis Milán's *El Cortesano*{es} (1561) talk between ladies and gentlemen still counted as part of court life; Barros's court is a world of men competing with men. She links the absence to the misogyny of anti-court writing, and to Mateo Vázquez's austerity, set against the notorious libertinism of his adversary Antonio Pérez [@bidwell-steinerFilosofiaCortesanaAlfonso2024, 208–11]. Women were nonetheless among the first players of the goose game at the Spanish court. In 1585 the infanta was one of those who won forty escudos from the king's jester, and Philip II wrote to his daughter Catalina that her sister had become a gambler at a new game [@infantesPinturaQueSe2010, 134 n. 22, 135; @woodChancingItPrint2019, 470]. The sources we cite do not name the infanta of the jester's letter. The first edition of the book itself was printed by a woman: María Ruiz, who ran her late husband's shop as “the widow of Alonso Gómez” and was royal printer until her death in 1595 [@sanchezEdicionesAntiguasFilosofia2016oct16, 174 and n. 17, 182]. And the word *pretendiente*, as the 1737 dictionary shows, could also name a wooer (see above).

#### A comparative frame: Biagioli's patronage economy

Mario Biagioli's study of Galileo at the Medici and papal courts (1993) gives a vocabulary for the same system, though his evidence is Florentine and Roman and a generation later than Barros. For Biagioli {q|biagioliGalileoCourtierPractice1993|pdf19|4|patronage was an institution without walls} [@biagioliGalileoCourtierPractice1993, 4]; opting out of it was social suicide [@biagioliGalileoCourtierPractice1993, 16]. Careers ran in jumps, broken by crises that usually followed a patron's death [@biagioliGalileoCourtierPractice1993, 30, 34]. A prince's reward was not a debt: {q|biagioliGalileoCourtierPractice1993|pdf66|51|for a courtier to receive a reward from his prince was structurally analogous to a Christian's receiving grace from God} [@biagioliGalileoCourtierPractice1993, 51]. He quotes Matteo Pellegrini's treatise on the court (1624): “it is in the royal interest to keep everybody suspended between fear and hope” [@biagioliGalileoCourtierPractice1993, 20]. Biagioli cites Barros's game itself, at second hand from Parker, as a court game in which a patron's death sent the player back to the start [@biagioliGalileoCourtierPractice1993, 35; @ref:parker1979].

The parallel is ours to draw, not Barros's: Lucero's reading of the dice as the patron's will [@sanchezFILOSOFIACORTESANAALONSO, 26, 45] and Biagioli's reading of reward as grace describe the same structure, in which effort is necessary but not enough.

### about-kind
title: What kind of book?

#### Between the ideal courtier and the disillusioned one

Barros's book joined a crowded shelf. Manuel Antonio Díaz Gito, reviewing Lucero's edition, frames the book between two kinds of court writing [@gitoTodaVidaEs2020, 184–85]. One is Baldassare Castiglione's *Courtier* (1528), read in Spanish from 1534 in Juan Boscán's version, whose new courtier followed the model of the perfect prince and orator, {q|gitoTodaVidaEs2020|pdf2|184|pertrechado ahora con las mejores armas del saber humanístico y la virtud como guía} (equipped now with the best weapons of humanist learning, with virtue as guide), as Díaz Gito puts it [@gitoTodaVidaEs2020, 184]. The other is satire. Antonio de Guevara's *Menosprecio de corte y alabanza de aldea*{es} (1539) was perhaps, for Martínez Millán, the first book to set out the social rules of the court, and many others on the theme followed it [@millanFilosofiaCortesanaAlonso1996, 467]; in such books, Díaz Gito writes, the court is painted “as a temple of Fortune, a well of vanity, corruption and vice” (our translation) [@gitoTodaVidaEs2020, 184–85]. One of them, Cristóbal de Castillejo, wrote a dialogue on the court, *Aula de cortesanos*{es}, whose lines on the competitors for office, {q|luceroFilosofiaCortesanaJuego2021juin20|pdf10|141|donde tantos ha de haber / hambrientos competidores} (where there must be so many hungry competitors), Lucero quotes beside Barros [@luceroFilosofiaCortesanaJuego2021juin20, 140–41 and n. 7].

Where the *Filosofía cortesana*{es} stands between the two is a question its readers answer differently. Díaz Gito places it between the courtesy book and the satire [@gitoTodaVidaEs2020, 185]. Lucero, in his thesis, places it among books of *avisos*, practical warnings: it “shares numerous commonplaces with anti-court literature”, he writes, but not its “exclusively negative overall conception of the Court and its moral condemnation” (our translation) [@sanchezFILOSOFIACORTESANAALONSO, 27]. For him it marks a turning point on the road from Castiglione's *Courtier* to Baltasar Gracián's *Discreto*: “a turning point at which the courtier's conduct looks for the first time to his interest alone”, in words he takes from Martínez Millán (our translation) [@sanchezFILOSOFIACORTESANAALONSO, 28, 43]. Luis Gómez Canseco, reviewing Lucero's edition, describes the same shift: “the perfect courtier saw himself turned into a petitioner, the ideal of life bound up with chivalry gave way to the eagerness to rise, political prudence directed to the good was replaced by discretion, which looked to one's own benefit. In the background stood Machiavelli, wrapped in a convenient, Christianized Tacitus” (our translation) [@cansecoLUCEROErnestoEd2021, 432–33].

#### Discretion, Fortune and Tacitus

Lucero's reading turns on two words. Prudence, a virtue, chooses only means that serve a good end; discretion, as the sixteenth-century writer Damasio de Frías defined it, handles the means to bad ends as well as good. It is discretion, not prudence, that Barros teaches [@luceroFilosofiaCortesanaJuego2021juin20, 140–41]. The means his book names are liberality, flattery, diligence and labor, and Lucero notes that Barros sets out dissimulation and simulation openly as ways to rise at court [@luceroFilosofiaCortesanaJuego2021juin20, 146 and n. 21]. He counts Barros among the second generation of Spanish Tacitists, readers of the Roman historian Tacitus, whose political thought he describes, after Antonio Álvarez-Ossorio, as a {q|luceroFilosofiaCortesanaJuego2021juin20|pdf15|146|razón de Estado de uno mismo} (reason of state of oneself), joined to a Senecan acceptance of failure at court [@luceroFilosofiaCortesanaJuego2021juin20, 146]. Lucero credits Martínez Millán as the first to set the book among court writings, emblems and Tacitist thought [@luceroFilosofiaCortesanaJuego2021juin20, 146 n. 21]. Martínez Millán read it through the older literature of Fortune, which he traces to fifteenth-century Castile, and through the Tacitism and Neostoicism of Justus Lipsius [@millanFilosofiaCortesanaAlonso1996, 466–69].

The tone is disputed too. Gómez Canseco hears under the moral {q|cansecoLUCEROErnestoEd2021|pdf5|433|una profunda y amarga sátira sobre la pretensión como modo de vida} (a deep and bitter satire on petitioning as a way of life) [@cansecoLUCEROErnestoEd2021, 433]. Díaz Gito finds a pessimistic view of the petitioner's life and a resigned pragmatism [@gitoTodaVidaEs2020, 184–85]. Marcos Méndez Filesi, against both, judged that, set beside the fatalism of Guevara and Castillejo, {q|GameGooseLargest|pdf0||Barros propone una filosofía algo más optimista} (Barros proposes a somewhat more optimistic philosophy), since labor can bring the petitioner to the palm [@GameGooseLargest]. Mateo Alemán's name for the book, *desengaño de pretensores*{es}, the disillusioning of office-seekers, is the one most often repeated [@sanchezEdicionesAntiguasFilosofia2016oct16, 170–71]. Lucero himself calls it, in one article, a work of *institutio*, of instruction, that teaches while it entertains [@luceroIdeaTrabajoFilosofia2019, 828]; in his thesis he writes that its form rules out reading it as *institutio* in the line of the *Courtier* [@sanchezFILOSOFIACORTESANAALONSO, 27].

#### Pictures that teach: the emblem

An emblem joined three parts: a motto, a picture and a verse, often followed by a prose commentary. The picture was a lesson, but also a puzzle the reader had to work out [@luceroFilosofiaCortesanaJuego2020, 117; @luceroFilosofiaCortesanaJuego2021juin20, 135, 137]. On Barros's board, Lucero shows, the square's name is the motto, the figure is the picture, and the couplets beneath it, with the prose in the book, complete the explanation [@luceroFilosofiaCortesanaJuego2020, 117]. The form was well known in Spain: Bernardino Daza's Spanish verse translation of Andrea Alciato's emblems had appeared in 1549 [@luceroIdeaTrabajoFilosofia2019, 824 n. 9]. Yet when Barros published, Lucero notes, only one emblem book by a Spanish author had appeared, Juan de Borja's *Empresas morales*{es}, and it had been printed in Prague; Juan de Horozco's *Emblemas morales*{es} followed in Segovia in 1589 [@luceroFilosofiaCortesanaJuego2021juin20, 135, 137]. Lucero therefore counts the *Filosofía cortesana*{es} among the first Spanish emblem collections, and a rare one devoted to a single subject [@luceroFilosofiaCortesanaJuego2020, 117; @sanchezFILOSOFIACORTESANAALONSO, 28]. Borja, as Martínez Sobrino and García Román observe, stripped the emblem of play to give it more moral, political and spiritual weight; Lucero points out that Barros did the opposite [@luceroFilosofiaCortesanaJuego2021juin20, 137 n. 2]. Lucero adds, in his own image, that it is “an emblem book that opens a seam along which the genre will run no further” (our translation) [@luceroIdeaTrabajoFilosofia2019, 828].

Emblems were also a courtly pastime in their own right. Lucero, after José Antonio Maravall, writes that {q|luceroIdeaTrabajoFilosofia2019|pdf4|824|Emblemas, enigmas, jeroglíficos y acertijos ponen el ingenio al servicio de la recreación del cortesano y de su aprendizaje por medio de imágenes, cauce que permite fijar con sencillez los conceptos más complejos a la memoria} (emblems, enigmas, hieroglyphs and riddles put wit at the service of the courtier's recreation and learning through images, a channel that makes it simple to fix the most complex ideas in the memory) [@luceroIdeaTrabajoFilosofia2019, 824 n. 9], and Mario Biagioli, writing about the Medici court, notes after Castiglione and others that {q|biagioliGalileoCourtierPractice1993|pdf126|111|skill in emblematics was a tool required of those who wanted to engage in courtly life} [@biagioliGalileoCourtierPractice1993, 111]. Until the Naples board was found, the book's pictures were known only from their verses and Barros's prose; Víctor Infantes wrote about it under the title “La presencia de una ausencia. La Emblemática sin emblemas” (the presence of an absence: emblematics without emblems) [@ref:infantes1996; @infantesPinturaQueSe2010, 129–30 and n. 12].

### about-goose
title: The Game of the Goose

#### Origins

Seville calls the game's beginnings “obscure” [@sevilleImportanceTextPrinted2026, 64]. The oldest notices he gathers come from Borso d'Este's Ferrara: a statute of 1463 for Argenta that forbids dice games, the goose among them, and a gift of two gold ducats in July 1465 to Borso's jester, Giovan Battista Scocola, “to play at the goose” (Seville's translation) [@sevilleImportanceTextPrinted2026, 64]. By then it was a gambling game, but Seville argues from its numbers, the 63 squares (the Grand Climacteric) and the geese set nine apart, that {q|sevilleImportanceTextPrinted2026|pdf3|64|originally it was conceived as a representation of the spiritual progress of a human life} [@sevilleImportanceTextPrinted2026, 64–65]. Seville knows no board from those decades: the earliest that survive, he writes, {q|sevilleImportanceTextPrinted2026|pdf3|64|are perhaps a century later: they are of wood, elaborately inlaid, and probably intended as diplomatic princely gifts} [@sevilleImportanceTextPrinted2026, 64]; Lucero names a reversible wooden chess-and-goose board in the Metropolitan Museum of Art [@luceroFilosofiaCortesanaJuego2020, 120].

#### From Florence to Madrid

Pietro Carrera, *Il gioco de gli scacchi* (Militello, 1617), wrote that the game was invented in Florence and that Grand Duke Francesco de' Medici sent it to Philip II [@sevilleImportanceTextPrinted2026, 69]. His words, as the page appears in a photograph posted to the Tarot History Forum in 2013 (our transcription; long s given as s) [@TarotHistoryForum; @ref:carrera1617, 25]:

> *E chiaro che gli huomini spiritosi dopo la prima inuentione della cosa sù l'istesso fondamento aggiungendo, ò mutando, ritrouano altre inuentioni, come è noto essere auuenuto al ritrouamento del gioco dell'Oca ne' tempi de' nostri padri, perchè questo gioco essendosi ritrouato in Firenze, e piacendo sommamente parue à Francesco di Medici grã Duca di Toscana di mandarlo alla Maestà del Rè Filippo II. in Ispagna, oue publicato diè materia à' buoni ingegni di ritrouarne altri poco differenti dal primo, fra' quali vi è il gioco detto la Filosofia Corteggiana ritrouato da Alonso di Barros spagnuolo.*{it}

(It is clear that men of wit, after the first invention of a thing, find other inventions upon the same foundation by adding or changing, as is known to have happened with the finding of the Game of the Goose in our fathers' time: for this game having been devised in Florence, and pleasing greatly, Francesco de' Medici, Grand Duke of Tuscany, thought fit to send it to His Majesty King Philip II in Spain, where, once published, it gave good wits the matter to devise others little different from the first, among which is the game called the *Filosofia Corteggiana*{it}, devised by Alonso de Barros, a Spaniard.) Carrera gives no date for the gift (see the notes below). Domini observed that it was uncorroborated [@decaceresTableroItalianoFilosofia2009, 101 n. 36].

Seville and Wood both point to letters of 1585 that bear out Carrera's account of the gift [@sevilleImportanceTextPrinted2026, 69; @woodChancingItPrint2019, 470]. On 24 August 1585, at Monzón, Gonzalo de Liaño (“Gonzalillo”), the king's jester, wrote to Francesco I cursing a servant of Luis Dovara, the Medici agent at the Spanish court, for bringing a game of the goose played with two dice; the prince, the infanta and Luis Tristán had won forty escudos from him, and the king enjoyed seeing him stung [@infantesPinturaQueSe2010, 134–35]. In the same letter he writes that his wife is dying of fever and that {q|infantesPinturaQueSe2010|pdf11|135|el rey nunca me ha querido dexar partir} (the king has never been willing to let me leave) [@infantesPinturaQueSe2010, 135]: a court servant could not go home without the king's leave. The letter was found by Nieves Baranda [@infantesPinturaQueSe2010, 134]. A day before, Philip II had written to his daughter Catalina that her sister had become a gambler at a new game brought by Gonzalillo: {q|infantesPinturaQueSe2010|pdf10|134|vuestra hermana se hace tahúr de un nuevo juego que ha traído Gonzalillo} (your sister is turning gambler at a new game that Gonzalillo has brought) [@infantesPinturaQueSe2010, 134 n. 22; @woodChancingItPrint2019, 470, 481 n. 69]. Wood takes the new game to be the goose game; Lucero calls the king's letter indirect evidence [@woodChancingItPrint2019, 470; @sanchezEdicionesAntiguasFilosofia2016oct16, 177 n. 36].

#### The standard game

Lucero reconstructs the rules then current [@luceroFilosofiaCortesanaJuego2020, 122–24]. Players race along a spiral of 63 squares by the throw of two dice [@luceroFilosofiaCortesanaJuego2020, 122; @woodChancingItPrint2019, 458]. A goose doubles the throw; the geese run in two series of nines, from 5 and from 9 [@luceroFilosofiaCortesanaJuego2020, 122]. A first throw of 9 goes at once to one of the two dice squares, 26 or 53, so that nobody can run the geese to victory on the first throw [@luceroFilosofiaCortesanaJuego2020, 122–23]. There are hazards: the Bridge (6), Inn (19), Well (31), Labyrinth (42), Prison (52) and Death (58), which sends the player back to the start [@decaceresTableroItalianoFilosofia2009, 102 n. 68; @woodChancingItPrint2019, 458]. Many hazards demand a payment to the pot [@sevilleImportanceTextPrinted2026, 66]. A player who lands on an occupied square displaces its occupant [@luceroFilosofiaCortesanaJuego2020, 122]. The last square must be reached exactly; an overthrow counts back [@woodChancingItPrint2019, 458].

#### What Barros kept and what he changed

Lucero lists what Barros kept: the 63 squares on a spiral, the rebound, the ban on two players sharing a square, mechanical squares, and payments to the pot or to other players [@luceroFilosofiaCortesanaJuego2020, 125].

What changed [@luceroFilosofiaCortesanaJuego2020, 124–25]:
- the geese became oxen, the squares of Labor, at 4, 12, 17, 23, 30, 34, 41, 48 and 57, in no numerical pattern; Barros says their placing keeps no order, because the matter has none;
- Death became the Death of the Patron and moved to 46;
- the Dice stand at 28 and 53, not 26 and 53, without the first-throw rule;
- Fortune (51) gives a second throw, so that a double six from 51 reaches 63.

Barros also dropped the Prison and the Labyrinth [@luceroFilosofiaCortesanaJuego2021juin20, 144 n. 15]. His Well of Oblivion costs a lost turn and payments, where the goose-game Well holds a player until another falls in [@decaceresTableroItalianoFilosofia2009, 95]. He did not use the goose rule of jumping from one dice square to the other [@decaceresTableroItalianoFilosofia2009, 95]. Lucero thinks Barros knew the goose game only partly, and emptied its numbering of symbolic sense {q|luceroFilosofiaCortesanaJuego2020|pdf10|126|por puro desconocimiento, a buen seguro} (out of plain ignorance, surely) [@luceroFilosofiaCortesanaJuego2020, 121, 126]; in the sequel he hedges: {q|luceroFilosofiaCortesanaJuego2021juin20|pdf4|135|probablemente por desconocimiento o quizá por interés} (probably out of ignorance, or perhaps by design) [@luceroFilosofiaCortesanaJuego2021juin20, 135].

#### Later themed geese

Carrera said that the game's publication in Spain led others to invent variants {q|sevilleImportanceTextPrinted2026|pdf8|69|a little different from the original, among which was the game known under the name of the Filosofia cortesana invented by Alonso de Barros of Spain} [@sevilleImportanceTextPrinted2026, 69]. Seville places Barros's game first among early themed variants; his next example is the *Le Ieu Royal de Cupidon*{fr} (Paris, c. 1640) [@sevilleImportanceTextPrinted2026, 68, 73]. In Rome, Altiero Gatti's *Gioco della scimia*{it} (1588) replaced the goose with a monkey; Wood reads it as a parody of Barros's board [@woodChancingItPrint2019, 475]. Wood gives Lucchino Gargano's sheet (Rome, 1598) as the earliest surviving one titled a Game of the Goose [@woodChancingItPrint2019, 454]. Thematic geese later multiplied; Collar de Cáceres cites d'Allemagne's ten groups and boards of up to 300 squares [@decaceresTableroItalianoFilosofia2009, 86, 102 n. 55].

#### Across Europe

Manfred Zollinger dates the spread: “After 1585, the Bolognese scholar Ulisse Aldrovandi gives the rules of this game, which is already fairly widespread in Italy and even has regional variants. From that time a remarkable diffusion can be seen: outside Italy, the game appears in Spain (1587), in Bavaria (1589), in England (1597) and in France (1597 or 1598)” (our translation) [@GameGooseLargest; @ref:zollinger2010]. Aldrovandi, Wood adds, called it a game of pure chance, invented for pleasure and play [@woodChancingItPrint2019, 461]. Collar de Cáceres lists early witnesses: an entry of 16 June 1597 in the register of the Stationers' Company in London, to John Wolfe, for “the new and most pleasant game of the goosse”; a board printed in Lyon c. 1597–1601 by the heirs of Benoît Rigaud; a printed catalog of the Roman print-sellers Michelangelo and Andrea Vaccari of 1614; and the note of Jean Héroard, physician of the young Louis XIII, that on 26 April 1612 the boy played at the goose [@decaceresTableroItalianoFilosofia2009, 85]. In Spain, Lucero sets Barros's game far earlier than the Balearic goose board from the Guasp press, dated c. 1652 [@sanchezFILOSOFIACORTESANAALONSO, 37].

#### Myths of origin

Readers who look for the game's origins will meet many stories: the Greek hero Palamedes, the Phaistos disc, a Chinese game of the Ming dynasty known as the promotion of the mandarins, a spiritual map of the road to Santiago linked to the Templars. Lucero lists them and sets them aside as outside his subject [@luceroFilosofiaCortesanaJuego2020, 119 and n. 4]. He does reject one modern claim, that Barros called the game the invention of a heretical Protestant sect: Barros never said so [@luceroFilosofiaCortesanaJuego2020, 119 n. 4]. Collar de Cáceres does not accept that four red panels on a table in Pieter Bruegel the Elder's *Triumph of Death* (c. 1568) can be shown to be the game [@decaceresTableroItalianoFilosofia2009, 85].

#### Notes on the sources

- Carrera's date for the gift: Collar de Cáceres gives c. 1576, and Infantes, reporting the same passage, c. 1567; Lucero, after another report of it, gives c. 1580 [@decaceresTableroItalianoFilosofia2009, 85; @infantesPinturaQueSe2010, 134; @sanchezEdicionesAntiguasFilosofia2016oct16, 177 n. 36]. The page as transcribed above says only “in our fathers' time”.
- Wood's text says the royal children lost the forty escudos, but the Spanish she quotes says Liaño lost them [@woodChancingItPrint2019, 470, 481].
- Bidwell-Steiner calls Liaño a *portero de Cámara*{es}, a doorkeeper of the king's chamber [@bidwell-steinerFilosofiaCortesanaAlfonso2024, 196].

### about-editions
title: The three editions

#### Three editions in a year

Three early editions are known, each in a single copy [@sanchezEdicionesAntiguasFilosofia2016oct16, 177]. Lucero Sánchez showed in 2016 that the Madrid edition of the widow of Alonso Gómez is the first and that Madrigal's is an expanded second edition in which Barros took part or consented [@sanchezEdicionesAntiguasFilosofia2016oct16, 169, 179, 191]. Among his reasons: G's first gathering, signed with pilcrows, was printed after the text, as the law of 1558 required of preliminaries, and its title page leaves the price blank because the official price (*tasa*) had not arrived in time; M prints the price on its title page [@sanchezEdicionesAntiguasFilosofia2016oct16, 179, 181]. C follows M in its preliminaries and text, but in the closing rules it follows G [@sanchezEdicionesAntiguasFilosofia2016oct16, 180 and n. 42].

#### Ghosts

The story of the first edition's disappearance and recovery is Lucero Sánchez's [@sanchezEdicionesAntiguasFilosofia2016oct16, 174–77], and we follow it. Tamayo de Vargas and Nicolás Antonio recorded an edition of 1587 printed by Alonso Gómez (Tamayo: “Madrid por Alonso Gomez 1587”); because Gómez had died in 1584, later bibliographers doubted it, and Ticknor's translators printed the date as 1567, a misprint that lived on in the bibliographies [@sanchezEdicionesAntiguasFilosofia2016oct16, 174–76]. Wilson in 1968 treated the Gómez imprint as a mistake [@wilsonCervantesItemEmmanuel1968, 364]. The Lisbon copy shows that the widow's shop printed it [@sanchezEdicionesAntiguasFilosofia2016oct16, 174–77]. Lucero tracked that copy down through library catalogs, but he says plainly that its rediscovery {q|sanchezEdicionesAntiguasFilosofia2016oct16|pdf8|176|no nos corresponde, o al menos no en sentido estricto} (is not ours, or at least not strictly). María Cristina González Hernández had located it correctly in 2012, in her doctoral thesis on Tamayo de Vargas's *Junta de libros*{es}; Manfred Zollinger had already listed both 1587 copies in 2010, but took them for copies of the same Madrigal edition [@sanchezEdicionesAntiguasFilosofia2016oct16, 176–77; @ref:gonzalez2012; @ref:zollinger2010]. Lucero's comparison of the texts showed that they are two editions, and he argues that the widow's is the first [@sanchezEdicionesAntiguasFilosofia2016oct16, 177, 179]. An Indies license of 17 November 1587 allowed Barros to print and sell in the Americas {q|luceroTableroJuego15882019|pdf5|200|la dicha pintura y moralidad della} (the said picture and its moralization) [@decaceresTableroItalianoFilosofia2009, 84, 101 n. 30; @luceroTableroJuego15882019, 200]. Whether an Indies edition followed, the sources we cite do not say. José Toribio Medina thought that nearly the whole edition had been shipped to the Indies, which would explain why no copy was found in Europe [@sanchezEdicionesAntiguasFilosofia2016oct16, 177 n. 35; @luceroTableroJuego15882019, 199].

#### Three boards, two lost

The license covers a picture on a large sheet with a separate moralization [@luceroTableroJuego15882019, 198]. M says the picture travels apart from the book [@debarrosFilosofiaCortesana1587, pdf 11]. Neither Madrid copy keeps a board. Dadson took the lack of copies, and of boards, as a sign of heavy use [@sanchezEdicionesAntiguasFilosofia2016oct16, 177]; Lucero thinks loose sheets not bound with the text were simply lost [@luceroTableroJuego15882019, 198]. He also accounts for the short run of printings: a fashion newly arrived from Italy created the need for a pocket guide, and as the game's rules became common knowledge the guide lost its use [@sanchezEdicionesAntiguasFilosofia2016oct16, 177–78].

Lucero argues that each Madrid edition had its own board [@luceroTableroJuego15882019, 203]. G twice places Poverty at 59, not 60, and has different Labor verses, so its board must have differed from Cartaro's [@luceroTableroJuego15882019, 203–04]; M's changed description of the central sea (it adds a patient angler) suggests a new plate, close to the Naples one but not identical [@luceroTableroJuego15882019, 205–06]. Lucero adds an argument from price. Madrigal's edition was priced at half a real for its four printed sheets. The workshop's usual price in 1580–90 was three maravedís a sheet, and even Hernando de Soto's illustrated *Emblemas moralizadas*{es} reached only three and a half {q|luceroTableroJuego15882019|pdf6|201|por causa de las estampas que tiene} (because of the prints it has). Only an engraved sheet sold with the book, he argues, explains the cost [@luceroTableroJuego15882019, 201–02 and n. 10]. His thesis states the claim in two forms: two boards, one for each Madrid edition [@sanchezFILOSOFIACORTESANAALONSO, 20], and, in its conclusions, at least one board with small differences for G and perhaps one very like Cartaro's for M, judging by the book's price [@sanchezFILOSOFIACORTESANAALONSO, 43–44]. Infantes, before G was found, thought the Naples board might copy a lost Madrid one [@infantesPinturaQueSe2010, 135].

#### Modern editions

- **Trevor J. Dadson, ed., Madrid: Comunidad de Madrid, 1987**, for the fourth centenary: study and transcription, facsimile of the Emmanuel copy of M, and a conjectural board, in a slipcase, 500 numbered copies not for sale [@ref:dadson1987ed; @infantesPinturaQueSe2010, 128–30; @sanchezFILOSOFIACORTESANAALONSO, 16]. The board was Víctor Infantes's design. It set the 63 squares out as an even grid on a 500 × 340 mm sheet, with Barros's verses and their instructions set at the matching numbers and an arrow to mark which way to read; its one picture, at the head of the sheet, was the book's two-faced Fortune with her motto. Infantes also records the three inks and the light ochre laid paper, and says he deliberately took no images from the emblem books [@infantesPinturaQueSe2010, 129–30]. Collar de Cáceres later objected that such a grid does not allow for the game's descent from the goose game, which runs in a spiral; he credited the board to Dadson, and Infantes explains that he had made it himself [@decaceresTableroItalianoFilosofia2009, 84–85; @infantesPinturaQueSe2010, 132]. Paula Lupiáñez López later made an art-school reconstruction, with pictures of her own and with board, dice and book kept together in a box [@infantesPinturaQueSe2010, 130, 132].
- **Luigi Ciompi and Adrian Seville**, online modernized transcription on giochidelloca.it, taken from Dadson, with many errors, Lucero says; Marcos Méndez Filesi reproduced it online in 2010 [@sanchezEdicionesAntiguasFilosofia2016oct16, 173 n. 14]. This edition reproduces its Spanish for the passages of M (see the editorial note).
- **Javier Blasco, ed., Valladolid: Agilice Digital, 2014**, an ebook that Lucero finds derivative of Ciompi and Seville and full of errors [@ref:blasco2014; @sanchezEdicionesAntiguasFilosofia2016oct16, 173 n. 14; @sanchezFILOSOFIACORTESANAALONSO, 17].
- **A facsimile of the Naples board**, Madrid: Biblioteca Nacional, 2016, cited by Rodríguez Mansilla; we have not seen it [@ref:bne2016; @rodriguezmansillaGRABADOPICARAJUSTINA2019, 188, 193].
- **Enrique Suárez Figaredo, ed.**, a paleographic text of C, *Lemir* 23 (2019): 203–226 [@sevilleImportanceTextPrinted2026, 72 n. 19; @sanchezFILOSOFIACORTESANAALONSO, 17 n. 2; @web:https://parnaseo.uv.es/lemir/Revista/Revista23/Textos/03_Filosofia_cortesana_Barros.pdf].

#### This edition

Our text is a new diplomatic, that is letter-for-letter, transcription of C, set beside a new transcription of Cartaro's board, with M's and G's readings reported where they differ. The Spanish of M's passages is reproduced from Ciompi and Seville's modernized transcription, and G's readings are quoted from Lucero's collation. The switch between G, M and C changes the texts shown and, under G, the square of Poverty. See the editorial note, “How this edition was made”.

### about-board
title: The board of 1588

#### Maker and making

The sheet is signed at lower right “Marius·Cartarius.Jnc Neap·1588·”: Mario Cartaro engraved it (*incidit*) in Naples in 1588 [board] [@decaceresTableroItalianoFilosofia2009, 86]. Seville's note on Cartaro, which he credits to the British Museum catalog, reads {q|sevilleImportanceTextPrinted2026|pdf9|70 n. 16|Engraver, print dealer and publisher, from Viterbo, b.1540/5 active in Rome 1557-1586/8 and in Naples by 1588} [@sevilleImportanceTextPrinted2026, 70 n. 16]. Wood says that he {q|woodChancingItPrint2019|pdf13|462|moved to Naples in 1586 to complete maps of the Kingdom} [@woodChancingItPrint2019, 462]; the Treccani biography records that the Regia Camera of Naples employed him from 1586 to draw maps, and that he died in Naples on 16 April 1620 [@web:https://www.treccani.it/enciclopedia/mario-cartaro_(Dizionario-Biografico)/]. Zollinger describes him as the viceroy's cartographer [@GameGooseLargest]. Collar de Cáceres lists his Roman work: Vignola's façade for the Gesù, maps, celestial globes and religious prints [@decaceresTableroItalianoFilosofia2009, 102 n. 65]. Bartsch's catalog of his prints does not include the board [@decaceresTableroItalianoFilosofia2009, 102 n. 64].

The technique is etching and engraving on a single sheet of paper, 531 × 404 mm [@woodChancingItPrint2019, 463; @web:https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/P_1869-0410-2463-]. Wood gives the usual size of printed boards as roughly 35–40 by 46–53 cm, printable on a royal folio and trimmed; this sheet is at the top of that range [@woodChancingItPrint2019, 479 n. 20]. Lucero notes that Italian engravers were prized for their etching, while Spanish emblem writers complained of Castilian block-cutters [@luceroDEDICATORIAFILOSOFIACORTESANA2019, 48].

#### Two privileges and three languages

The title runs along the top and ends at lower right: “FILOSOFIA CORTESANA DE ALONSO DE BARROS CRIADO DEL REY N[UEST]RO S[EÑ]OR”, with “Con su priuilegio” beneath [board]. A second line grants a privilege of His Excellency, the viceroy, for ten years in the Kingdom of Naples [board] [@GameGooseLargest]. The verses of the squares are in Italian above and Spanish below [@decaceresTableroItalianoFilosofia2009, 86, 91]. The rule labels above the hazards are in Italian, and only the Well's is in both languages [@GameGooseLargest; board]. Lucero lists what is in one language only: the corner mottoes in Spanish, the swan's Latin “Nosce te ipsum”, the Italian “Guarda l'fine” at the gate, the title in Spanish [@luceroTableroJuego15882019, 203 n. 14]. He takes the bilingual text as aimed at the Spanish colony in Naples and at local courtiers, and as evidence that Cartaro copied an earlier print [@luceroTableroJuego15882019, 203 n. 14].

Naples was then a kingdom of the Spanish crown, {q|luceroTableroJuego15882019|pdf8|203|al fin y al cabo territorio de la corona} (territory of the crown, after all), in Lucero's words [@luceroTableroJuego15882019, 203 n. 14]. Zollinger describes the second privilege as that of the king's representative, the viceroy, and Cartaro as the viceroy's official cartographer [@GameGooseLargest]; the sources we cite do not name the viceroy. Lucero gives three reasons for printing in Naples: Italian engravers were prized for their skill with etching, the goose game itself was Italian, and, above all, Vázquez had Italian ambitions for his lineage [@luceroDEDICATORIAFILOSOFIACORTESANA2019, 48–49]. Wilson remarks that {q|wilsonCervantesItemEmmanuel1968|pdf3|364|Apparently the Italian printer had no ñ in the fou[nt] [in] which the poem is set}, so that Cervantes's sonnet reads “sennalarse” and “pequenna” (in the Vienna copy both words are blotted; our transcription reads *señalarse*[?]{es} and *pequenna*[?]{es}) [@wilsonCervantesItemEmmanuel1968, 364–65].

#### Layout

A gate at bottom left leads to square 1, the peacock; the numbers run counter-clockwise along the bottom, up the right side, across the top and down the left, then turn inward in a second ring to the palm at 63 [board] [@woodChancingItPrint2019, 462–63]. Balusters divide the bays, as on other Italian goose boards; Collar de Cáceres sees in the spiral of balusters the spokes of Fortune's wheel [@decaceresTableroItalianoFilosofia2009, 88, 90]. Each bay with a picture has it above and, where the square has one, a heading and two couplets below; lettering reads from outside the track, so the top row is upside down for a player seated at the foot of the sheet (our observation) [board]. The nine Labor squares show yoked oxen under hanging fruit [@decaceresTableroItalianoFilosofia2009, 91]. Most other squares carry only a number and a plant (our observation) [board].

The four corners hold a dolphin wound round an anchor, “Date prisa a espacio” (make haste slowly), top left; a half-reclining woman with a single forelock, Occasion, “No me pierdas” (don't lose me), top right; a hand pointing to a clock, “Haesta la postrera” (until the last), bottom right; and the entrance gate with the swan on a skull, bottom left [board] [@decaceresTableroItalianoFilosofia2009, 92–93]. The engraved forms are “Dateprisa aespacio” and “Haesta la postrera”; Lucero and Collar de Cáceres print “Date prisa despacio”, and Collar reads “Ha esta la postrera” [board] [@luceroTableroJuego15882019, 203 n. 14; @decaceresTableroItalianoFilosofia2009, 92–93]. Barros's text explains only three corner figures [@barrosFilosofiaCortesanaMoralizada1588, pdf 57]. The center is the Sea of Suffering: ships, a galley, an angler, a net-fisher, fish and sea monsters [board] [@decaceresTableroItalianoFilosofia2009, 99]. Wood compares its sea monsters to those of maps, fitting Cartaro's trade [@woodChancingItPrint2019, 466]. Rodríguez Mansilla notes that the Italian goose boards he compares share the spiral but have no sea at the center [@rodriguezmansillaGRABADOPICARAJUSTINA2019, 190].

#### Board and book

The board does not carry the rules in full: Lucero points out that Barros put no summary of rules on the sheet, so the book was needed [@luceroTableroJuego15882019, 202]. What the board has are short labels (“Paga”, “Alla sorte ·n·° 28 ·Paga”, “Giuoca ·2· uolte ·”) [board]. The two do not always agree. The Fortune on square 51 differs from the book's woodcut, and her banner reads “cambio et muto il consiglio” where C's text has “Yo trueco, y mudo el co[n]sejo” [@barrosFilosofiaCortesanaMoralizada1588, pdf 45; @rodriguezmansillaGRABADOPICARAJUSTINA2019, 191; @decaceresTableroItalianoFilosofia2009, 104 n. 113; board]. The Spanish lines of squares 10 and 53 stand in the reverse order to the book's [@decaceresTableroItalianoFilosofia2009, 104 n. 100; @rodriguezmansillaGRABADOPICARAJUSTINA2019, 190]. C's text never numbers the palm square; only the board gives it 63 [@barrosFilosofiaCortesanaMoralizada1588, pdf 53, pdf 68–69; board].

#### Provenance and rediscovery

The British Museum acquired the sheet in 1869: its record gives it as {q|britishMuseumRecord2012|pdf1||Purchased through Sotheby's (24-7.ii.1869)}, through the dealer Edward Daniell, from José de Salamanca y Mayol, Marquis of Salamanca, with José de Madrazo as an earlier owner [@britishMuseumRecord2012; @web:https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/P_1869-0410-2463-]. The record describes the lettering as {q|britishMuseumRecord2012|pdf1||numerous explanatory inscriptions in Italian and Spanish} and reads the signature “Mario Cartaro inc Neap 1588” [@britishMuseumRecord2012]; on the plate the name is Latinized, “Marius·Cartarius” [board]. It was thus in a Spanish collection before 1869 (our inference from the record). Seville's registration number, 1869,0410.2463.+, sits next to Gargano's goose board of 1598, 1869,0410.2465.+ [@sevilleImportanceTextPrinted2026, 65, 71]. Zollinger reports that an Australian blogger put the sheet online in 2008 without knowing what it was [@GameGooseLargest]. Collar de Cáceres (2009) and Zollinger (2010) then identified and published it independently [@infantesPinturaQueSe2010, 130, 133; @luceroTableroJuego15882019, 202 n. 12]. It was shown in the Museum's 2017 display “Prints in Play” [@web:https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/P_1869-0410-2463-].

#### Among other boards

Seville writes that {q|sevilleImportanceTextPrinted2026|pdf9|70|The unique example of its game sheet is held in the British Museum} [@sevilleImportanceTextPrinted2026, 70]; Zollinger called it the oldest dated goose game known [@GameGooseLargest]. Collar de Cáceres sets beside it Gatti's *Gioco della scimia*{it}, also of 1588, whose track is likewise a spiral of bays divided by balusters [@decaceresTableroItalianoFilosofia2009, 86, 88]. Wood reads such Roman boards as systems of rules and as maps of journeys, whose moralizing works as an apology for gambling in a decade of papal edicts against dice; for her {q|woodChancingItPrint2019|pdf14|463|Each game therefore provides a pretext for gambling} [@woodChancingItPrint2019, 451, 463]. Wood accounts for the scarcity of boards by their life in taverns, where a sheet glued to a table served until it wore out and was covered by another; the impressions she examined, by contrast, look scarcely handled [@woodChancingItPrint2019, 469, 479 n. 20]. Seville adds that an intaglio plate, unlike a woodcut, makes it easy to engrave rules on the sheet [@sevilleImportanceTextPrinted2026, 66].

Closest in theme among the prints Wood discusses is Matteo Florimi's etching of a courtier's career told in steps. Wood describes it: {q|woodChancingItPrint2019|pdf18|467|A young man ascends a short staircase at the right to enter the court through a portal of honour, lifted up by hope and ambition, only to descend a longer staircase of vices at the left and be ejected from the court as an impoverished elderly man, as hope flies away} [@woodChancingItPrint2019, 467]. Bidwell-Steiner sees a probable kinship with the ancient *Table of Cebes*, a picture of the soul's progress that also has 27 special stations in a labyrinth, and of which she counts several Spanish editions in the sixteenth century [@bidwell-steinerFilosofiaCortesanaAlfonso2024, 211].

#### Notes on the sources

- The giochidelloca.it catalog, in the machine-translated copy we have, gives the owner as “British Museum. Donation Lady Charlotte Schreiber” [@GameGooseLargest]; the Museum's own record gives a purchase in 1869 [@britishMuseumRecord2012].
- A hand-colored version of the sheet circulates online: it illustrates two 2013 posts on the *La Bella Donna* blog (its file is named for a class handout, “class-apa”) and a 2013 thread on the Tarot History Forum [@FilosofiaCortesanaPath; @TarotHistoryForum]. It follows Cartaro's plate line for line, with flat modern colors on white paper; we take it for a modern coloring of a reproduction, not an early impression (our observation). The sources we cite record no colored impression and no impression other than the British Museum's [@sevilleImportanceTextPrinted2026, 70; @britishMuseumRecord2012].

### about-reception
title: Readers, then and now

#### Paratexts of 1587

The approval, dated 13 January 1587, is signed by Alonso de Ercilla, the poet of *La Araucana*{es}; he judged the work ingenious, useful to the commonwealth as honest and pleasant entertainment, and the best of its kind he had seen [@wilsonCervantesItemEmmanuel1968, 367, 369]. Ercilla also appears on the endorsement of Barros's request for a privilege, which calls the work “un descurso de pretensores” [@luceroTableroJuego15882019, 200–01].

Pedro Liñán de Riaza's sonnet, in M, praises the book for trying {q|luceroDEDICATORIAFILOSOFIACORTESANA2019|pdf2|34|reducir a placer la pesadumbre / de pretensiones que consumen vidas} (to turn into pleasure the burden of petitions that consume lives) [@luceroDEDICATORIAFILOSOFIACORTESANA2019, 34]. Cervantes's sonnet, also first in M, compares the small book to a precious stone and promises the reader the thread through the courtly labyrinth [@wilsonCervantesItemEmmanuel1968, 366; @bidwell-steinerFilosofiaCortesanaAlfonso2024, 197]. Lucero reads it as Cervantes's third attempt to win Vázquez's favor, after his verse epistle from Algiers and *La Galatea*{es}; he never won it [@sanchezEdicionesAntiguasFilosofia2016oct16, 180–81 and n. 41]. Until Wilson's article of 1968, Infantes writes, the sonnet was the only reason critics noticed the book [@infantesPinturaQueSe2010, 128]: Fernández de Navarrete printed it in 1819, and Schevill and Bonilla printed it in 1922 from the Vienna copy of C [@wilsonCervantesItemEmmanuel1968, 363–64]. The printed line reads “laberintio” [@bidwell-steinerFilosofiaCortesanaAlfonso2024, 197]; Rodríguez Mansilla quotes it as “laberinto” [@rodriguezmansillaGRABADOPICARAJUSTINA2019, 192].

#### Early readers

Mateo Alemán, in his prologue to Barros's *Proverbios morales*{es} (1598), says the *Filosofía cortesana*{es} could also be called {q|sanchezEdicionesAntiguasFilosofia2016oct16|pdf2|170|desengaño de pretensores} (the disillusioning of office-seekers) [@sanchezEdicionesAntiguasFilosofia2016oct16, 170–71; @wilsonCervantesItemEmmanuel1968, 364]. Gutierre Marqués de Careaga, *Desengaño de Fortuna*{es} (Madrid, 1612, fol. 225), quoted the Labor couplets with approval [@ref:careaga1612; @wilsonCervantesItemEmmanuel1968, 370]; Pérez Pastor used that quotation to show that the book was not the *Proverbios* [@sanchezEdicionesAntiguasFilosofia2016oct16, 170, 174 n. 15]. Carrera (1617) names Barros as the inventor of a goose variant [@sevilleImportanceTextPrinted2026, 69; @decaceresTableroItalianoFilosofia2009, 85]. Collar de Cáceres supposes that Carrera, a Sicilian, knew the Naples edition, if only because it was near [@decaceresTableroItalianoFilosofia2009, 85].

Fernando Rodríguez Mansilla argues that the title-page engraving of *La pícara Justina*{es} (1605), a border of 24 pictured squares round a ship of idlers, parodies Barros's board: 24 hours against 63 years, a cross against the palm, the river of oblivion against the sea of suffering, a sleeping Idleness against the oxen of labor [@rodriguezmansillaGRABADOPICARAJUSTINA2019, 183–84, 188–90]. He takes the joke as aimed at the same courtly readers who played Barros's game, not as an attack on it [@rodriguezmansillaGRABADOPICARAJUSTINA2019, 191–92]. He admits as a possible objection the gap between the two works, {q|rodriguezmansillaGRABADOPICARAJUSTINA2019|pdf3|185|poco más de tres lustros, de 1587 a 1605} (a little more than fifteen years, from 1587 to 1605) [@rodriguezmansillaGRABADOPICARAJUSTINA2019, 185]. *Justina* also uses the word *penseque*, Barros's “I Thought…” square; Lucero leaves open whether the game shaped the later use [@lucerosanchezAsnoPenseQue2019, 171–72].

#### Modern scholarship

- **E. M. Wilson (1968)** described the Emmanuel College copy of M, summarized the game in English and confirmed that it was not the *Proverbios* [@wilsonCervantesItemEmmanuel1968, 363–71]. He admitted he could not follow every detail of the rules [@wilsonCervantesItemEmmanuel1968, 367].
- **Trevor J. Dadson (1987)** edited M with a facsimile and a conjectural board, and published Barros's library [@ref:dadson1987ed; @ref:dadson1987lib; @infantesPinturaQueSe2010, 128–30; @decaceresTableroItalianoFilosofia2009, 83].
- **Víctor Infantes** designed the 1987 board, wrote on the work as “emblemática sin emblemas” (1996), published the 1585 Liaño letter (2010) and returned to it in *Ludo ergo sum*{la} (2014) [@ref:infantes1996; @ref:infantes2014; @infantesPinturaQueSe2010, 129–30, 134–35; @luceroTableroJuego15882019, 204 n. 16; @sanchezEdicionesAntiguasFilosofia2016oct16, 177].
- **José Martínez Millán (1996)** read the book as a treatise on court behavior and placed Barros in the *castellanista* faction; he had not been able to consult Dadson's 1987 edition, with its conjectural board [@millanFilosofiaCortesanaAlonso1996, 461 n. 4, 463–64].
- **Fernando Collar de Cáceres (2009)** published the Naples board, documented Barros's life and read the board's images against the emblem books [@decaceresTableroItalianoFilosofia2009, 81–104].
- **Manfred Zollinger (2010)** identified the board independently and described it with its rules [@ref:zollinger2010; @GameGooseLargest; @infantesPinturaQueSe2010, 133].
- **Kelli Wood (2019)** set it among Roman printed gambling boards of 1588–1600 [@woodChancingItPrint2019, 453, 462–63].
- **Ernesto Lucero (2016–2021)** showed that the Lisbon copy is a separate edition and argued that it is the princeps, proposed the lost Madrid boards, and wrote on the dedications, labor, the “I Thought…” square and the rules; his critical edition appeared in 2019 [@sanchezFILOSOFIACORTESANAALONSO, 12–13].
- **Marlen Bidwell-Steiner (2024)** read the game as casuistic practice and as a picture of an all-male court [@bidwell-steinerFilosofiaCortesanaAlfonso2024, 195–96, 208–11].
- **Adrian Seville (2026)** used it as his first case of a board game whose companion book carries its meaning [@sevilleImportanceTextPrinted2026, 70].

Lucero notes that modern critics neglected the book for the showy board [@luceroTableroJuego15882019, 198]. Biagioli (1993) cited the game at second hand from Geoffrey Parker [@ref:parker1979], as {q|biagioliGalileoCourtierPractice1993|pdf50|35|a court game not unlike today's "Monopoly"} [@biagioliGalileoCourtierPractice1993, 35]; Wilson had compared it to Snakes and Ladders and Monopoly [@wilsonCervantesItemEmmanuel1968, 367]. Amateur English translations of the board circulated from 2013 on the La Bella Donna blog [@TranslationFilosofiaCortesana; @GiocoDellocaBella].

### about-debates
title: Where scholars disagree

The sources we cite divide on several questions about the whole work. Disagreements about single squares are recorded in the Atlas, and those about Barros's life in “What the sources do not settle”, above.

- **Satire or encouragement?** Luis Gómez Canseco hears {q|cansecoLUCEROErnestoEd2021|pdf5|433|una profunda y amarga sátira} (a deep and bitter satire) under the moral, and Díaz Gito a pessimistic view of the petitioner's life [@cansecoLUCEROErnestoEd2021, 433; @gitoTodaVidaEs2020, 185]; the publisher's page for Lucero's edition, in the English version we have, speaks of {q|FilosofiaCortesanaBarros|pdf0||a pessimistic and bitter feeling} [@FilosofiaCortesanaBarros]. Marcos Méndez Filesi found the book {q|GameGooseLargest|pdf0||algo más optimista} (somewhat more optimistic) than the anti-court tradition, since labor can bring the petitioner to the palm [@GameGooseLargest]. (See “What kind of book?”)
- **Stoic or not?** Martínez Millán linked the book to the Neostoicism of Lipsius and to Seneca, Díaz Gito finds in it a Stoic and Senecan reflection on the power of Fortune, and Lucero a Senecan acceptance of failure [@millanFilosofiaCortesanaAlonso1996, 468–69; @gitoTodaVidaEs2020, 184; @luceroFilosofiaCortesanaJuego2021juin20, 146]. Bidwell-Steiner disagrees: the square of What Will They Say? shows that, for Barros, effort is worth nothing without the approval of others, and so {q|bidwell-steinerFilosofiaCortesanaAlfonso2024|pdf7|201|contradice las interpretaciones que sitúan el texto en una vertiente neoestoica} (contradicts the readings that place the text in a Neostoic current) [@bidwell-steinerFilosofiaCortesanaAlfonso2024, 201].
- **Can Fortune be mastered?** Martínez Millán thought Barros the first writer to treat fortune scientifically, as something with a logic that the courtier can understand and plan for [@millanFilosofiaCortesanaAlonso1996, 468]. Lucero answers that the dice decide everything: {q|luceroIdeaTrabajoFilosofia2019|pdf9|829|Entonces, Fortuna siempre vence.} (Then Fortune always wins.) Barros, he writes, {q|luceroFilosofiaCortesanaJuego2021juin20|pdf17|148|solo es víctima del razonamiento falaz} (is only the victim of the fallacious reasoning) {q|luceroFilosofiaCortesanaJuego2021juin20|pdf17|148|tan propio del tahúr que, en plena vorágine, delira al pensar que comprende las leyes del azar} (so typical of the gambler who, in the thick of the frenzy, deludes himself that he understands the laws of chance), and the human wish to control one's fate ends as {q|luceroFilosofiaCortesanaJuego2021juin20|pdf18|149|un dulce engaño} (a sweet deceit) [@luceroIdeaTrabajoFilosofia2019, 829; @luceroFilosofiaCortesanaJuego2021juin20, 148–49].
- **Can one lose?** Lucero also holds that {q|luceroFilosofiaCortesanaJuego2021juin20|pdf15|146|no se pierde nunca en la oca de Barros, aunque no se obtenga el premio final} (one never loses in Barros's goose game, even without winning the final prize), because learning the strategy, and mastering oneself, is reward enough [@luceroFilosofiaCortesanaJuego2021juin20, 146].
- **An excuse for gambling, or a case-book?** For Kelli Wood the moralized Roman boards of these years let players perform virtue while they enjoyed gambling: {q|woodChancingItPrint2019|pdf14|463|Each game therefore provides a pretext for gambling} [@woodChancingItPrint2019, 460, 463]. Bidwell-Steiner reads Barros's game as licit recreation, *eutrapelia*, whose book gives casuistic guidance for each case the dice produce [@bidwell-steinerFilosofiaCortesanaAlfonso2024, 206–08]. (See “Playing for money”.)
- **Faction or a wider change?** Martínez Millán placed Barros in Vázquez's *castellanista* faction, and Lucero reads the board's praise of labor as that faction's ideology; Bidwell-Steiner prefers a wider change in the codes of court life [@millanFilosofiaCortesanaAlonso1996, 463; @luceroFilosofiaCortesanaJuego2020, 118; @bidwell-steinerFilosofiaCortesanaAlfonso2024, 195–96]. (See “Factions”.)
- **Picture first, or text?** Víctor Infantes read the royal license as showing that {q|infantesPinturaQueSe2010|pdf5|129|primero fue la gestación de un «pliego grande» que contenía el juego} (first came the making of a “large sheet” that held the game), and only then its moral explanation [@infantesPinturaQueSe2010, 129]. Lucero replies that the license's order is only its drafter's, that Alciato's emblems had their verses before their pictures, and that Barros's own request for a privilege calls the work a {q|luceroTableroJuego15882019|pdf5|200|descurso de pretensores} (discourse on petitioners), not a picture; one could even argue that {q|luceroTableroJuego15882019|pdf5|200|primero fuera la letra y después la imagen} (the text came first and the picture after) [@luceroTableroJuego15882019, 199–201 and nn. 6–7].
- **What did the Indies license cover?** Read in the excerpt Collar de Cáceres gives in his text, the cédula seems to concern the picture alone; Lucero notes that Collar prints the full document in a footnote and finds there a license covering picture and text together [@luceroTableroJuego15882019, 199–200; @decaceresTableroItalianoFilosofia2009, 84].
- **How many lost boards?** Lucero argues that each Madrid edition had its own board; Dadson took the missing boards as worn out by use, Lucero as loose sheets lost; in his thesis Lucero's claim is at times more cautious [@luceroTableroJuego15882019, 198, 203; @sanchezEdicionesAntiguasFilosofia2016oct16, 177; @sanchezFILOSOFIACORTESANAALONSO, 20, 43–44] (see “Three boards, two lost”).
- **The first goose game in Spain?** David González Ramírez calls it {q|ramirezAlonsoBarrosFilosofia2020|pdf3||el primer juego de la oca de nuestra historia} (the first goose game in our history) [@ramirezAlonsoBarrosFilosofia2020, pdf 3]. The goose game itself was being played at the Spanish court in 1585 [@infantesPinturaQueSe2010, 134–35]; Lucero's claims are that Barros's is the first themed version on record and the first Spanish description of the game [@luceroFilosofiaCortesanaJuego2020, 117; @sanchezFILOSOFIACORTESANAALONSO, 37].
- **Was the book popular?** Lucero takes three editions in little more than a year as a sign that it was [@sanchezEdicionesAntiguasFilosofia2016oct16, 177]. The publisher's page for his edition says it {q|FilosofiaCortesanaBarros|pdf0||went almost unnoticed in its time} [@FilosofiaCortesanaBarros]; González Ramírez finds it curious that after three editions in little more than a year the printers forgot the book [@ramirezAlonsoBarrosFilosofia2020, pdf 3].
- **Did Barros succeed?** González Ramírez speaks of his {q|ramirezAlonsoBarrosFilosofia2020|pdf3||fructífera trayectoria en la corte} (fruitful career at court), though he was never wholly satisfied; Díaz Gito writes that Barros aspired to higher posts all his career {q|gitoTodaVidaEs2020|pdf2|184|sin gran éxito} (without great success) [@ramirezAlonsoBarrosFilosofia2020, pdf 3; @gitoTodaVidaEs2020, 184]. (See “Barros on his own board” in the introduction, and “Alonso de Barros”.)
- **What kind of book?** Lucero calls it a book of *avisos* in his thesis and a work of *institutio* in an article (see “What kind of book?”).

### about-rules
title: How the game is played

#### What we have to go on

Three texts give rules, and they differ. G and C end with a short section of rules, which Lucero finds laconic and in agreement on the concrete rules [@luceroTableroJuego15882019, 203 n. 13]. M replaces it with a longer “Declaración del juego y orden de jugarle”, with a worked example for three players [@sanchezEdicionesAntiguasFilosofia2016oct16, 189–90]. The prose sections of all three also state rules square by square [@barrosFilosofiaCortesanaMoralizada1588, pdf 27–28, pdf 47–52]. The Naples board adds short Italian labels [board]. G is known to us only through Lucero's quotations and tables [@sanchezEdicionesAntiguasFilosofia2016oct16, 183–90; @luceroTableroJuego15882019, 204].

#### Rules common to all three

G's wording is quoted only for its first rule and for Poverty and the overshoot; Lucero says the rest agrees with C [@luceroTableroJuego15882019, 203 n. 13; @sanchezEdicionesAntiguasFilosofia2016oct16, 189].

- Move by the points thrown; if two land on one square, the newcomer stays and the first takes the square the newcomer left [@barrosFilosofiaCortesanaMoralizada1588, pdf 66; @sanchezEdicionesAntiguasFilosofia2016oct16, 189]. Barros calls this “el vso de la competencia” (the custom of rivalry) [@barrosFilosofiaCortesanaMoralizada1588, pdf 28].
- Labor (the oxen, 4, 12, 17, 23, 30, 34, 41, 48, 57): move on again as many squares as the throw that brought you there [@barrosFilosofiaCortesanaMoralizada1588, pdf 66; board] [@luceroFilosofiaCortesanaJuego2020, 124].
- Pass of Hope (15): go on to the Favorite (26), paying one stake “for each” (M: “for each of the two”) [@barrosFilosofiaCortesanaMoralizada1588, pdf 66–67; @debarrosFilosofiaCortesana1587, pdf 12]. Board: 15 “Al priuato ·n[umer]º· 26. Paga”; 26 “Paga.” [board].
- Well of Oblivion (32): miss a turn, pay one stake to each player and two to the pot “para sogas” (for ropes) [@barrosFilosofiaCortesanaMoralizada1588, pdf 67]. Board: “Per le fune ·1· et ·2· fermesi / Para·sogas ·1· y ·2· estese” [board].
- What Will They Say? (36) to the Dice (28); False Friendship (39) to the Prodigal (7); Change of Ministers (43) to Flattery (10); Death of the Patron (46): start the game again; House of Fortune (51): throw twice; “I Thought…” (55) to Diligence (20) [@barrosFilosofiaCortesanaMoralizada1588, pdf 67–68]. Board labels on 36, 39, 43, 46 and 55 add “Paga”; 51 reads “Giuoca ·2· uolte ·” [board].
- Poverty to the Dice at 53, with alms [@barrosFilosofiaCortesanaMoralizada1588, pdf 68; @luceroTableroJuego15882019, 204]. Board: “Limosina et alla sorte·53” [board].
- Overshoot the palm: go back the excess, paying one stake “por cada vez que boluiere atras de qualquier suerte que sea” (each time one goes back, of whatever kind) [@barrosFilosofiaCortesanaMoralizada1588, pdf 68–69; @luceroTableroJuego15882019, 204].

#### G (Madrid, 1587, first edition)

Headed “Esto se juega en esta forma”; its first rule speaks of “el dado”, the die [@sanchezEdicionesAntiguasFilosofia2016oct16, 189]. Poverty stands at 59 [@sanchezEdicionesAntiguasFilosofia2016oct16, 180 n. 43, 189; @luceroTableroJuego15882019, 204]. The poor player is given alms (“danle limosna”), with no amount [@luceroTableroJuego15882019, 204]. G has no sentence on how the game is won; its rules end at the overshoot clause, set in the shape of a goblet, and “Deo gratias” [@sanchezEdicionesAntiguasFilosofia2016oct16, 189–90].

#### M (Madrid, 1587, Madrigal)

M's Declaración adds [@debarrosFilosofiaCortesana1587, pdf 11–12; @GameGooseLargest; @millanFilosofiaCortesanaAlonso1996, 470–71]:
- the sheet is spread on a table or pasted to it;
- each player first puts one stake, of whatever value is agreed, into the pot;
- play is with two dice marked on every face from one to six, or with six *arenillas* marked on one face;
- two or more may play, as many as wish; each has a distinct marker (a ring, a two-real coin, a doubloon in the example) and plays in turn;
- a player displaced at the very start is left without a square;
- the poor player receives one stake from each other player, pays nothing for going back, and is the one exception to the overshoot charge;
- whoever lands exactly on 63, the palm, takes the whole pot, both the opening stakes and everything paid in during play.

The worked example applies these rules. Pedro, on 6, throws 9 to reach 15, reads “Al privado” above it, pays one stake and moves to 26, paying another. Rodrigo, on 5, throws 10, reaches 15, moves to 26, finds Pedro there and takes the square, paying two stakes. Pedro goes back to 5, where Rodrigo had been, paying one stake “porque volvió atrás” (because he went back) [@debarrosFilosofiaCortesana1587, pdf 12]. The example ends by saying the same holds for the other squares and for more players [@debarrosFilosofiaCortesana1587, pdf 12].

#### C (Naples, 1588, with Cartaro's board)

Headed “EL YVEGO SE juega en esta forma”, it opens “EL Que lança el dado” (whoever throws the die) [@barrosFilosofiaCortesanaMoralizada1588, pdf 66]. Poverty is at 60, as in M and on the board [@barrosFilosofiaCortesanaMoralizada1588, pdf 68; board]. It gives alms without an amount, like G [@barrosFilosofiaCortesanaMoralizada1588, pdf 68]. It lacks M's rule for the start, M's exception for Poverty, and any rule for winning; the palm is never numbered in the text [@barrosFilosofiaCortesanaMoralizada1588, pdf 66–69]. On the board the palm's own motto, “Ne il poco ne lassai · / Ni lo mucho·ni la poco” (neither too much nor too little), states the exact-throw rule, as Barros explains in the prose [board] [@millanFilosofiaCortesanaAlonso1996, 481].

#### What this digital version has to decide

The sources leave gaps. For each, the options, what the sources say, and the default we propose.

1. **How many dice in G and C.** G and C say “el dado” [@sanchezEdicionesAntiguasFilosofia2016oct16, 189; @barrosFilosofiaCortesanaMoralizada1588, pdf 66]. M says two dice, or six *arenillas* [@debarrosFilosofiaCortesana1587, pdf 11]. M's own last rule also says “con los puntos del dado” while using two dice, so the singular can be generic (our observation) [@debarrosFilosofiaCortesana1587, pdf 12]. The 1585 Liaño letter describes the goose game at court as played with two dice [@infantesPinturaQueSe2010, 135]. Barros's Fortune rule, that two throws from 51 can win, fits two dice, with 12 the most points in one throw [@luceroFilosofiaCortesanaJuego2020, 125; @bidwell-steinerFilosofiaCortesanaAlfonso2024, 203]. The Dice squares picture three dice (1, 2, 3 at 28; 4, 4, 3 at 53), and Lucero notes that the goose game's dice squares show two [@luceroFilosofiaCortesanaJuego2020, 124; @GameGooseLargest; board]; no text makes the picture a rule for play. Options: one die; two dice. This edition's choice: two dice in all three versions, with one die available as a variant for G and C. The *arenillas* (also *harinillas*) are six dice marked on one face only [@web:https://dle.rae.es/arenilla; @GameGooseLargest]; Zollinger identifies them with the Italian *farinacci* and the French *jarinets*, the dice of the later game Cloche et Marteau [@GameGooseLargest]. No source we cite says how a throw of them was counted, so we do not implement them.
2. **The alms in G and C.** Both say only “danle limosna” [@luceroTableroJuego15882019, 204; @barrosFilosofiaCortesanaMoralizada1588, pdf 68]. M specifies one stake from each other player [@debarrosFilosofiaCortesana1587, pdf 12]. Wilson, Martínez Millán and Rodríguez Mansilla, all reading M, have the others give to the poor player [@wilsonCervantesItemEmmanuel1968, 368; @millanFilosofiaCortesanaAlonso1996, 470, 481; @rodriguezmansillaGRABADOPICARAJUSTINA2019, 190]. Bidwell-Steiner, citing Lucero's edition, has the poor player give alms, probably from the moral text's ambiguous “MAndanle dar limosna” [@bidwell-steinerFilosofiaCortesanaAlfonso2024, 204; @barrosFilosofiaCortesanaMoralizada1588, pdf 52]. M settles it for M. Options for G and C: one stake from each player (M's rule); one stake from the pot; no payment. This edition's choice: M's rule.
3. **Which backward moves cost a stake.** All three versions charge one stake for going back “de cualquier suerte que sea” [@barrosFilosofiaCortesanaMoralizada1588, pdf 68–69]. The phrase can mean only the overshoot at the end, or any throw that sends a player back. M's example charges the displaced player one stake for going back [@debarrosFilosofiaCortesana1587, pdf 12], and M exempts the poor player from paying, which only makes sense if the move from Poverty to 53 would otherwise cost (our observation) [@debarrosFilosofiaCortesana1587, pdf 12]. The board prints “Paga” on every square that sends a player back except Poverty (36, 39, 43, 46, 55; not 60) but not on Fortune, and prints it on the forward move 15–26 [board] (for the label at 26 itself, see “Landing directly on the Favorite”, below); we read these labels as the same general rule, not an extra charge, because M's Declaración lists no separate payment for those squares (our observation) [@debarrosFilosofiaCortesana1587, pdf 12]. Options: (a) charge only the overshoot; (b) charge every backward move: overshoot, displacement, and the squares that send back; (c) as (b) plus a second charge on the labeled squares. This edition's choice: (b) in all versions; in G and C, whether the poor player also pays for the move to 53 is not stated; we exempt that player, as M does. This reading of M's exemption, and its extension to G and C, is the edition's own (DECISION); squares 36 and 53 say so too.
4. **A special square sends you onto an occupied square.** For a forward jump, M's example settles it: Rodrigo, sent from 15 to 26, takes Pedro's square, and Pedro goes back to the square Rodrigo had left before his throw, paying a stake [@debarrosFilosofiaCortesana1587, pdf 12]. For a backward move (39 to 7, 43 to 10, 36 to 28, 55 to 20, 60 to 53, 46 to the start) the sources are silent. Options: (a) the general rule, with the displaced player moving to the square the mover occupied before the throw, which can move the displaced player forward; (b) the displaced player moves to the square the mover was sent from; (c) no displacement on backward moves, and both share the square. This edition's choice: (a), which follows the wording of the rule and M's example; we flag it in play when it moves a player forward. The same choice applies when a labor move ends on an occupied square.
5. **Starting position.** M: a player displaced at the start “se ha de quedar sin casa”, because the newcomer had no square before [@debarrosFilosofiaCortesana1587, pdf 12]. Players therefore begin off the track, before the gate (our reading) [@debarrosFilosofiaCortesana1587, pdf 12]. The gate itself carries no number; square 1 is the peacock [board]. C lacks M's clause [@barrosFilosofiaCortesanaMoralizada1588, pdf 66]. This edition's choice: all markers start off the board at 0, in front of the gate; a player displaced from a square reached by an opening throw returns to 0; the Death of the Patron returns a player to 0.
6. **Purses.** Only M speaks of money at the start: one stake of an agreed value [@debarrosFilosofiaCortesana1587, pdf 11]. No source we cite gives a purse, a limit on debt, or what happens when a player cannot pay. Lucero observes that the game's slow progress limits its money consequences [@luceroFilosofiaCortesanaJuego2020, 126]. Options: an unlimited purse, with a running balance that may go negative; a fixed purse, with a bankrupt player out of the game; a fixed purse, with a bankrupt player continuing without paying. This edition's choice: each player starts with 20 stakes and may go negative; the balance is shown, and the winner takes the pot.

Further points the sources leave open:

- **Fortune (51), “juega dos veces”.** Two readings: one more throw, or two more throws. C's prose says the player who reaches Fortune plays twice, “estando el juego de manera, que co[n] dos suertes se gana la polla, y con las demas se pierde, o se dilata” (the game being so arranged that with two throws the pot is won, and with the others it is lost or put off) [@barrosFilosofiaCortesanaMoralizada1588, pdf 47]. Wilson, unsure of the Spanish, suggested that two throws totaling exactly twelve win [@wilsonCervantesItemEmmanuel1968, 368]. Lucero reads it as one extra throw, placed at 51 so that a throw of twelve wins [@luceroFilosofiaCortesanaJuego2020, 125]; Collar de Cáceres reads the board's “Giuoca ·2· uolte ·” as two additional throws [@decaceresTableroItalianoFilosofia2009, 96]. Our translator notes that on Cartaro's board exactly two throws win from 51: twelve, straight to the palm, and six, to the oxen at 57 and on by six more to 63 (our observation) [@barrosFilosofiaCortesanaMoralizada1588, pdf 47; board]. That reading makes “dos suertes” two winning throws, not two throws in a row. This edition's choice: one extra throw at once; two extra throws can be chosen in the setup.
- **Poverty in G.** G puts Poverty at 59 while Cartaro's board shows it at 60 [@luceroTableroJuego15882019, 204; board]. Under G the game treats 59 as Poverty and 60 as a plain square, and marks the change on the board image.
- **Labor in G.** G had eight Labor verses of its own, four of them not in M, and Lucero's thesis infers that G's Labor squares were spread more evenly [@sanchezEdicionesAntiguasFilosofia2016oct16, 187–88; @sanchezFILOSOFIACORTESANAALONSO, 20]. G's positions are not known to us; under G we keep Cartaro's Labor squares and show G's verses in G's printed order.
- **The Well.** C says the player pays “auiendo estado sin jugar vna mano” (having been a round without playing), which can mean that the player pays after missing their turn or on arrival [@barrosFilosofiaCortesanaMoralizada1588, pdf 67]. This edition's choice: the player pays on arrival and misses their next turn.
- **Turn order.** M: “por su orden”, and in the example Pedro plays first [@debarrosFilosofiaCortesana1587, pdf 11]. The rule that the youngest starts, given on the La Bella Donna blog, is not in the sources we cite [@TranslationFilosofiaCortesana].
- **Special squares reached by a rebound or a transfer.** No source we cite says whether a square reached by counting back from 63, or by a transfer, takes effect. This edition's choice: a square reached by rebound takes effect; a square reached by transfer does not (so 60 to 53 does not trigger another move).
- **Landing directly on the Favorite (26).** The board writes “Paga.” at 26 [board]. The rules mention a stake there only in the Pass of Hope clause and say nothing of a player who lands on 26 directly [@barrosFilosofiaCortesanaMoralizada1588, pdf 66–67; @debarrosFilosofiaCortesana1587, pdf 12]. Collar de Cáceres reads the board's label as a charge on every arrival, whether the player comes directly, by Labor, or from square 15 [@decaceresTableroItalianoFilosofia2009, 94]. DECISION: we follow the board. A player who lands on 26 directly, by a throw or by a Labor move, pays one stake to the pot; a player sent there from the Pass of Hope pays the two stakes of the Hope rule, one at 15 and one at 26, and no third.
- **Endless Labor.** If a Labor move reached by counting back from the palm would lead back to the same oxen (a throw of twelve from 57 returns to 57), the player stops on the oxen. The sources do not foresee the case; this is our decision.
- **Counting back onto the oxen.** Because a square reached by counting back takes effect, a player who counts back onto the oxen at 57 moves on again by the same throw, and may pass the palm a second time, count back again and pay a second stake: from 58, a throw of eleven runs on to 63, back to 57, on again past the palm and back to 58, for two stakes. DECISION: we keep this double rebound through 57, since it follows from the two rules as written.
- **Counting back onto Poverty.** In M the exemption for the poor player stands inside the overshoot rule: whoever counts back onto Poverty {q|debarrosFilosofiaCortesana1587|pdf12|pdf 12|no solo no paga, sino que todos le dan cada uno un tanto} (not only does not pay, but everyone gives him a stake each) [@debarrosFilosofiaCortesana1587, pdf 12]. The game therefore charges no stake for a count-back that ends on Poverty (59 under G, 60 otherwise), and none for the move from Poverty to 53; the other players pay their alms as usual. Neither G nor C has M's exemption; applying it to them is our DECISION (see item 3 above).
- **Displaced from the Well.** If a newcomer lands in the Well (32) while another player is in it, the general rule sends the first player to the square the newcomer left. The sources do not say whether that player still misses a round. DECISION: the displaced player keeps the lost round. Barros's Well costs a lost round and payment for the ropes; release because another player has fallen in is the goose-game rule, which Collar de Cáceres says Barros did not keep [@decaceresTableroItalianoFilosofia2009, 95].
- **Fortune and the Well.** With the setup option of two extra throws at Fortune, a player whose extra throw ends in the Well loses any extra throw still owed. The sources do not foresee the case. DECISION.
- **Displacement.** A displaced player sent backward pays one stake, as Pedro does in M's example, and so does a player sent back before the gate at the start. A player displaced forward pays nothing. The square a displaced player is moved to does not act, even if it is the Well or Fortune [@debarrosFilosofiaCortesana1587, pdf 12]. DECISION, following the wording of the general rule and M's example.
- **What this edition implements.** Two dice (one die available); a stake for every move back, including being displaced backward (the overshoot only, as an option); no stake for a count-back that ends on Poverty, and none for the move from Poverty to 53; the poor player receives one stake from each other player, in all three versions; one stake for landing on the Favorite (26) directly, and two, as the Hope rule says, for coming to it from 15; a player displaced from the Well still misses the round; players start before the gate, and a player displaced from a square reached by an opening throw returns there; each purse holds 20 stakes and may go below zero; a square reached by counting back from 63 takes effect, even when that sends the player past the palm again, and a square reached by being sent there does not; with two extra throws at Fortune, a fall into the Well cancels the throw still owed; the winner takes the whole pot. <!-- The rules engine is js/game.js; each of these choices is marked DECISION there, beside the source of each rule. -->

### about-money
title: Playing for money

#### Dice, cards and the moralists

Lucero sets Barros's choice against the standing of the games then in fashion: cards above all, and dice, drew the censure of moralists and the prohibitions of the law, and were played regardless [@luceroFilosofiaCortesanaJuego2020, 118–19]. The goose game, in his account, is governed by chance alone, and Barros's version adds stakes to it. Even so, Lucero met no moralist who named the goose game in a condemnation, and Izu Belloso's study gave him no trace of the ban in the *Novísima recopilación* that José Antonio González Alcantud reports [@luceroFilosofiaCortesanaJuego2020, 119 and nn. 3, 5]. Castilian gaming law itself lies outside the sources we cite, and this edition does not describe it.

Bidwell-Steiner turns to the casuists, who drew distinctions. Francisco de Alcocer's *Tratado del juego*{es} (1559) set board games among the “mixed” games, {q|bidwell-steinerFilosofiaCortesanaAlfonso2024|pdf13|207|mixtos: porque parte estan en la ventura y parte en saber los jugar} (mixed, because they lie partly in luck and partly in knowing how to play them), and held that no game is a sin in itself, only through its circumstances or a bad end [@bidwell-steinerFilosofiaCortesanaAlfonso2024, 207]. Pedro de Covarrubias's *Remedio de jugadores*{es} (Burgos, 1519) allowed play as a needed rest for the soul [@luceroIdeaTrabajoFilosofia2019, 823; @bidwell-steinerFilosofiaCortesanaAlfonso2024, 207 n. 50]. In this light she classes Barros's game as *eutrapelia*, recreation of a worthwhile kind, and its book as casuistry made playable: whatever case the dice produce, the text has counsel for it, though the counsel is far from univocal [@bidwell-steinerFilosofiaCortesanaAlfonso2024, 207–08].

#### A betting game for a moralist

The choice of a betting game was awkward for a book dedicated to Mateo Vázquez. Lucero counts this among the work's contradictions: a betting game was {q|luceroFilosofiaCortesanaJuego2020|pdf2|118|siempre poco grato a los ojos de un moralista, y Mateo Vázquez lo era} (always unwelcome in a moralist's eyes, and Mateo Vázquez was one) [@luceroFilosofiaCortesanaJuego2020, 118]. José Luis Gonzalo Sánchez-Molero found it hard to separate the book from Vázquez's repeated criticism of courtiers' idle lives, {q|sanchezEdicionesAntiguasFilosofia2016oct16|pdf13|181|en especial por su afición hacia todo tipo de juegos que incluían apuestas de dinero} (in particular their fondness for all kinds of games that involved betting money) [@sanchezEdicionesAntiguasFilosofia2016oct16, 181 n. 47]. Of Barros's second patron, García de Loaysa, a man who had served him for thirty years wrote, by way of praise, that among the things Loaysa never knew how to do were speaking with a woman and taking cards in hand [@luceroDedicatoriaProverbiosMorales2019, 105]. The royal license, for its part, calls the work {q|luceroTableroJuego15882019|pdf3|198|útil a la República por ser de honesto y gustoso entretenimiento} (useful to the commonwealth, as honest and pleasant entertainment) [@luceroTableroJuego15882019, 198]; that insistence on honest play may answer the same unease (our inference). In Rome, meanwhile, the papacy issued edicts against gambling at least thirty times between 1590 and 1674, and Kelli Wood reads the moralized Roman boards as a way for players to perform virtue while they enjoyed gambling [@woodChancingItPrint2019, 451, 460].

#### Who played

In 1585 the players at court included the prince and the infanta, with the king enjoying his jester's losses [@infantesPinturaQueSe2010, 134–35]. Barros wrote, in Fernando Rodríguez Mansilla's words, for {q|rodriguezmansillaGRABADOPICARAJUSTINA2019|pdf10|192|pretendientes y funcionarios en sus ratos de ocio} (petitioners and officials in their leisure hours) [@rodriguezmansillaGRABADOPICARAJUSTINA2019, 192]. Madrigal's rules say that {q|luceroFilosofiaCortesanaJuego2021juin20|pdf10|141|cuantos más fueren tiene más donaire el juego por el derribarse de los competidores unos a otros} (the more players there are, the more fun the game is, because the competitors knock one another down) [@luceroFilosofiaCortesanaJuego2021juin20, 141; @debarrosFilosofiaCortesana1587, pdf 11]. Lucero, marking the thought as his own fancy, pictures the fun of {q|luceroFilosofiaCortesanaJuego2021juin20|pdf17|148|afrontar la partida con solicitantes cuyos tejemanejes uno conoce muy bien, quizá junto a valedores con los que bromear} (playing the game against petitioners whose maneuvers one knows very well, perhaps with patrons at hand to joke with) [@luceroFilosofiaCortesanaJuego2021juin20, 148]. Bidwell-Steiner stresses the social side of play: reciting and glossing the text together was, in her words, {q|bidwell-steinerFilosofiaCortesanaAlfonso2024|pdf12|206|una actividad que produce chistes, motes y discusiones burlonas} (an activity that produces jokes, quips and mocking disputes) [@bidwell-steinerFilosofiaCortesanaAlfonso2024, 206]. Lucero thinks the small formats suited a book that very probably had to travel with the sheet and be consulted at the table during play [@sanchezEdicionesAntiguasFilosofia2016oct16, 180]. Lucero adds that a single sheet cost much less to produce than a pack of cards, and, from a surviving hand-drawn board, that players very likely also scratched improvised tracks on the ground [@luceroFilosofiaCortesanaJuego2020, 121]. Barros's worked example has its three players mark their places with a ring, a two-real coin and a doubloon [@debarrosFilosofiaCortesana1587, pdf 12].

#### What things cost

The sources we cite give a few prices and wages from Barros's world. They do not give the value of the coins in maravedís in 1587, so the figures below can be compared only where they share a unit.

- **The book.** Madrigal's edition was priced at half a real for its four printed sheets. Lucero calls this unusually high: the workshop's books of 1580–90 were usually priced at three maravedís a sheet, the *Quijote* nearly twenty years later at three and a half, and Hernando de Soto's illustrated *Emblemas moralizadas*{es} at three and a half because of its prints [@luceroTableroJuego15882019, 201–02 and n. 10].
- **Barros's wages.** In a memorial Barros wrote that he had served the king for twenty-four years on wages of 30,000 maravedís [@millanFilosofiaCortesanaAlonso1996, 463 n. 10]. <!-- The figure is read from a poorly scanned page ("3(XXI0 ñus de gajes" in the corpus text); check against the printed page before relying on it. -->
- **The Silos office** of 1587 carried a salary (*quitación*) of 20,021 maravedís [@luceroDEDICATORIAFILOSOFIACORTESANA2019, 36].
- **A patron's library.** García de Loaysa's library was valued at about 20,000 ducats; Lucero reckons that Barros would have needed 250 years of his wages as *aposentador* to earn as much [@luceroDedicatoriaProverbiosMorales2019, 109 n. 20].
- **A jester's losses.** Liaño lost forty escudos at the goose game in 1585 [@infantesPinturaQueSe2010, 135].

This edition's game counts in stakes, as Barros's rules do; Barros left the value of a stake to the players, {q|debarrosFilosofiaCortesana1587|pdf11|pdf 11|un tanto del valor que se concertaren} (one stake, of whatever value they agree) [@debarrosFilosofiaCortesana1587, pdf 11].

## D. Editorial note: how this edition was made

**Who made it.** This edition was made by Claude, an AI model made by Anthropic, at the request of Scott B. Weingart; “we” in these pages means the edition. **What was checked.** After it was written, the edition was checked against its sources in separate passes, also by Claude, each made by an instance that had not written the part it checked: the transcription of the 1588 book, letter by letter, against the page images of the Vienna copy; the inscriptions on the board, and every description of what the board shows, against the British Museum photograph; the Spanish of the Madrid edition against Ciompi and Seville's transcription; the readings of the first edition against Lucero's tables; and every passage the edition quotes, with the English given for it, against the source at the page cited (the short quotations marked as verified in the source files are also checked by program). Doubtful readings were looked at a second time, and the notes say which remain doubtful. The English of the book and of the board was read against the original for errors of sense. A further pass compared the edition's prose and its translations with the sources, in Spanish, Italian, French and English, and with the existing English translations of Barros, looking for words quoted without quotation marks, sentences translated or paraphrased too closely, and ideas used without credit; what it found has been quoted, credited or rewritten. These checks are not a specialist's review: the transcriptions, translations and interpretations have not been reviewed by a specialist in early modern Spanish or Italian, and the edition is a draft until they have been. None of the source material gathered for the project was used by Anthropic to train its models. See Credits and rights, below.

**Base texts.** The book is Barros's third edition, C (Naples: Iosep Cacchij, 1588), transcribed from the Google Books scan of the only copy recorded (Lucero 2016), Vienna, Österreichische Nationalbibliothek, 35 V 49 [@barrosFilosofiaCortesanaMoralizada1588]. We chose C because the only board known to survive was made for it, in Naples in the same year [@luceroTableroJuego15882019, 202]. The board is Mario Cartaro's sheet of 1588, British Museum 1869,0410.2463.+, transcribed from two photographs [board]. The text of M (Madrid: Pedro Madrigal, 1587) comes from the modernized transcription by Luigi Ciompi and Adrian Seville on giochidelloca.it, made from Trevor J. Dadson's edition of 1987, and from the plates in Wilson 1968 [@debarrosFilosofiaCortesana1587; @sanchezEdicionesAntiguasFilosofia2016oct16, 173 n. 14; @wilsonCervantesItemEmmanuel1968, 366]. The Spanish of M shown on this site, in the M passages on the Book page and in the “Declaración del juego” on the Play page, is reproduced from that transcription, and the edition's English of M is translated from it. That Spanish is Ciompi and Seville's work, not this edition's, and it is not covered by the edition's dedication to the public domain. M's readings are reported, not adopted. G (Madrid: widow of Alonso Gómez, 1587) is known to us only through Lucero's published collation, and its readings are cited from him [@sanchezEdicionesAntiguasFilosofia2016oct16, 183–90].

**Transcription.** The transcription of C is diplomatic, that is, letter for letter. It keeps the spelling, u/v, i/j, accents, capitals and punctuation of the print. Long s is given as s; ligatures are resolved. Abbreviations are expanded in square brackets (q̃ as q[ue]; a tilde over a vowel as [n] or [m]). Word division is normalized where the print's spacing is ambiguous. Line breaks are not kept; paragraphs and verse lines are. Page starts, signatures, running heads and catchwords are marked. Doubtful readings are marked [?], illegible letters [illeg.], and misprints [sic]. Nothing is corrected or supplied from another edition. Where a letter is only broken or damaged in this copy and what it is remains evident, the transcription prints the evident letter and the segment's note records the damaged form as printed. The board transcription follows the same rules. It also records the engraver's run-together words and the Italian above the Spanish in each square.

**Translation.** The English aims to be faithful first and readable second: nothing added, nothing dropped, every figure of speech kept, in modern, slightly elevated English, in American spelling. Barros's long periods may be split, never merged or reordered. Words with double senses (*suerte*, a throw and a lot; *trabajo*, work and hardship; *cuesta*, a slope and “it costs”; *casa*, a house of the board and a household) are flagged in notes. Fixed terms are used throughout (*pretendiente*, petitioner; *valedor*, patron; *privado*, the Favorite; *tanto*, a stake; *polla*, the pot). Each verse has a fixed rhymed rendering, shown to players, and a literal one beneath it. Barros calls the squares of the board *casas*, houses; the translation of his text keeps his word, and everywhere else this edition says “square”. It says “petitioner” for *pretendiente*, not “suitor”, and “case” for a petitioner's *negocio*, not “business”.

**The version switch.** Players can choose G, M or C. The board image is always Cartaro's, the only board known. Under G, Poverty stands on 59, not 60, and G's own couplets are shown for the eight Labor squares where its text differs from the board. Under M and C the rules are the same; under M, each square also shows the passages M adds or changes. Whichever edition is chosen, Barros's prose explanation of each square is quoted from C, because G's text is known to us only through Lucero's collation and M's only through a modernized transcription. M's “Declaración del juego” and its worked example are used under every edition to settle what G and C leave unsaid. Where all three are silent, the decisions set out in “How the game is played” apply; the setup screen and the rules panel link to them.
