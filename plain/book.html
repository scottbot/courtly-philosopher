Translator’s note

ES here is not from Ciompi and Seville's transcription, which opens with the Naples title page (“En Nápoles por Iosep Cacchÿ. 1588”) and so has no text of the Madrigal title. ES is copied from Lucero's line-for-line transcription of the Madrigal title page (Lucero Sánchez 2016, 179) (Wilson's facsimile of the Emmanuel copy's title page, Fig. 1, reads “Seuilla” (Wilson 1968, 366)). How M differs from C: C's title (C-007c–C-007h, pdf 7) sets FILOSOFIA / CORTESANA / MORALIZADA in capitals, has “sancta general Inquisicion”, and ends with the imprint “En Napoles por Iosep Cacchij” (no date). M prints no imprint on the title page. Instead it has Mateo Vázquez's arms in an oval woodcut with the motto IN HOC SIGNO VINCES, and the official price (“Tassado en medio Real”); both are absent from C (Lucero Sánchez 2016, 179–80; Wilson 1968, 366). “criado del Rey” = a member of the royal household (Barros was a royal aposentador). “moralizada” is kept as “moralized” because the book presents itself as the moral reading of the picture.

C-009b · leaf A2r–A3v · page images (PDF pages 9, 10, 11, 12)

POR quanto por parte de vos Alonso de Barros, nuestro criado, nos ha sido hecha relacion, que vos aueys compuesto vna pintura intitulada Filosofia Cortesana, con ciertas differencias de figuras y letras que se contiene en un pliego grande, y la aueys moralizado en vna relacion a parte[.?] Y nos suplicastes, que teniendo consideracion a lo que en ello aueys trabajado, os diessemos licencia, y ma[n]dassemos que vos, o la persona, ò personas que vuestro poder huuieren, lo pedays[?] imprimir, y ve[n]der enestos nuestros reynos, y no otras algunas, o como la nuestra merced fuesse: Y porque auiendose visto por nuestro mandado, parecio ser obra de mucho ingenio, y que serà vtil à la Republica, por ser de honesto y gustoso entretenimiento. Por la presente os damos licencia y facultad, para que por tiempo de diez años primeros siguientes, contados desde el dia de la fecha desta nuestra cedula en adelante, vos a la persona, o personas, que para ello vuestro poder huuieren, podays imprimir y ve[n]der enestos nuestros Reynos y señorios la dicha pintura, y moralidad della. Y ma[n]damos, que otras personas algunas no lo puedan imprimir, ni vender, ni imprima[·] , ni vendan enestos dichos nuestros reynos y señorios, so pena que qualesquier personas que sin tener el dicho vuestro poder lo imprimieren, o hizieren imprimir, o lo vendieren, o hizieren vender, pierdan toda la impression que hizieren i vendieren, i los moldes y aparejos con que lo hizieren, i mas incurran por cada vez en pena de cinquenta mil marauedis. La tercia parte para nuestra camara. Y la tercia parte para el Iuez que lo sentenciare. Y la otra tercia parte para el denunciador. Y mandamos, que cada pliego de la dicha pintura con la dicha moralidad, se venda al precio que por los del nuestro Consejo fuere tassado. A los quales, y a los Presidentes, y Oydores de las nuestras audiencias, y a otros qualesquier juezes y justicias destos dichos nuestros Reynos y señorios, mandamos assimismo, que guarden y cumplan, y hagan guardar y cumplir esta nuestra cedula, y lo en ella contenido. Fecha en Madrid a nueue de Febrero, de mil y quinientos y ochenta y siete años.
Whereas, on behalf of you, Alonso de Barros, our servant, report has been made to us that you have composed a picture entitled Filosofía cortesana, with a number of different figures and verses, which is contained on one large sheet, and that you have written a moral explanation of it in a separate account; and you begged us that, taking into consideration the labor you have put into it, we should give you license, and order that you, or the person or persons holding your power of attorney, and no other persons whatever, might print and sell it in these our kingdoms, or as our pleasure might be. And because, having been examined by our order, it appeared to be a work of great ingenuity, and one that will be useful to the commonwealth, being honest and pleasant entertainment: by these presents we give you license and authority so that, for the term of the ten years next following, counted from the day of the date of this our warrant onward, you or the person or persons holding your power for it may print and sell in these our kingdoms and lordships the said picture, and its moral explanation. And we order that no other persons whatever may print it or sell it, nor print nor sell it in these our said kingdoms and lordships, on pain that any persons who, without holding your said power, should print it or cause it to be printed, or sell it or cause it to be sold, shall lose the whole impression that they make and sell, and the formes and equipment with which they make it, and shall further incur, for each offense, a penalty of fifty thousand maravedís. One third for our Chamber. And one third for the judge who passes sentence. And the other third for the informer. And we order that each sheet of the said picture, with the said moral explanation, be sold at the price that shall be set by those of our Council. To whom, and to the Presidents and Judges (oidores) of our Audiencias, and to any other judges and officers of justice whatever of these our said kingdoms and lordships, we likewise order that they keep and fulfill, and cause to be kept and fulfilled, this our warrant and what it contains. Given in Madrid on the ninth of February, in the year one thousand five hundred and eighty-seven.

Translator’s note

(1) Pintura is the printed board, “one large sheet” (pliego grande); the relacion a parte / moralidad is this book. The privilege licenses both, and prices them together “each sheet of the said picture with the said moral explanation”. Letras here are the inscriptions/verses on the board. (2) lo pedays[?] imprimir is rendered as podays (“might print”); the transcription notes the e may be an ink-filled o. (3) vos a la persona (pdf 10) is rendered “you or the person”: the printed a is probably an ink-filled o, and the parallel formula on pdf 9 has o. (4) ni imprima[·] , ni vendan: the n (or tilde) of impriman failed to print; the doubled “print … sell … nor print nor sell” is the legal formula, kept. (5) moldes y aparejos: the type set up in formes, and the press equipment. (6) nuestra camara: the royal treasury (the Cámara). Consejo: the royal Council, which set the official price (tasa) per sheet. Oydores: the judges of the Audiencias, the high courts. (7) Cedula: a royal warrant (cédula). The maravedí was the Castilian money of account. (8) y no otras algunas: feminine, so “no other persons”, not “no other kingdoms”; the privilege repeats the restriction below (“otras personas algunas no lo puedan imprimir”).

Translator’s note

SONNET.

C-014c · leaf A4v · page image (PDF page 14)

REduzir a plazer la pesadumbre
De pretensiones; que consumen vidas;
Aprender a ganar: y a ver perdidas
Las esperancas con incierta lumbre:
Mirar como arrojadas de la cumbre,
Quanto mas leuantadas: mas caydas
Estan nuestras venturas reduzidas
Al fallo de ambiciosa seruidumbre;
Esta filosofia no hallada
En el discurso de la edad primera
Que tuuo sus desseos limitados.
Estaua a vuestra pluma reseruada,
Come[?] si de Platon regida fuera;
Para norte de gusto: y de cuydados[.?]
To turn to pleasure all the heaviness
of petitions for office, which consume lives;
to learn to win, and to behold as lost
the hopes, in an uncertain light;
to see how, flung down from the very summit,
the higher they were raised, the further fallen,
our fortunes stand reduced
to the sentence of ambitious servitude:
this philosophy, which was never found
in the course of that first age of the world
which held its own desires within their bounds,
was kept in reserve for your pen,
as if it had been governed by Plato,
to be a pole-star both for pleasure and for cares.

Translator’s note

Line-by-line version, unrhymed. The whole sonnet is one period: the infinitives (REduzir … Aprender a ganar: y a ver … Mirar) stand in apposition to “this philosophy”, which is the subject of Estaua … reseruada. (1) pesadumbre: both “weight” and “grief”; “heaviness” keeps both. REduzir a plazer is echoed by reduzidas (l. 7), and Barros's “Al lector” says his aim was to “reduzir a orden” the business of petitioning. (2) pretensiones: petitions for office or favor (guide: pretensión = petition). (3) ganar … perdidas: gaming words, win and lose, for the book is a game. (4) con incierta lumbre may go with ver (to see by an uncertain light) or with esperancas (hopes that shine uncertainly); the English “in an uncertain light” keeps both. esperancas is so printed, without cedilla. (5) de la cumbre: from the summit of favor; the rise and fall is the image of Fortune's wheel. (8) fallo: a judgment or verdict (legal sense, rendered “sentence”). In card play fallo is also being void in a suit, and fallar is “to fail”; a gaming pun may be intended, but the evidence here does not settle it. (9–11) la edad primera: the first age of classical myth (the Golden Age), when people kept their desires within bounds and there were no petitions and no court; so the “philosophy” of petitioning had no occasion to be found. discurso: the course (of time), also “discourse”, the word Barros uses for his own “discurso de pretensores”. (12) vuestra pluma: Barros's pen (the sonnet addresses the author). (13) Come[?] is blotted in this copy; read Como, as in Wilson's transcription of the Madrid 1587 (Madrigal) printing and in the Lemir text of this Naples copy. Plato stands as the philosopher par excellence: Barros's “philosophy” is as well ordered as if Plato had governed it. No particular dialogue need be meant. The modernized transcription of the Madrid 1587 text by Luigi Ciompi and Adrian Seville (giochidelloca.it) reads “como de Platón regida fuera”, dropping si; that line is a syllable short, and the page image has si. (14) norte: the pole-star, the navigator's guide, the image that Cervantes's sonnet on the facing page develops into a whole sea. The final stop after cuydados is blotted.

C-015c · leaf A5r · page image (PDF page 15)

QVal vemos del rozado y rico Oriente;
La blanca y dura piedra señalarse[?]
Y en todo (aunque pequenna[?]) auentajarse
A la mayor del Caucaso eminente.
Tal este humilde al parecer presente
Puede: y deue mirarse: y admirarse:
No por la cantidad; mas por mostrarse
Ser en su calidad tan excelente.
El que nauega por el golfo insano
Del mar de pretensiones; vera al punto
Del cortesano laberintio el hilo.
Felice ingenio: y venturosa mano
Quel deleyte: y prouecho puso iunto
En juego alegre, en dulce y claro estilo;
As we see, from the rosy and rich East,
the white and hard stone distinguish itself,
and in every way (though it is small) surpass
the greatest of the towering Caucasus,
so this present, humble as it seems,
can, and should, be looked upon and wondered at,
not for its quantity, but for showing
itself to be so excellent in quality.
Whoever sails across the maddened gulf
of the sea of petitions will see, upon the instant,
the thread of the labyrinth of the court.
Happy the wit, and fortunate the hand,
that put delight and profit together
in a merry game, in sweet and lucid style.

Translator’s note

Line-by-line version, unrhymed. Structure: Qual … Tal (“As … so”): an octave of comparison (the small gem, the small book), then a tercet on what the book shows the petitioner, then a tercet of praise. (1) rozado is the Naples spelling; the Madrid 1587 (Madrigal) printing, in Wilson's facsimile, reads rosado, “rosy”, and that is the sense: the East rosy with dawn, the East of rich gems. (2) La blanca y dura piedra: a white, hard stone from the East, which, though small, outdoes the greatest of the Caucasus. The hardness points to the diamond (diamante, the “adamant” of the ancients), prized for its hardness and water-clear whiteness; the finest came from the East (India). A pearl is possible: pearls were counted among the precious stones, and oriente is also the jeweler's word for a pearl's luster. But a pearl is not “hard”. The notes consulted do not discuss the point; the translation keeps “stone” and leaves the gem unnamed, as Cervantes does. señalarse[?]: the letters after se- are blotted; read señalarse (to stand out, distinguish itself), as in the Madrid 1587 facsimile, and Schevill and Bonilla printed sennalarse from this Naples copy (as Wilson reports). (3) pequenna[?]: two n-forms or an ñ run into a blot; the word is pequeña, “small” (Madrid 1587 has pequeña; Schevill and Bonilla print pequenna from this copy: the Neapolitan printer used nn for ñ). (4) A la mayor del Caucaso eminente: “the greatest [stone] of the lofty Caucasus”. The Caucasus is the classical byword for towering mountains (Prometheus was chained to one of its crags), so the comparison is between the gem and a whole mountain of rock: value against bulk. eminente is both “towering” and a word of rank. (5) presente: “gift” and “here present”; the English “present” keeps both. The gift is the book offered to Mateo Vázquez, a small duodecimo; the octave turns on quantity against quality (cantidad … calidad), the book's smallness against its worth, a point the dedication itself makes (“tan pequeña obra en las palabras, y tan gra[n]de en la substancia”). (6) mirarse: y admirarse: a jingle, “looked at and wondered at”, kept as closely as English allows. Ser (l. 8) has a blotted initial. (9–10) el golfo insano / Del mar de pretensiones: golfo is the open sea far from shore (compare Barros's opening, “no se engolfando en alta mar”); insano is “mad”, “raging”, and also “unhealthy”. The “sea of petitions” is the sea at the center of the board (the Sea of Suffering), which Bidwell-Steiner (2024) sees illustrated there. The modernized transcription of the Madrid 1587 text by Ciompi and Seville reads “por golfo insano”, dropping el; that line is a syllable short, and both the page image and the other two witnesses (the Madrid 1587 facsimile and the Lemir text of this copy) have el. al punto: “at once”. A glance at the dice's puntos (points) is possible in a poem about a game, but the notes consulted do not settle it. (11) Del cortesano laberintio el hilo: Ariadne's thread, which led Theseus out of the Cretan labyrinth; here the book is the thread through the labyrinth of the court. laberintio is so printed here and in Madrid 1587; it is not a misreading. Collar de Cáceres (2009) notes that “labyrinth” was also a name for such race games, so the court-labyrinth is also the board. (12) Felice: an Italianate or poetic form of feliz. venturosa mano: the “lucky hand” of the author, with a possible glance at the game, where a mano is a round of play. (13–14) Quel = que el. Joining “delight and profit” in a “sweet” style is the Horatian precept (Ars poetica 343) that the poet who mixes the useful with the sweet wins every vote; the sonnet ends by praising the book in those terms.

AL LECTOR.

TO THE READER.

C-016b · leaf A5v–A6v · page images (PDF pages 16, 17, 18)

MAteria es esta del pretender, donde qua[n]do todos los escritores de buen entendimiento, que ha[n] auido, y ay oy en el mu[n]do, quisieran mostrarse, por mucho que huuieran continuado la pluma quedaran cortos, segun los altos y baxos de prospera, y aduersa fortuna, co[n]tentos, y quexas de agrauios que en ella ay. Y quando yo lo quede, quedare disculpado, pues[?] mi intento no ha sido sino[?] mostrar entre burla[?] y juego, las veras[?] y desuenturas que siguen a vna larga pretension, y reduzir a orden lo que tan sin ella se trata. El que con mas curiosidad[?] lo quisiere mirar, muestre su habilidad en hazer otra cosa mejor, y no ponga mucho estudio en notar sus faltas, que en ninguna de las de letras humanas dexa[n] de hallarlas los ojos cabilosos de los que las miran. Y es de creer, que todos como yo, han desseado acertar, y no por esso se han librado deste peligro. Esta consideracion disculpa mi atreuimiento, con que si la obra no fuere tal, como yo quisiera, su breuedad la assegura de ser fastidiosa, que de lo malo, no es lo peor.
This matter of petitioning for advancement is one in which, even if all the writers of good understanding who have ever been, and who are in the world today, wished to show their powers, however long they had kept their pens going they would fall short, given the highs and lows of prosperous and adverse fortune, the satisfactions, and the complaints of injuries that there are in it. And if I fall short of it, I shall be excused, since my intention has been nothing but to show, in jest and in play, the earnest truths and misfortunes that follow long petitioning, and to reduce to order what is handled so entirely without it. Whoever should wish to look at it more curiously, let him show his skill by making something better, and not put much study into noting its faults; for in no work of humane letters do the captious eyes of those who look at them fail to find them. And it is to be believed that all, like me, have wished to hit the mark, and not for that have they been spared this danger. This consideration excuses my boldness; so that, if the work should not be such as I would wish, its brevity keeps it from being tiresome, which, of bad things, is not the worst.

Translator’s note

(1) pretender: to seek office or favor at court; first occurrence of the verb in the book, rendered “petitioning for advancement” (guide). (2) entre burla y juego, las veras: the Spanish pair burlas / veras, “jest” and “earnest”: the game is play, what it shows is in earnest. The upper lines of pdf 17 are slurred and blotted, and pues[?], sino[?], burla[?], veras[?] and curiosidad[?] are read from letter outlines; the sense of the passage is secure. (3) reduzir a orden: compare Liñán's sonnet, “REduzir a plazer”. (4) curiosidad: care, minute scrutiny. (5) de las de letras humanas is so printed, with an extra de (Ciompi and Seville's modernized transcription of the Madrid 1587 text has “de las letras humanas”); rendered as the evident sense, “in no work of humane letters”. cabilosos: “cavilling”, captious. (6) The last clause is proverbial in shape: brevity is a merit even in a bad work, since a bad thing that is short is not the worst kind of bad thing. disculpa-|do is divided across the page and joined in ES.

A MATEO VAZQVEZ de Leca, del conseio de su Magestad, y su secretario, y de la santa general Inquisicion, arcediano de Carmona, y canonigo en la santa yglesia de Seuilla.

TO MATEO VÁZQUEZ de Leca, of His Majesty's Council and his Secretary, and of the Holy General Inquisition, Archdeacon of Carmona, and Canon in the Holy Church of Seville.

Translator’s note

Mateo Vázquez de Leca was Philip II's secretary from 1573 until his death in 1591 and the patron of the court faction historians call castellanista (Wilson 1968, 365, 367; Lucero 2019d, 109 n. 21; Lucero 2019b, 37, 42). (1) “el dado”: “the die”, singular, but the text elsewhere presupposes two dice: a “throw of eleven” at 53 (C-052b) and, before this part, a losing throw at 28. The Madrid 1587 “Declaración” specifies two dice (or “seis arenillas”, six pieces marked on one side only); 1588 does not say how many are used. “el dado” may be generic (“the dice”). (2) “se señala a tantas casas, como puntos echo”: “echo” = “echó”, “he threw”. “se señala”: sets one's marker (señal); the 1588 text never describes the counters or the opening stakes, which 1587 explains (each player puts “un tanto” of an agreed value into the pot, “la polla”, and uses their own object as a marker). (3) “si dos dan en vna casa, se queda el segundo, y el primero toma la que el otro dexo”: the newcomer stays, and the player who was there moves to the newcomer's former square: the two exchange places. The 1587 clause for the start of the game (the displaced first player is left without a square, since the newcomer had none) is absent from 1588. 1588 does not say here whether the displaced player pays; the closing clause (“paying one stake for each time he goes back, of whatever kind”) may be meant to cover it (in 1587's worked example the displaced player pays one stake “porque volvió atrás”, because he went back). (4) “si da en los bueyes”: the oxen stand in the squares of Labor (on the 1588 board, 4, 12, 17, 23, 30, 34, 41, 48 and 57; the text gives no numbers). “passa otras tantas casas adelante, como pu[n]tos echo, para llegar alli”: the throw that brought the player there is counted again, so that its value is doubled. The rule is not said to cost anything. (5) “pagando vn tanto por cada vna” (15 → 26) is ambiguous: (a) one stake for each of the two squares, Hope and the Favorite, two stakes in all; (b) one stake for each square passed over between 15 and 26. Reading (a) is supported by the 1587 text (“por cada una de las dos”, “for each of the two”) and by its worked example (the player pays one stake for the hope offered and another for the favor expected at 26), and by the board, which engraves “Paga” beside both 15 (“Al priuato ·n[umer]º· 26. Paga”) and 26 (“Paga”). Where the stakes go is not stated; presumably the pot. “vn tanto”: one stake, a counter of the value agreed at the start. (6) Well of Oblivion (32): three things, as the Spanish lists them: one stake to each other player; two stakes into the pot “para sogas”, “for ropes” (to haul the player out: the moral text, in the section on the Well, calls them “sogas de liberalidad”, “ropes of liberality”); and “auiendo estado sin jugar vna mano”, literally “having stayed one round without playing”. The participle leaves the order unclear (pay after missing the turn, or pay and miss it); in either case the player misses one round. The board label reads “Para·sogas ·1· y ·2· estese”, “For ropes, 1 and 2, and let him stay”. What the G readings change for the game

  1. Poverty is on 59, not 60. G says so twice, in the moral text and in the rules (Lucero 2019a, 204; Lucero Sánchez 2016, 189). M, C and the Naples board have 60. From Poverty the player goes back to the Dice at 53 in every version.
  2. Different Labor couplets, in a different order. G has four couplets found nowhere else (g1, g2, g3, g6) and lacks four that M, C and the board have (t1, t2, t6, t8). Its order of the eight is g1, g2, g3, t5, t7, g6, t4, t3 (Lucero Sánchez 2016, 187–88). A G board would have carried these verses on its eight later Labor squares; which verse stood on which square is not known.
  3. Laconic rules. G has “Esto se juega en esta forma”, one die (“el dado”), the rule for two on one square, and at the end the rules for Poverty and overshooting the palm (Lucero Sánchez 2016, 189). The G text Lucero quotes does not state the opening stakes, the number of players, markers, two dice or arenillas, how the game is won, that the palm is on 63, the size of the alms, or the Poverty exemption, and it breaks off before the place where M treats the clash at the start of the game. All of these are in M, and C is silent on all of them.
  4. ”Deo gratias” closes G's rules (M and C “Laus Deo”). The rules' last lines are set in the shape of a goblet (Lucero Sánchez 2016, 189).
  5. Smaller shifts that touch the pictures. G's swan has only a trumpet, with no death's head (G-09). The central sea has painted ships but no angler (G-38). The man at the palm is only “as if exerting himself” (G-37). The Dice at 53 is not described as a throw of eleven (G-34). There is no Fortune woodcut or description (G-30). These are Lucero's grounds for thinking that the lost G board differed from the surviving Naples board, and was perhaps less elaborate (Lucero 2019a, 205–6).
  6. Possibly a different verse at the Dice (28): “la mejor suerte es azar” (G-21). Unconfirmed; see the note there.
  7. One point where the Naples board agrees with G against M and C: the line order of the verse at 53 (G-35). That the board reverses the book's lines is noted by Rodríguez Mansilla (Rodríguez Mansilla 2019, 190); that its order is G's is our observation.

What is NOT known about G

  • The full text. We have only Lucero's excerpts. Most of G's moral text for each square, most of its rules, its privilege, its approval and its dedication have not been quoted to us. Unquoted passages must not be assumed to match M or C.
  • G's original spelling and punctuation, except on the title page. The excerpts are modernized by Lucero.
  • The rules between “si dos dan en una casa” and “Y de la Pobreza”. Lucero implies they run parallel to M and C (Lucero Sánchez 2016, 189 n. 58; Lucero 2019a, 203 n. 13), but gives no text.
  • G's wording of the first Poverty passage (fol. 16v), of which we know only the number 59 (Lucero 2019a, 204).
  • The first Labor couplet (“Nunca se siente el trabajo…”, t0), and every non-Labor verse, in G's wording. Lucero states that only the Labor couplets change (Lucero 2019a, 203 n. 15), but the fragment “la mejor suerte es azar” (G-21) may be an exception.
  • The G board. No copy survives. Its existence, and its differences (Poverty on 59; G's Labor couplets; perhaps a more even spread of the Labor squares; perhaps a different technique and lower quality), are Lucero's inferences from the text and from the price of the Madrigal book (Lucero 2019a, 204–6; Lucero Sánchez 2020, 20, 43–44). The square numbers of G's Labor squares, its images, its labels, and whether it bore the rule labels that M quotes (“Al privado … 26”) are all unknown.
  • Whether G was meant to be played with one die or two. G's text says “el dado”; M says two dice or six arenillas but also “el dado”.
  • The location of several readings in Lucero's tables (“está pintado” G-39, probably at the Dice; “Y ansí” G-18, “la mejor suerte es azar” G-21).
